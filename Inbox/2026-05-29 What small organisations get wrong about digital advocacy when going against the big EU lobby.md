Power

29.05.2026

A small NGO with two Brussels staff and the tiniest, barely scraped together budget is writing a position paper on a major piece of legislation. Across town, a tech company's ten-person public affairs team has been working the same file for a year and a half and has met, through great insistence, with all essential stakeholders. Both will submit to the same consultation portal.

This asymmetry is the whole story, really. Nobody formally bars small organisations and civil society from EU policymaking. But when the 162 biggest corporate lobby players declare at least [343 million euros a year](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2025/02/eus-lobby-league-table) in lobbying spend, and NGOs collectively report around [163 million euros](https://www.ftm.eu/articles/civil-society-shrinking-space-europe), the playing field is not levelled before anyone walks onto it.

\-

By Viktorya Muradyan

#### The gaps are real, but not where you think

We can't just blame big organisations for having more money. Just because they are bigger or better funded does not mean their messages are stronger, that the political mood supports their agenda, or that they win all the time. As Alberto Alemanno, Professor of EU law at HEC Paris and Founder of The Good Lobby, puts it, *"Money buys you the infrastructure to harvest that intelligence systematically. But I've seen well-resourced civil society groups lose not because they were outspent (they always are), but because they were outpaced."*

Talk to anyone who works in Brussels advocacy, and you hear about three problems, over and over.

The first is the **intelligence gap**. Big lobby teams monitor legislative files religiously. They know a dossier is moving before it goes public, they track which MEPs are leading on it, and they read the signals from closed-door negotiations between Parliament, Council, and Commission. Most NGOs find out something is moving when Politico or Euractiv write about it, and by then the window for meaningful input has often closed. Everyone calls Brussels slow, but it can be incredibly fast: while full procedures are lengthy, individual steps can leave you with a few days for amendments or a limited number of weeks to finish trilogues. You may wait months for a communication to drop, then get 72 hours to respond to fifty amendments.

The second is the **access gap.** Not just getting a meeting, but getting the right one, in the limited time you and your stakeholders or politicians have between Strasbourg, Green Week, May holidays, teleworking days, and everything else. Understanding who the lead and shadow negotiators actually are on a file, which Council attaché in which rotating presidency is driving the text or the opposition to the text. That kind of map used to require years of expensive relationship-building. It's much harder to create as an isolated small organisation with little to no resources.

The third is the **credibility gap**. Corporate lobbyists produce polished research, fund think tanks, and commission legal opinions. Small organisations often can't match that volume or speed. But they usually have something corporate actors struggle to fake: legitimacy, lived experience, the ability to speak for constituencies that actually exist. The trick is converting that into a format a policymaker can pick up and use.

#### The "strategic thinking problem"

When I asked Alemanno what small organisations consistently get wrong, he didn't talk about technology. *"Lack of a clear theory of change,"* he said. *"Most come to Brussels with a perfectly valid concern and immediately start producing outputs without ever asking: which specific decision-maker needs to change their position, by when, and what would actually move them?"*

That is the unsexy part. No AI aggregator or GPT can fix it. Digital mobilisation works when it activates a constituency that policymakers know exists but haven't heard from directly. The European Citizens' Initiative is the clearest example of this: it is one of the few formal mechanisms that actually forces the Commission to respond. When the End the Cage Age campaign collected 1.4 million signatures, the Commission committed to banning cage farming by 2023. Then it quietly shelved the proposal. The Good Lobby helped file complaints with the EU Ombudsman and eventually took the case to the Court of Justice, arguing that the Commission had failed its procedural duty to follow through. That is now the first time a citizens' initiative has gone to court to hold the Commission accountable. The mobilisation got the signatures. Procedural knowledge is what turned them into legal pressure.

*"Civic actors often have legitimacy that corporate lobbyists lack, but they dissipate it through unfocused effort and often by demonising procedures,"* says Alemanno.

But digital campaigns backfire when small organisations **mistake visibility for influence, as Alberto Alemanno puts it.** Just because your organisation gets a petition signed by 15.000 people or gets a campaign video viewed more than one million times, it does not mean that the organisation will manage to pierce through the noise or get an actual meeting with a key figure working on the file. *"Brussels officials* \[it also applies to national ones\] *are remarkably skilled at ignoring noise that doesn’t carry electoral or procedural consequences. Digital mobilisation without a close grasp of the institutional and substantive legal framework tends to backfire or remain irrelevant."*

The other key element of strategic thinking is coalitions and coalition-building. *"Policymakers are highly attuned to who is not in the room,"* Alemanno tells me. The same goes for any European or national initiative which has been created without consulting a sufficiently diverse set of stakeholders. *"A campaign backed exclusively by environmental NGOs reads differently than one where trade unions, SME associations, or consumer groups are co-signatories. I’ve seen well-resourced organisations lose precisely because their coalition was a perfect echo chamber."*

#### What is a small organisation’s holy grail against a big lobby?

The Good Lobby's experience suggests that the tools which make the biggest difference are not expensive or complicated. What matters most is prelegislative monitoring, keeping track of parliamentary activity, coordinating across coalitions, and above all, knowing how to map who actually holds the pen on a decision.

In practice, that means paying attention to the pre-legislative phase, which is where most small organisations lose the race before it starts. The Commission publishes impact assessments before proposing new legislation, and the Regulatory Scrutiny Board reviews those assessments for quality. Those documents reveal the Commission's thinking, its preferred options, and the evidence it is relying on. They are public, free to read, and most NGOs ignore them. Similarly, the interservice consultation process, where different Commission departments weigh in on a draft proposal, often signals where internal tensions lie and which arguments are gaining traction. If you are only engaging once a legislative text is formally published, you have already missed the stage where the framing gets set.

Once a proposal enters the legislative pipeline, the European Parliament's Legislative Observatory, known as OEIL, tracks every procedure through every stage: which committee is responsible, who the rapporteur is, what amendments have been tabled, and when votes are scheduled.

Then there are the transparency tools that help you understand the lobbying landscape around your issue. LobbyFacts.eu shows who is lobbying whom and how much they are spending. EU Integrity Watch makes declared meetings and financial interests searchable. AsktheEU.org lets anyone file freedom of information requests for internal Commission documents. Spend half an hour on these before your next meeting with a Commission official, and you will know who else has been in that room and what they have been arguing. Together, they give a small team access to intelligence that used to be reserved for organisations with big monitoring budgets.

Finally, there is AI, which Alemanno was refreshingly honest about. *"It's lowered the cost of the first draft,"* he told me, *"briefings, consultation responses, stakeholder analyses, but only if you know what you're asking for and can create added value."* Very often, during advocacy, particularly when it comes to big packages of legislation, organisations which manage to analyse and drop narratives the quickest can indeed manage to dominate the media discourse. But AI is just a stepping stone in your advocacy, as *"knowing which fight to pick, which coalition partner to trust, which MEP is actually persuadable remains one hundred per cent human,"* Alemanno said.

#### Where do we start the change?

Tomorrow, when you sit down to work on your project or file, run through this and see what you can do differently.

- **Be** " **useful first and loud second** "**.** Focus on what problems you can solve for policymakers before being loud about how outraged your organisation is. Provide data, case studies, and real stories. There will be time for public tough love later, but showing up with something usable gets your foot in the door.

- **Treat consultation responses as strategic documents.** The Commission gets flooded with replies, sometimes thousands. But a well-written and argumented response can be recycled into position papers, media talking points, and briefing notes. Write it once, use it everywhere.

- **Don't go to war alone.** Pick coalition partners as varied as possible. A joint position from eight credible civil society groups gets read differently than eight separate letters. Use Notion, Signal groups, and shared Google Docs to coordinate without wasting time on unnecessary meetings.

- **Use AI, but leave your desk regularly.** Draft faster, but make sure your submission contains something only you can provide. If you know a file is coming, start early: commission research, gather field evidence, build a foundation before others have a chance to catch up and time publishing your findings right before the public discourse on the topic starts.

- **Learn to love procedures.** The European Citizens' Initiative is one of the most underused formal mechanisms in EU democracy, and it now has real digital infrastructure behind it. Procedures are not your enemy, they can become your leverage points.

- **Map your stakeholders and be persistent.** Sometimes it takes a year of consistent emailing and calling to get a meeting. Stubbornness, when polite and respectful, usually leads to attention.

- **Go to them. Don't wait for them to find you.** Build mailing lists. Write subject lines that exhausted Policy Officers and parliamentary assistants will actually click on. Personalising emails takes time, but we all know we’re more likely to read and reply to the ones that feel like they were written for us.

- **Prepare counterarguments, not just arguments.** Know who has been in the room before you and what they pushed for. Sometimes, pointing out the gaps in a competitor's position is what makes people pay attention to yours.

- **Don't mistake visibility for impact.** Social media campaigns may not even reach your target audience. Digital advocacy is one part of winning, not the whole thing.

- **Learn from peers.** Look at [campaigns that worked](https://europeancommission.medium.com/european-citizens-initiative-gave-europeans-a-real-say-with-students-young-people-e5c8c5571da), like End the Cage Age or Right2Water, which led to the revision of the EU Drinking Water Directive. Study what they did, but also study where similar efforts fell short.
