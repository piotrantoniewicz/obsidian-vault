*Why investing in the people, systems, and infrastructure behind the work is essential to sustaining it*

For decades, the nonprofit sector has been held to a familiar standard: the less an organization spends on itself, the better. Overhead is waste. Administration is a distraction. Every dollar not going directly to programs is a dollar taken away from the mission.

Nonprofits have responded accordingly. Keep overhead low, and funding follows. It’s a compelling story—and one that has done real damage.

Scholars call it the nonprofit starvation cycle. Funders hold unrealistic expectations about operating costs, and nonprofits underinvest in essential systems to meet them. Over time, those choices weaken the infrastructure that allows programs to grow, staff to stay, and impact to endure. Ultimately, communities receive less than they could.

And the financial pressure behind those decisions is only growing. A [survey of nonprofit leaders conducted by Grassi](https://www.grassiadvisors.com/premium-tools/2024-nonprofit-survey-resilience-in-the-face-of-rising-demands-and-costs/) found that 81% reported higher operating costs in the past year, with an average increase of 15%. At the same time, 73% reported increased demand for their programs. Nonprofits are being asked to do more while the cost of doing that work continues to rise.

## The infrastructure imperative

Operational excellence starts with a simple idea: delivering impact depends on having the people and systems to sustain it. A reliable CRM helps preserve donor relationships. Sound financial controls build funder trust. And investing in staff helps organizations hold onto the knowledge and relationships that keep the work moving forward.

That makes talent an operational priority, not simply an HR concern. According to Sage’s [2025 Nonprofit Technology Impact Report](https://www.sage.com/en-us/blog/nonprofit-tech-impact-report/), 58% of nonprofits cite hiring and retention as their biggest external barrier, ranking it above funding concerns and economic instability. Turnover can also carry significant costs through recruiting, onboarding, and the institutional knowledge that leaves with an employee.

Investing in competitive compensation, professional development, and a healthy staff culture isn’t spending away from mission. It builds the stability the mission depends on.

*A nonprofit that struggles to retain its people will also struggle to retain institutional knowledge, trusted relationships, and continuity in its work. Staff stability is organizational stability.*

## Donor retention as an operational strategy

That same principle applies to donors. [Momentive Software’s 2025 research](https://momentivesoftware.com/research-study/nonprofit-trends/) found that 56% of organizations with a donor retention strategy experienced revenue growth, with donor retention rates of 77% compared with 61% for organizations without one. Yet 46% of nonprofits reported having no donor retention strategy at all.

The gap isn’t a lack of understanding. Nonprofit leaders know retention matters. The challenge is having the capacity to do it consistently. Good retention requires reliable systems, useful data, and enough staff time to follow through—whether that means segmenting communications, acknowledging gifts personally, or making sure donors hear how their support made a difference.

Those investments may happen behind the scenes, but donors experience the result: an organization that knows who they are, communicates with purpose, and gives them a reason to stay connected.

## Rethinking the overhead narrative

The good news is that the conversation around overhead has begun to change. More funders are recognizing that strong programs depend on strong organizations, and approaches like [trust-based philanthropy](https://www.trustbasedphilanthropy.org/multi-year-unrestricted-funding) are making more room for unrestricted, multi-year support.

But nonprofits have a role in changing the conversation, too. Operational investment becomes easier to understand when organizations make the connection to mission clear. A stronger system can mean fewer donor relationships falling through the cracks. Investing in staff can preserve expertise and continuity. And better processes give people time to spend on the work only they can do.

A compelling mission can inspire people to give, volunteer, and care. Sustaining that work requires an organization strong enough to carry it forward. The nonprofits best positioned for what comes next will understand that operational excellence isn’t separate from the work. It’s what allows the mission to endure.
