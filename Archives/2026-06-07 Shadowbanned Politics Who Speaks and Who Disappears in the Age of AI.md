Power

07.06.2026

Artificial intelligence is becoming an invisible editor of democratic life, deciding which political claims surface, which disappear and which are subtly reshaped. Large language models sit between citizens and information, drawing on vast datasets that include Wikipedia, news archives and social media, while operating under content policies and safety rules few outsiders understand.

As new European regulations clamp down on political advertising and platforms tighten their own rules, the risk is that AI systems designed to reduce harm end up narrowing legitimate debate and entrenching existing power structures.

\-

The First Draft of Political Reality: How AI Is Reshaping Who Gets Heard

For most of the internet age, political visibility ran through a familiar set of gatekeepers. Journalists, editors and social-media algorithms decided what surfaced and what sank. Conversational AI has now stacked another layer on top. Citizens ask chatbots and AI-enhanced search engines about candidates, policies and scandals, and the answers come back distilled from a model's training data and its makers' content policies.

The change is quiet, and it runs deep. Instead of sifting through several sources, a user is handed a single, authoritative-sounding summary. Disputed questions get flattened into a bland middle. Minority or emerging views can disappear entirely, either because they are thin in the training data or because a filter has marked them as harmful. For a growing number of people, the model is becoming the first draft of political reality, and it carries none of the transparency or accountability we once demanded of a public broadcaster or a newspaper.

This is not a hypothetical worry. When the researcher David Rozado ran eleven political orientation tests across two dozen large language models, twenty-three of them produced answers that sat left of centre, a pattern later studies have repeatedly confirmed. The point is not which way the models lean. The point is that they lean consistently, in a direction set by training data and design choices that no voter ever sees or votes on.

So the basic democratic question is straightforward. Who gets to define the boundaries of acceptable speech inside these systems, and on what mandate? The answer will shape not only what citizens see today, but what tomorrow's models learn from.

#### Wikipedia: battleground and blueprint

Nowhere is the collision between open knowledge and AI clearer than in Wikipedia and its sister projects. The encyclopaedia ranks among the most visited sites on the planet and acts as a reference layer for everything from voice assistants to search snippets. Its structured cousin, Wikidata, turns up again and again as a knowledge graph inside AI training and retrieval.

That centrality makes it a target. Election seasons bring waves of coordinated editing, as activists try to sanitise biographies, slip in favourable framings, or smear opponents. The platform has held up better than most social networks. Volunteer editors, working from community rules on neutrality and sourcing, patrol changes in real time, while machine-learning tools scan for likely vandalism and flag suspect edits for human review.

That hybrid model, algorithmic triage paired with human judgment, is a rough blueprint for knowledge governance in the AI era. It accepts that machines are needed to handle scale, but refuses to hand them the final call. And it is transparent. Disputes are logged, talk pages are public, and the rules are argued out in the open.

The launch of Grokipedia in October 2025 shows what the alternative looks like. Elon Musk's xAI positioned it as a neutral, fact-based rival to a Wikipedia he has called captured by activists, with entries generated and reviewed by the Grok model rather than a community of editors. The differences are revealing. There is no open editing, only a form for suggesting corrections. Provenance is murky, and researchers quickly found that large numbers of articles had been adapted, sometimes close to word for word, from the very encyclopaedia it set out to replace. One computational study of 1,800 matched article pairs found heavy textual overlap alongside systematic differences in framing on contested topics.

Strip out the open dispute resolution, the visible edit histories and the human arbitration, and you do not get neutrality. You get a confident summary whose biases are harder to see and harder to challenge. If AI companies and regulators are serious about pluralism, Wikimedia's messy, community-driven process deserves to be treated as a benchmark, not an afterthought.

#### The squeeze on political speech

At the same time, rules designed to curb opaque political advertising are narrowing the field of legitimate speech in less visible ways. The EU's Transparency and Targeting of Political Advertising regulation, the TTPA, imposes strict disclosure duties on political ads across every medium. Its definition is broad, reaching issue-based messaging, and it explicitly covers podcast publishers, streaming services and connected TV. In audio formats, a spoken disclaimer detailing sponsor, funding and targeting can eat much of a short ad slot, which makes the format close to unusable for paid political messaging.

The platforms have piled their own restrictions on top, and the pattern is consistent. Microsoft moved to ban political ads from September 2025. Google suspended them. Meta withdrew from political, electoral and social-issue advertising across its EU platforms in October 2025, calling the requirements unworkable. LinkedIn had shut the door years earlier. Large, established parties and governments can fall back on earned media, direct mail and ground operations. Smaller parties, new civic movements and under-resourced NGOs cannot.

Layer these constraints onto algorithmic curation and the effects get unpredictable. If models are trained mostly on mainstream news and heavily moderated platforms, and if the paid channels that once reached everyone else are choked off, the spectrum of visible political ideas can shrink without any explicit censorship order. The result is a quiet incumbency bias, baked into both the ad market and the informational substrate underneath it.

#### Beyond the comfort zone: where politics actually happens

One of the least discussed drivers of political invisibility is self-selection by campaigns themselves. Many democratic actors gravitate toward the outlets they personally respect, the quality newspapers, the policy magazines, the high-end news sites. Those outlets matter, but they mostly reach people who are already engaged and, often, already convinced.

The voters most likely to swing an outcome are somewhere else. They are in gaming apps, entertainment platforms and lifestyle sites, where politics is background noise rather than the main event. Reaching them at scale has always meant going where the attention actually sits, not where a campaign feels comfortable being seen. Plenty of civic and pro-democracy campaigns balk at that, because it feels unseemly to run next to a puzzle game or a reality-TV recap.

In the age of AI, that squeamishness carries a sharper cost. If models and recommender systems increasingly mirror mainstream consumption patterns, then actors who refuse to inhabit the spaces where most people spend their time will end up talking to ever narrower slices of the electorate. Making sure pluralist perspectives show up in the feeds, the search results and the AI answers that ordinary people meet requires a willingness to be present in those spaces, not just to criticise them from a distance.

#### Keeping humans in charge of judgment

The appeal of AI moderation and AI content generation is obvious. Scale, speed, consistency. For platforms drowning in hate speech, harassment and disinformation, systems that can filter or downrank at the point of ingestion look like a gift. For campaigns short on time and money, a model that drafts emails, posts and scripts in seconds looks just as tempting.

Community-governed projects point to a hard limit, though. You cannot automate away political judgment. Wikimedia's tools can flag a suspicious edit, but only a human, armed with context and a feel for language, can reliably tell satire from smear, or an honest mistake from a coordinated operation. The same holds for campaigns. AI can propose talking points. Deciding whether a message is fair, truthful and strategically sound stays a human job.

The risk in the current hype cycle is over-delegation. Institutions under budget and regulatory pressure start letting classifiers decide which political messages are too risky to run, letting models summarise complex debates without oversight, leaning on automated fact-checking to referee arguments that are really about values. What you get from that is not a neutral moderation regime. It is a technocratic filter, shaped by the blind spots of whoever trained it.

#### Designing AI as democratic infrastructure

If AI is here to stay, and every signal says it is, then the task for democracies is to shape it as infrastructure that supports pluralism rather than a black box that quietly narrows it. That means making real choices. Training datasets should draw on diverse sources, including community media and non-elite discourse, not only the most respectable outlets. Content policies should be built with input from a range of political actors, especially those representing marginalised groups, and they should be open to public scrutiny and appeal.

Regulators can help by moving past disclosure toward substantive standards for contestability and redress. If a party or civic group finds its messages systematically suppressed or distorted by an AI-powered system, there should be a clear, independent route to investigation and correction. Public institutions might also back open, auditable models and knowledge graphs alongside the proprietary ones, building on the Wikimedia ethos of transparency and community oversight.

Above all, democracies should resist the temptation to treat AI as an apolitical tool that can fix the messiness of public debate. Disagreement, agitation and discomfort are not bugs. They are signs that pluralism is still breathing. The job of AI should be to help citizens navigate that complexity, by making information findable, context clear and the rules of engagement fair, not to sand it all down into a frictionless but anaemic consensus.

In the contest to win the AI vote, the real electorate is not the models. It is the citizens whose perceptions they shape. Whether those citizens keep seeing democracy as a system that acknowledges their voices, or come to experience it as a scripted performance run by machines they do not control, will turn on political choices being made right now, largely out of public view.
