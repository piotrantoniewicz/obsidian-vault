[Part 3 ended with advice to score your opportunities](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/21-things-ai-could-do-here-heres-where-we-started-wendy-clow-wbljf/?trackingId=igxXX%2FF6RaavEzMC8oegBA%3D%3D) by difficulty and start at the easy end. This edition is about the thing that catches you out even when you do that, and it is the pattern behind the missteps I have made with AI this year.

I promised this last part would be written from the things that went wrong for me, so all four below are mine and all four are recent. In all four the tool did exactly what I asked. What I got wrong was building it ahead of where the organization actually was.

### The report with nothing to report

In May I built a daily summary of my own business numbers. Revenue, active clients, new conversations, subscribers, followers, all pulled together and waiting for me at six every morning.

Journey to Impact has one client on a fixed monthly retainer. The revenue line is the same number every month by definition. New conversations were often zero, which was the accurate figure at the time. The report was accurate and there was almost nothing in it that could change.

I turned it off in July, along with the collector behind it, and I will turn it back on when there is a CRM underneath it and enough happening to be worth reading. The build was less than a day of work, which is a cheap lesson at that price. The version of this that worries me is the nonprofit that buys a reporting tool on a three-year contract for the same reason.

### The easiest item on our list, which was not easy

Our audit at Habitat Michigan produced 21 opportunities. The one rated highest impact and lowest difficulty was a searchable place to keep our written procedures, so people stop asking the same three colleagues the same questions. I stand by that score. The tooling really is straightforward.

What the score does not capture is that a searchable library of procedures assumes you have written procedures current enough to be worth searching.

I found this out sideways, while building something adjacent. I wanted a set of plain documents describing the organization so any of our staff could hand them to an AI tool and get answers that fit us rather than a generic nonprofit. The staff section had been written during our audit interviews in the spring. By the time I picked it up in August, four of the people named in it were no longer with our organization and one who had joined was not in it at all. The program structure I had written down was tidy on paper and did not match how the work is actually divided between people.

Building the search tool would have taken an afternoon. Getting to the point where it had something accurate to read is still going. That second thing is a documentation project, and it has never been anybody's priority because it has never been the most urgent thing in a given week.

### The rollout that assumed we could roll out

I designed our training plan as a phased introduction, one department first, learn from that group, then widen. That is the cautious approach you are supposed to take and I built the whole sequence around it.

Then I checked the plan we actually pay for. The setting is organization-wide, on or off for everybody, and the per-department version is a feature of a more expensive tier. There was no way to give it to one department and not the rest.

The fix works and it is what we are doing, which is to control who gets trained rather than who gets access. But I had written a sequence that rested on a capability I had never confirmed we had, and I found out at the point where I was ready to start.

### The list that outran us

The fourth one is the audit itself.

Twenty-one opportunities came out of it. Twelve were rated high impact. Over the following months, working from the easy end as [Part 3](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/21-things-ai-could-do-here-heres-where-we-started-wendy-clow-wbljf/?trackingId=igxXX%2FF6RaavEzMC8oegBA%3D%3D) described, we finished about four of them. The other seventeen are still sitting on the list.

I do not regret running the audit and I would run it again. What I would change is what I did with the output. A list of 21 items handed to a lean team functions as a backlog, and a backlog has a cost. Everyone can see the seventeen things not being done. I built a plan sized for an organization with more capacity than the one I actually work in.

### What the four have in common

In all of them the AI part is the part that worked. What I had not done was ask what each thing assumed was already in place.

A daily metrics report assumes something generating enough data to move. A searchable procedure library assumes written, current procedures. A context document assumes somebody has recorded how the organization actually works. A phased rollout assumes your plan supports phasing. A 21-item roadmap assumes someone has the hours to build 21 things.

So I ask one question now before building anything, which is what does this depend on, and do we have it. Documentation at a nonprofit never reaches a finished state, so this is a question to ask while you build.

### Why nonprofits run into this more than most

Starting with AI is cheap and quick. Building the foundations underneath it is slow, and that work is rarely funded. I have never seen a grant that pays for writing down how your programs work, and I have never been asked to present a documentation project at a board meeting.

There is also real pressure pointing the other way. A funder asks what you are doing with AI, a board member forwards an article, and the easiest thing to show either of them is a tool. Showing them that you spent two months getting your program records accurate is a harder conversation, even though it is the thing that makes the tool work.

Lean teams also do not have anyone whose job includes keeping written things current, so the gap between what your documents say and what is true tends to open without anyone noticing and takes a while to close.

### What I do differently now

Before I build anything, I write down what it reads from, and then I check that the thing exists and is current. That single step would have caught three of the four above.

If the answer includes the phrase "our procedures" or "our records," I now treat the procedures or the records as the actual project, and the AI tool as the small piece at the end of it.

I confirm what our plan supports at the start, before I design around it.

And I build the smallest version that runs on what we have today, instead of the version that would be right if the foundations were already in place.
