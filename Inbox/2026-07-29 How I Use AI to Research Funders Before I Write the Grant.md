A family foundation had been sitting on our grant list for weeks, and everything about it looked easy. There was no submission deadline and no application window to plan around, which made it feel like the kind of opportunity you could take a swing at during any quiet week. That is usually where the trouble starts, because a funder with no deadline never creates the pressure that makes you do your homework.

Last week I finally did the homework. It took about twenty minutes, because I had AI do the reading.

Three things came back that the homepage never mentioned. Our program area was not one of the three priorities they actually fund. They favor a region of the country we are not in, and Michigan falls into the category of places they will consider but do not go looking for. Then, sitting in the frequently asked questions, there was this: *one application per calendar year, and a decline locks you out for two full years.*

That last detail changed the entire decision. A rolling deadline feels like a free shot right up until you learn that a no costs you the next two years. What looked like a low stakes application was actually a one chance opportunity where a rushed submission would be worse than not applying at all.

I would never have learned any of that from the page everyone reads.

### The step nobody talks about

The Chronicle of Philanthropy quoted me last spring on where AI gets dangerous in grant writing, and I stand behind that. AI will happily help you sound larger and more established than you actually are, and funders can tell.

But there is a step that happens well before you write a single word, and it gets almost no attention. You have to decide which funders deserve your hours in the first place. That decision is made by reading, and **reading long dull documents with real attention is one of the things this technology does better than a tired human at four in the afternoon.**

Here is the math that made me build a workflow around it. A serious grant application takes somewhere between fifteen and forty hours once you count the narrative, the budget, the attachments, the internal approvals, and the follow up. If you have thirty funders on your list and even four of them were never going to fund you, that is potentially a hundred hours spent on applications that had no path to yes before you started.

The application that comes back declined is not the expensive part, because at least that one had a real chance. The expensive part is the week you spend on a funder whose own published guidelines already ruled you out on page four.

### The workflow, in five steps

**1\. Collect the real pages, not the homepage.**

Go get the guidelines, the eligibility requirements, the frequently asked questions, the deadline or review cycle page, and whatever page lists what they will not fund. The homepage is marketing and it is written to make you feel welcome. The disqualifying details almost always live in the frequently asked questions or the exclusions list.

**2\. Extract the same seven facts for every funder.**

Ask for funding priorities, geography served, eligibility requirements, the grant range from smallest to largest, deadlines and review cycle, what they explicitly will not fund, and the reapplication rules after both an award and a decline. Using the same seven fields every time means you can lay funders side by side and compare them honestly, rather than comparing your vague memory of one against your enthusiasm for another.

Tell it to write "not stated" when a fact is missing from the pages you provided. That one instruction prevents the most common failure, which is a confident invented deadline.

**3\. Write your own organizational profile once.**

Capture your legal name, EIN, tax status, annual budget, geography, populations served, program areas, and the things you already know have gotten you screened out before. This takes about ten minutes and you reuse it for every funder from then on.

Skipping this step is why most people get useless results. Without a profile to score against, the AI has nothing concrete to compare and it will simply tell you the funder sounds like a wonderful match for your important work.

**4\. Force an honest verdict.**

This is the step that actually matters, and it is the one I want you to take away even if you ignore everything else.

Tell it plainly that its job is to find the reasons you would be declined. Something close to this:

> "Assume I am going to apply, and argue against me. Give me the three most likely reasons this funder declines us. If our fit is weak, say so plainly rather than finding me an encouraging angle."

When you leave that instruction out, you get a cheerful advocate. When you put it in, you get the two year lockout clause. I ran both versions on the same funder and got opposite recommendations, which is a useful reminder that these tools are shaped far more by how you ask than by what they know.

**5\. Decide, and write down why.**

You are choosing among three outcomes: apply, apply only with a specific reframe, or pass. Whichever you pick, record the reasoning somewhere durable. Next July you will be looking at the same list, and the version of you that already did this research deserves to be heard.

### What it cannot do

AI reads and it organizes. It does not decide.

Verify anything that gates a real decision against the funder's own page before you act on it, particularly deadline dates, grant ranges, and eligibility rules. A hallucinated deadline is genuinely worse than doing no research at all, because you will trust it.

It also cannot tell you whether your program story is strong enough to win. In our case the research surfaced one legitimate angle, which was framing our home repair work as health and independence for elderly homeowners rather than as affordable housing. Whether that story is compelling enough to spend our one annual application on is a judgment call, and that judgment belongs to the people who know the program.

### Where to start this week

Pull the three funders on your fall calendar that you are least sure about. Find their frequently asked questions page. Ask the uncomfortable version of the question and see what comes back.

Twenty minutes per funder, and you will either save yourself a week of work or you will go into the application knowing precisely which objection you need to answer.
