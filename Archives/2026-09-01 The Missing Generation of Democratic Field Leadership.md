[Amy Pritchard, ❌👑](https://open.substack.com/users/3645766-amy-pritchard?utm_source=mentions) And I am standing up **Democracy University**. We’re building it around several things: introductory training for people new to organizing, additional skills training and mentoring for people with some experience, and a way for senior campaign staff to network and share what they’ve learned. We’re also working to make it possible for these organizers to stay in politics with good-paying jobs and benefits.

Hundreds of people have already signed up to be trained and to serve as trainers, and hundreds, if not thousands, more are looking for jobs. [Amy wrote about this in her last post,](https://democracyuniversity.substack.com/p/the-pebble-in-the-hourglass) and it’s real.

But signups alone don’t solve the deeper problem. We also have to make up for a missing generation - thousands of people who should have gained hands-on experience on presidential campaigns, especially the long, grinding nominating process that got truncated in 2020 and disappeared almost entirely in 2024.

Those experienced presidential campaign veterans usually become some of our best campaign strategists. Right now, we’re not producing enough of them.

Before I was a media consultant, I was an organizer. I spent almost two years working to elect Walter Mondale president - eleven straw polls, caucuses, and primaries. Endless phone calls, thousands of miles driven, county meetings, volunteers recruited and lost, always trying to find one more person willing to take responsibility.

That was my apprenticeship. My Malcolm Gladwell 10,000 hours. We lost. But I learned.

Nobody taught me organizing in a three-day seminar. I learned it by organizing - by making mistakes, watching experienced people work, being given more responsibility, and eventually learning how to develop other people.

For generations, presidential campaigns served as one of the Democratic Party’s largest leadership-development programs. That system has been badly disrupted.

The result: a missing generation of Democratic field leadership.

Not a missing generation of people who’ve worked on campaigns - we have plenty of campaign alumni. The missing generation is organizing directors, regional directors, and experienced field organizers who know how to turn money, data, offices, and volunteers into an actual organization.

*We need to train thousands of new organizers, and help more experienced staff learn how to lead.*

**The ladder broke in the middle**

It would be wrong to say there was no Democratic organizing in 2020. There was a large, serious presidential primary through 2019 and into early 2020. Elizabeth Warren reported more than 1,000 staff and over 100 offices nationally. Michael Bloomberg briefly employed roughly 2,400 people across 43 states and territories.

Thousands of people entered the profession, knocking doors and learning the basics.

Then the process stopped. The competitive primary effectively ended after Super Tuesday. COVID arrived. Offices closed. Door-to-door canvassing stopped, and the Biden campaign didn’t begin limited face-to-face canvassing again until October.

The problem wasn’t that nobody got trained in 2020. The problem was that the traditional organizer-development ladder broke in the middle. That ladder normally has three stages.

**A competitive primary** recruits the next generation. Multiple candidates need organizers. They hire young people, send them into unfamiliar communities, and teach them how to hold one-on-one meetings, recruit volunteers, identify local leaders, and build teams. Because there are multiple campaigns, there are multiple doors into the profession - people without established political connections get real opportunities, and different campaigns test different organizing cultures and strategies.

**A long primary** identifies and promotes the best people. Field organizers become regional directors. Regional directors learn to manage staff. State directors learn how to scale an operation, allocate resources, and change a plan that isn’t working. The best organizers aren’t just the people who hit their contact goals - they’re the people who can develop volunteers, solve problems, and help other organizers improve.

**The general election** is the final exam. Organizers learn to build staging locations, cut and distribute turf, manage thousands of volunteers, chase ballots, and hold an organization together under pressure. They learn what happens when the plan meets reality.

In 2020, many young organizers got the first part of that education. They missed the final semester. Then in 2024, there was no serious Democratic presidential primary to recruit and develop the next class - Biden secured the nomination without facing a major challenger.

When Kamala Harris became the nominee, she inherited an existing campaign and ground operation built for someone else. She had weeks, not years, to make it her own - not enough time to build a leadership culture and move the strongest people through the normal progression from organizer to manager.

**The biggest loss was in the middle**

The Democratic Party still had experienced people at the top. Harris brought veterans of previous campaigns into senior positions - no shortage of people who knew how to write a national plan, interpret polling, or manage TV advertising.

The more serious weakness was one or two levels below that:

Organizing directors, Deputy organizing directors, Regional directors…

Experienced field organizers capable of coaching first-time staff

These are the people who translate the national plan into a functioning local organization. They know when the dashboard is wrong. They know whether a volunteer team is real or the same four people counted repeatedly, which local leader can bring twenty people into a room, and when organizers are hearing something at the doors that the models are missing.

Those skills come from repetition - and we ran out of repetitions. Someone who entered politics at 22 in 2020 was only 26 in 2024, having worked a shortened primary and a largely virtual presidential campaign before being asked to supervise first-time organizers. Someone who was 22 during the last fully traditional Democratic primary, in 2016, was already 30 by 2024.

That means almost the entire under-30 Democratic field workforce entered 2024 without ever experiencing a normal presidential general-election field operation. That’s an extraordinary gap.

**More mobilization. Less organizing.**

A campaign can hire people quickly to knock doors, make calls, and send texts. It takes much longer to develop organizers who can identify leaders and teach other people to organize. Those are not the same things.

A canvasser’s product is voter contacts. An organizer’s product is more organizers. A field leader’s product is more leaders.

**When experienced managers are scarce, campaigns lean on whatever’s easiest to count: doors knocked, calls attempted, texts sent, shifts filled. Those numbers tell us activity is happening - but activity isn’t organization.**

An experienced organizer asks different questions. Who became a leader this week? Which volunteer can now run a meeting without staff? What are voters telling us that our research is missing? What will still exist in this community after the campaign leaves?

A canvass operation can disappear the day after Election Day. A developed leader may stay active for forty years.

**Less local knowledge**

Competitive primaries force campaigns into communities they’d otherwise ignore - rural counties, working-class towns, communities of color, states that will get almost no attention in the fall. Organizers learn where people gather, which institutions matter, and what arguments sound different outside Washington.

Without that experience, campaigns become more dependent on voter files, models, and national messages. They may know a great deal about voters as data points. They know far less about them as members of actual communities.

That distinction matters more every year, as trust in institutions declines and voters increasingly get information through creators, community pages, group chats, and local networks. A campaign cannot understand that environment entirely from a dashboard. It needs organizers who understand the community.

**Some of the apprenticeship moved outside campaigns**

Political campaigns aren’t the only places people learn to organize. Some of the most serious organizing in recent years has happened around labor, abortion rights, immigrant rights, racial justice, schools, and housing. In some places, the apprenticeship didn’t disappear - it moved outside the formal campaign structure.

Minnesota is the clearest example. When Operation Metro Surge brought thousands of federal immigration agents into the state, the most important organizing response didn’t begin in a presidential field office. It grew out of immigrant-rights organizations, faith communities, unions, schools, lawyers, and ordinary people trying to protect their neighbors - building communication networks, monitoring federal activity, delivering groceries, helping with rent, and showing up when a neighbor was in danger.

By early February, the Immigrant Defense Network reported that nearly 30,000 Minnesotans had been trained as constitutional observers to monitor and document law-enforcement activity. Human Rights Watch found that the infrastructure looked spontaneous from the outside but was actually built through months of planning and training.

That’s actual organizing. It began with a real need, not a contact goal. People learned whom they could trust, how to communicate quickly, how to take responsibility, and how to keep working under extraordinary pressure. Nobody handed them a walk packet, told them to hit a quota, and sent them home.

And those networks didn’t disappear when the crisis passed. After Peggy Flanagan’s victory in the 2026 Democratic Senate primary, she said much of the organizing built to support neighbors during Operation Metro Surge shifted toward turning out voters in the primary. The Minneapolis Star Tribune concluded the grassroots reaction helped fuel her nearly 20-point victory and record turnout - she won almost 60 percent of the vote in a race expected to be far closer.

Her volunteers knocked on more than 200,000 doors between June 1 and the August 11 primary, despite a serious financial disadvantage: Angie Craig had raised roughly $12 million to Flanagan’s $6 million as of July 22.

We should be careful about overstating the connection - not everyone who organized against ICE became a Flanagan volunteer, and no campaign should claim ownership over a movement built by communities protecting themselves. But the connection is real. People who learned to protect their neighbors became people capable of mobilizing voters. Relationships built during a social crisis became political capacity.

That’s the part of the story Democrats should study. Some of the best organizers are already working in immigrant rights, labor, schools, reproductive freedom, housing, climate, and community defense. Our job isn’t to absorb those movements or treat them as temporary vendors - it’s to recognize the leadership they’re producing, treat their organizations as partners, and build pathways that let their organizers bring their skills into electoral politics without destroying the trust that made them effective.

**Fewer doors into the profession**

A multicandidate presidential primary creates many separate hiring doors. A young organizer without connections in Washington can prove themselves by building something in a county far from headquarters. An incumbent campaign relies more heavily on existing relationships - narrowing the pipeline and reducing the number of organizing models being tested.

Competitive primaries can be wasteful and chaotic. But they’re also laboratories - some campaigns build strong volunteer organizations, others burn young managers out, and people learn from both. Without a competitive primary, there are fewer experiments and fewer people moving rapidly into real responsibility.

**The 2024 campaign had “bodies” - but bodies aren’t experience**

By the closing weeks of 2024, the Harris operation had 353 field offices and more than 2,500 staff members in the battleground states. That’s a large field operation. But hiring 2,500 people is not the same as having 2,500 experienced organizers.

Formal training helps - the DNC says it trained more than 10,000 party leaders, staff, and activists during 2024. But seminars can’t replace months of recruiting volunteers, managing turf, resolving conflicts, and running an actual GOTV operation. You can teach someone the voter file in an afternoon. You cannot teach judgment in an afternoon.

You can put 2,000 people on a payroll overnight. It takes time to put them through the apprenticeship that gives them the judgment and experience they’ll need to lead.

This didn’t begin with COVID. Democratic presidential campaigns operated nearly 1,000 field offices in 2008, roughly 780 in 2012, 538 in 2016, and roughly 350 in 2024. Offices alone don’t win elections - an empty office is just a lease - but they’re places where organizers train volunteers and pass knowledge down. As the infrastructure shrank, so did some of the places where the apprenticeship happened.

**This did not, by itself, cost Democrats the election**

I wouldn’t argue the leadership-development gap alone caused the 2024 defeat. The economy, inflation, and the broader political environment were much larger forces.

The Harris field operation probably helped narrow the loss - research generally finds field offices have small but real effects, often around one percentage point, and Harris performed relatively better in heavily contested battlegrounds than Democrats did in states with far less campaign activity.

The problem wasn’t the absence of a ground game. It was that the ground game had fewer experienced people in the middle who could turn money, offices, data, and volunteers into a durable organization - meaning more dependence on short-term paid canvassing, less effective volunteer development, and less permanent capacity left behind after the campaign closed.

**Rebuild the ladder**

The answer isn’t another election-year training program. We need to rebuild the apprenticeship system.

First, keep the strongest organizers employed - and recognize where organizing is already happening. State parties, unions, ballot-measure campaigns, and community-organizing programs should retain the strongest people from 2024 and 2026, but we should also recruit from and learn from organizers already developing through immigrant-rights work, labor, and housing fights. We shouldn’t pull people out of their communities to fill campaign jobs - we should invest in the organizations where they’re already building trust and give emerging leaders room to move between issue organizing and electoral work.

Second, give people real responsibility in 2026 and 2027. People become managers by managing. The best 2024 organizers should be running programs in 2026; the best 2026 managers should be coaching apprentices in 2027 - on real campaigns and organizing programs, not webinars.

Third, evaluate managers by the leaders they produce. Contact goals and turnout numbers matter, but an organizing director should also be judged by how many field organizers became regional directors and how much of the organization remained after Election Day. A manager who hits every door goal but develops no one has only completed half the assignment.

Fourth, create a leadership-retention system for the 2028 presidential primary. A competitive primary could start rebuilding the pipeline, but when candidates leave the race, their strongest organizers shouldn’t simply be laid off. The party, state parties, and allied organizations need a system for immediately retaining and redeploying that talent - the primary should produce leaders for the general election, not casualties of the primary.

**The real shortage**

Democrats do not suffer from a shortage of smart people, or people willing to knock doors and work impossible hours. We suffer from a weakened system for turning those people into experienced organizers - and turning experienced organizers into leaders. That system used to run almost automatically, through repeated campaigns. It no longer does. We have to build it intentionally.

And we have to recognize that some of the people we need are already being trained in places the traditional campaign establishment doesn’t always see - organizing workers, defending immigrants, fighting for schools and housing and their neighbors, building real networks around real needs.

The strongest organizers from 2024 should become the managers of 2026. The strongest managers from 2026 should train the apprentices of 2027. The strongest apprentices from 2027 should become the regional and organizing directors of 2028. And the leaders emerging from unions, community organizations, and social movements should be treated as partners in building the future of the Democratic Party - not lists to be rented six weeks before Election Day.

We need to train at all levels, but we don’t simply need more organizers. We need to start producing - and recognizing - organizers who know how to produce leaders.
