## WHERE MY MIND IS

### Maybe AI adoption isn’t the problem. Maybe the way we are adopting it is.

Last week, I opened the AASP Summit in Milwaukee talking about AI readiness.

I went in thinking a lot about something I have been saying for a while now: *AI readiness is not the same thing as AI adoption.*

An organization can have ChatGPT. Copilot. An AI policy. A few enthusiastic early adopters. A board member asking what the AI strategy is.

And still not be ready.

So obviously what stuck with me from the session was what people brought back into the conversation afterward.

Questions about the future of work. About trust. About whether AI is actually improving the work — or simply helping us produce more of it.

One question in particular has been following me around:

> ***How much of the problem is really choosing right AI tools — and how much comes from the way people inside an organization are thinking about and using it?***

We spend enormous energy trying to make the output better. Better prompts. Better models. Better workflows. Better accuracy.

But sometimes the thing that needs work is not the outputs or tools we choose. It is the ecosystem around it.

- Does someone have permission to question what the tool produced?
- Does the person reviewing it have enough time to actually review it?
- Does leadership know where the boundaries are?
- Can someone say, “I don’t think AI belongs here,” without being treated as resistant to change?
- Does the organization know what it wants people to keep learning, practicing, questioning, and doing for themselves?

Those are not side conversations we have after we finish talking about AI.

***They are the real AI conversations.***

That is where my mind is this week.

## ONE IDEA TO SIT WITH

### I had the data to create an AI Equity Score. I chose not to.

This week, the **AI Equity Project 2026** went out into the world.

This is the third year of the project. More than 2,400 organizational responses across those three years have helped us understand how nonprofits are navigating AI, including 880 organizations participating in the 2026 research.

And with three years of data, you can calculate a lot of things.

Readiness. Familiarity. Governance. Data equity. Accountability. Community participation. Funding. Policy.

Back in 2024, I seriously considered bringing some of those measures together into a single AI Equity Score.

Yes, I could see the appeal immediately.

A score is concrete. It gives an organization something to point to. It can help people say, “We are here, and we would like to get there.” One number also travels remarkably well through reports, presentations, dashboards, funding conversations, and yes, LinkedIn posts.

But three years later, **I still choose not to create one.**

The more data we collected, the more I realized that the contradictions were often more useful than the ranking.

Nearly 70% of respondents in 2026 reported some organizational AI use. Only 12% said that use was guided by organization-wide practices or expectations. Only 21% had a formal accountability process in place if AI caused harm.

So how should I score an organization that uses AI extensively but cannot tell me who is responsible when something goes wrong?

What about one with a beautifully written policy but little community participation?

What about an organization that has moved slowly precisely because staff are asking hard questions about privacy, trust, equity, or mission?

Should speed earn points? Should caution lose them?

At some point, that stops being a math question. It becomes a values decision.

> **The moment I assign a weight to something, I am no longer only measuring it. I am making an argument about its importance.**

That does not mean scores are inherently bad. I make my living helping organizations collect, analyze, and make sense of data.

But measurement has power.

A score does not simply describe what matters. It can quietly decide what counts as progress.

That is why the 2026 report says:

> **One score would diminish the tensions the data asks us to notice.**

An organization may feel ready while lacking shared guidance.

It may have a policy without clear accountability. It may use AI widely while communities have little voice in how it is adopted. A high score could reward visible activity without revealing who holds power, who carries risk, or whether caution is being respected.

*Accountability is the rope between intention and consequence.*

That is why I love this image from the report.

One person climbing. Another person holding the rope. Trust, risk, dependence, and responsibility are all visible at once.

Accountability is often discussed as though it were a box an organization can check.

But accountability becomes real when we can answer harder questions: Who is holding what? Who is exposed if something slips? Who can intervene? Who responds when harm occurs? Who is responsible for repair?

A score might tell me that an accountability process exists.

It is much worse at showing me where the weight actually lands.

That is part of why I am increasingly interested in data that leaves us with a better question, not merely a cleaner number.

> Sometimes clarity comes from a number.
> 
> ***Sometimes clarity comes from refusing to pretend numbers can hold everything.***

## ONE THING TO SEE

### Sometimes the better demographic question makes the spreadsheet harder. And that should be acceptable.

I am working on a survey that was also run a couple of years ago, so one goal is to compare the new results with the earlier ones.

Then we got to the racial identity question.

In the earlier survey, one response option was “Middle Eastern.” In the new version, after discussion about terminology and identity, that category changed to “Southwest Asian and North African (SWANA).”

That sounds like a small wording change.

Analytically, it is not.

If 8% selected “Middle Eastern” before and 12% select “SWANA” now, I cannot simply say representation increased by four percentage points. Some people may identify differently with the newer wording. The measure itself changed.

That is one of the tensions in longitudinal research: consistency makes comparison easier, but better questions sometimes require us to change what we asked before.

So I would rather improve the question and document the change than preserve wording purely for a cleaner trend line.

We can still compare the years. We just need to be honest that the categories are related, not identical, and interpret any difference with that context.

> ***Good longitudinal research is not always about asking exactly the same question forever. Sometimes it is about knowing when consistency is serving the research — and when consistency is asking people to carry the cost of our convenience.***

## FROM MY DESK

A few things occupying my brain, browser tabs, whiteboard, calendar — and occasionally the floor around my desk — right now:

**01\. AI Equity Project 2026.**  
Three years. More than 2,400 organizational responses. One hundred pages. Many, many spreadsheets. A framework, workbooks, a checklist, community reflections, and approximately seventeen moments where I thought, *surely this report is finished now.*

It is finished now.:)

This year, the biggest questions are less about whether nonprofits are using AI and more about who is shaping that use, who is accountable for its consequences, and whether governance, funding, participation, and evaluation are keeping pace with adoption.

I am incredibly proud of this body of work.

And also very ready for *you* to carry some of it now.

**02\. Lighthouse humans.**  
I saw this recently and immediately wanted to keep it.

> “Lighthouse humans don’t stop the storm. They stop you from believing the storm is all there is.”

That feels like a decent description of the humans I am most grateful for.

The people who do not pretend the work is easy, that the systems are fine, or that the questions are simple.

But they remind you there is still a shore.

I think good colleagues, thoughtful leaders, careful researchers, kind friends, and the occasional brave truth-teller all do some version of that.

For the record, I aspire to be at least 12% more lighthouse and 8% less raccoon-in-a-spreadsheet this fall.

## BEFORE YOU GO

**Have you experienced a data or AI harm?**

Since 2024, alongside the annual AI Equity Project survey, we have kept an ongoing space where people can tell us about harms they have experienced or witnessed involving data or AI.

Some are obvious.

Others are ordinary enough that we may not immediately call them harm.

- A community missing from a dataset.
- An AI-generated stereotype quietly making its way into someone’s work.
- A person being asked for deeply personal information with no clear reason why.
- A staff member being expected to use AI without guidance.
- A tool making an important decision without a meaningful path for someone to question it.

Those submissions have helped shape another part of this work: [AI Equity Harm Signals — 24 Ways AI Can Hurt, and How to Notice Sooner.](https://www.namastedata.org/freebies)

If something has happened in your organization, community, or work that you think we should be paying attention to, I would genuinely like to hear it.

[Report your data or AI harm here.](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSfJMN6TpayQlyUm_S8MEHUnTBBzXEKKH_KTQvTGxJ_ZJySoQg/viewform)

You do not need a perfect explanation for what happened.

**Sometimes** ***“something about this felt wrong”*** **is enough reason to start paying attention.**

## Why This Newsletter Exists

A lot of what I work on at **[Namaste Data](http://www.namastedata.org/)** follows me beyond the workday.

*Who gets counted. Who gets missed. What a survey result actually means. Whether the demographic data tells a different story than the overall average. Who makes the AI decision. Who has the power to question it. What happens after we collect all this information.*

All very relaxing material for a brain that is supposedly struggles to choose dinner every night.

This newsletter gives those questions somewhere to sit — with curiosity, humor, the occasional Vancouver pictures, and you.

They are also the questions I spend my working days helping nonprofits and philanthropy untangle: designing better surveys, making sense of complicated data, finding the story underneath the percentages, preparing for AI without abandoning judgment, and building practices that are useful ***because*** they are thoughtful, not merely efficient.

I care deeply about this work enough to say I want us to have enough room to breathe while we do it.

So bring your complicated questions. Your unfinished thoughts. Your “something about this feels off, but I cannot explain it yet.”

Those are often my favorite places to begin.

There is room for those here.

And having written to you for the past few years, I hope you know you belong here, *always*.
