**Fiona Fung** leads the teams behind Claude Code and Cowork at Anthropic (overseeing Boris Cherny and the entire engineering and PM team). Before Anthropic, she spent 11 years at Microsoft building Visual Studio and TypeScript and then moved to Meta, where she started Facebook Marketplace (now generating over $100 billion in GMV annually), worked on Meta’s first smart glasses and AR glasses, and led infrastructure, growth, integrity, and safety teams at Instagram. She’s been an engineer for over 25 years and has a unique perspective on how the role of building software is changing.

## My biggest takeaways from this conversation:

1. **Anthropic engineers now ship 8x more code per quarter than they did a few years ago.** This number was stable for over four years and then shot straight up this year. With PMs, designers, and engineers all checking in code at that throughput, the bottleneck has shifted from “How do we build this?” to “How do we know this works as intended?”
2. **The biggest unsolved problem for teams today is verification.** Coding is largely solved. But how do you verify that the experience you built is what you intended? Fiona’s team uses a “bad vs. sad” framework: bad is an unrecoverable error (like a crash), sad is a recoverable pain point (like flickering or drop in conversation). They give each team agency to build and ship quickly, but they track bad and sad events for their surface areas to quickly identify issues.
3. **The roles are blurring—engineers need product sense, PMs need to code, everyone becomes a builder.** PMs are no longer bottlenecked waiting for engineering, and engineers are no longer blocked waiting for design or content. The most valuable people are generalists with strong product sense and the agency to ship end-to-end.
4. **As engineers increasingly work independently with their own teams of agents, teams need new ways to learn from each other.** Fiona’s team started “pairwise programming lunches,” where engineers work side by side, not necessarily on the same thing, and pick up new patterns by watching how others use Claude Code and Cowork.
5. **Fiona’s team has shifted from six-month roadmaps to just-in-time monthly planning.** She tried doing lightweight six-month roadmaps when she joined Claude Code, but a few months in, she realized her team had barely referenced them. Now they do monthly planning on a simple spreadsheet with a simple list of this month’s priorities.
6. **Anthropic built a dashboard that counts how often users swear at Claude Code.** Back in September, amid visible user frustration, an engineer suggested tracking swear words and Fiona loved it. It’s become a proxy for things evals struggle to capture: whether the experience is actually delightful, not just technically accurate.
7. **When Fiona hires managers, they have to start as individual contributors.** This gives them time to learn the codebase, build rapport with the team, and understand what it’s like to be an engineer on the team before taking on management responsibilities. It prevents the trap of immediately reaching for your “manager toolbox” instead of learning the specific context.
8. **Fiona uses “routines” to automate her daily rituals as a manager.** She used to read user feedback channels over coffee and hand-pick fixes to assign teammates, Now a Claude “routine” runs every morning, kicking off agents that analyze feedback across multiple channels, identify themes, and generate PRs to address issues. Her prediction is that work is shifting from manual synchronous prompting to asynchronous agent management (i.e. loops).
9. **Look for latent demand to discover new business opportunities.** Cowork emerged when the team noticed that non-coders were using Claude Code for tasks like analyzing MRIs or recovering wedding photos. That signal—people jumping through hoops to make something work with your product—tells you there’s something there.
10. **One of Claude Code’s cultural values is: you have permission to kill any process that isn’t working.** Fiona brought in six-month planning from her previous experience, then killed it herself when it wasn’t serving the team. Always ask: is this process still serving its purpose?
11. **Another core principle: “What’s better than me doing it? Having Claude do it.”** This pushes everyone to keep checking whether a task can be automated—even writing the post that announces a model launch. Fiona admits that after decades of shipping software by hand, she still has to remind herself to ask it.
12. **Culture, not code, is what keeps her up at night.** To Fiona, culture is a living thing, not a poster on a wall. Her nightmare is the manager who says, “everything’s fine” while the room is on fire. She pushes hard for open talk about what’s not going well, because that’s the only way the team can fix it together.
