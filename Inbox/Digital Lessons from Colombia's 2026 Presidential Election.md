A year ago, almost nobody outside Colombian legal circles had heard of Abelardo de la Espriella. He was a flamboyant defence lawyer from Barranquilla known for representing some of the country's most controversial clients, for owning clothing and alcohol brands and a Miami restaurant, and for having once recorded music albums. Not exactly the typical presidential profile.

\-

The Tiger Who Came From Nowhere

This weekend, de la Espriella was confirmed as president-elect of Colombia with 12.9 million votes, the most ever cast for a winning presidential candidate in Colombian history. He achieved this with a movement that did not formally exist as a political party, starting from essentially zero name recognition nationally, running against a well-funded left-wing senator backed by an incumbent government, and winning a second round by less than a percentage point in one of the highest-turnout elections the country had ever seen.

The explanation for this is not simple. Colombia is a complicated country, deeply polarised, battered by insecurity and frustrated with the Petro government's handling of peace negotiations and the economy. Any serious analysis of the election has to grapple with those realities. Some of the tactics used by the campaign also raised serious concerns, including questions about bloc voting, the role of evangelical church networks, and pressure from employers on their staff. Context matters enormously, and what worked in the specific Colombian political moment of 2026 will not automatically translate elsewhere. Read this as a set of observations about digital strategy, not a recommendation to import a playbook wholesale.

With that caveat front and centre, the digital operation that put "El Tigre" in the presidential palace deserves serious study. Because stripped of the Colombian specifics, it is one of the most instructive political communication campaigns in Latin America in a decade.

#### Building a Movement Before Building a Party

Most political campaigns start with a party, inherit its structure, use its infrastructure, and then go looking for voters. De la Espriella did the opposite. He started with a digital movement, built an audience, and used that audience to manufacture the political legitimacy required to get on the ballot.

Defensores de la Patria (Defenders of the Homeland) began not as a registered political entity but as a social media community. During the first half of 2025, supporter groups started forming organically on social networks and at regional events, long before the campaign was formally registered. The campaign website did not look like a political party page. It looked like a membership platform. It offered personalised referral links so that supporters could recruit other supporters and track their own contribution to the movement's growth. It had a WhatsApp join link embedded directly on the page. The language was military and fraternal: "kit de batalla," "ejército de patriotas," "misión."

The strategic concept the campaign built around was what analysts later described as "rejection and revenge," an emotional architecture that gave voters who hated the Petro government not just a candidate to vote for but a community to belong to and a collective act of vengeance to participate in. That is a different thing from a policy platform. It is an identity. And identity travels on digital networks far more efficiently than policy does.

By July 2025, De la Espriella formally registered the Defensores de la Patria campaign committee and launched a signature drive to secure independent ballot access, eventually collecting 4.8 million signatures, of which nearly two million were validated by electoral authorities. The signature drive was itself a digital mobilisation exercise: each supporter was assigned a personalised link, incentivised to recruit others, and guided through the logistics of collecting and submitting physical signatures via detailed online instructions. The campaign effectively gamified civic participation.

The lesson is transferable and profound. If you are starting from outside the established party system, your first job is not to announce a candidacy. It is to build a community with a shared identity that feels worth belonging to. The ballot access comes later. The followers come first.

#### The Brand Came Before the Manifesto

The "El Tigre" nickname was not a slogan. It was a symbolic anchor that collapsed his entire political persona into a single, instantly legible image: strength, aggression, an apex predator. Every element of the campaign's visual identity extended from that central metaphor.

The tiger appeared as logo, merchandise, digital sticker, TikTok filter, campaign slogan ("Firme por la Patria"), and eventually as the branding that Salvación Nacional adopted on its congressional ballots, voters knew the tiger logo before they knew the party name. Rather than generic campaign merchandise, the team produced luxury goods including a watch called "Tigris," a Swiss-movement piece framed as a patriotic artefact. Buying one was positioned as participation in a cause, not a purchase.

This is the logic of lifestyle branding applied to politics, and it works differently from traditional campaign identity because it invites voters to carry the brand rather than simply receive the message. A sticker on a phone, a watch on a wrist, a filter on a TikTok video: these are acts of public identity declaration that every social media algorithm treats as earned content rather than paid advertising. The campaign spent relatively little on Meta advertising but dominated digital conversation because its supporters were doing the distribution work for free.

Unlike other right-wing candidates who spent heavily on Meta advertising, De la Espriella managed to dominate the online conversation, particularly on X, through a network of right-wing influencers, activists, and engaged supporters rather than paid placement.

The lesson: if you are a new political force without broadcast budget, your brand needs to be something people want to display, not something you push at them. Design for sharing, not for exposure.

#### Short, Simple, Emotional. Then Repeat.

One of the most cited analyses of the campaign, published by La Silla Vacía after the result, drew a direct parallel with Clinton's 2016 defeat to Trump: Cepeda filled expensive public squares that held at most 30,000 people, while De la Espriella could reach millions through a single digital appearance. Cepeda's scripted speeches did not compete with De la Espriella's campaign songs, short videos, and brief direct posts that any voter could understand in ten seconds.

This contrast is not about charisma. It is about format discipline. De la Espriella's team understood that political content on TikTok and Instagram does not operate like a rally speech and does not operate like a news interview. It is entertainment that happens to carry a political message, and it lives or dies in the first two seconds. The campaign produced a relentless volume of short, punchy, emotionally direct content. At one point, this included an AI-generated video of De la Espriella dancing with lion-headed humanoid figures to loud music, framed as a campaign moment and designed to go viral rather than to communicate policy. Critics called it a TikTok show rather than a presidential campaign. It got shared anyway.

The political format discipline extended to message consistency. Across every platform, in every format, the core emotional proposition stayed the same: Colombia is broken, the establishment is the problem, the Tiger is the force that will fix it. Not three messages. One.

#### An AI Chatbot as Campaign Infrastructure

This one is easy to miss because it looks like a novelty, but it was operationally significant. The Defensores de la Patria app and website featured a conversational AI assistant named Ana, positioned as "the virtual assistant of Dr Abelardo de la Espriella."

Ana was designed to answer voter questions about campaign proposals, De la Espriella's background, and how to vote on 21 June. The interface explicitly noted that conversations with Ana helped the campaign gather intelligence: "Your questions help the campaign."

This is a genuinely clever piece of infrastructure. A campaign AI chatbot does three things simultaneously: it provides voter service at scale without requiring human staff, it filters and qualifies interested voters for follow-up contact, and it generates data about what questions voters are actually asking. That last function is often more valuable than the first two. Knowing what voters are confused about, what concerns them most, what they need explained, is exactly the audience intelligence that traditionally required focus groups, surveys, and significant budget.

The chatbot was powered by a tool called DIGIT2AI, indicating the campaign was working with specialist political AI vendors. For a movement that claimed to be financed entirely through effort and creativity rather than donations or alliances, it was a sophisticated deployment.

#### Winning the Diaspora as a Digital-First Strategy

In the second round, De la Espriella's support was particularly dominant in the diaspora and in Colombia's interior departments, while Cepeda carried Bogotá and the outer departments. The diaspora result was not accidental.

Colombian communities abroad, particularly in the United States, Spain, and other parts of Latin America, are intensely online political communities. They consume Colombian political content through the same TikTok and YouTube channels as voters inside the country, but they lack access to physical campaign infrastructure: rallies, door knocks, local party networks. For a digitally dominant campaign like De la Espriella's, this was not a problem. It was an advantage. His content reached diaspora communities as easily as it reached a voter in Medellín, and those communities, often economically motivated, security-conscious, and already sceptical of the Petro government, were fertile ground.

The campaign did not build a separate diaspora strategy. Its digital-first approach made diaspora outreach free. Any campaign running primarily on organic social content should be asking the same question: where are the communities that are underserved by physical campaigning and overrepresented online?

#### What the Opponent Did Wrong (and Why It Matters)

No article about this campaign is complete without acknowledging the other side, because some of De la Espriella's advantage was not what he did but what Cepeda failed to do.

Cepeda consistently spoke to his own base. He chose a running mate who reinforced his existing support rather than expanded it. His campaign spoke the language of the left to an audience that was already left-wing, and assumed that was sufficient. He did not treat undecided voters as the primary target until the second round, by which point the gap had already opened.

This is the fundamental strategic error that digital campaigns are particularly merciless at exposing. When you campaign primarily on organic social content, the algorithm tells you quickly whether your message is resonating beyond your base. De la Espriella's content was designed from the start to travel into new audiences. Cepeda's was designed to satisfy the ones he already had.

The digital lesson is not just about tactics. It is about audience definition. The question is not "how do we reach our supporters?" It is "how do we reach the people who have not yet decided?" If those are not the same question, your digital strategy needs rethinking.

#### A Final Thought on the Tiger's Model

De la Espriella won the Colombian presidency with a movement that is still technically not a political party, from a standing start, against a well-funded incumbent-backed candidate, in the most participated election in the country's modern history. He did it by building identity before policy, community before candidacy, and by understanding that in a fragmented digital media landscape, simplicity and emotional resonance travel further than expertise and detail.

Whether you agree with his politics or not, and there are serious reasons for concern about some of what he represents, the campaign is a case study in how political outsiders can use digital tools to bypass the institutional advantages of established parties.

Europe has its own version of this dynamic unfolding in several countries. The tools are the same. The context, thankfully, is often different.
