Power

29.06.2026

In December 2023, Hungary passed Law LXXXVIII on the protection of national sovereignty, creating a Sovereignty Protection Office with broad investigative powers and no judicial review of its findings. In February 2026, Advocate General Juliane Kokott told the Court of Justice of the European Union that the law breaches a sweeping list of EU fundamental rights and internal market freedoms.

In the period in between, civil society in Hungary spent two years operating under a law that international jurists now politely call structurally incompatible with EU membership.

\-

This is not a uniquely Hungarian story. Russia adopted the original "foreign agents" law in 2012 and expanded it repeatedly. Georgia followed in 2024. Kyrgyzstan in the same year. Slovakia drafted similar provisions. Bosnia's constitutional court struck down its version in May 2025. Hungary's expanded "Transparency in Public Life" bill, proposed in May 2025, would let the Sovereignty Protection Office classify EU funding itself as "foreign" for the purposes of restricting Hungarian NGOs. The Brussels Commission has opened infringement procedures. The Brussels Commission has also opened a great many infringement procedures.

For NGOs and democracy campaigners working in Europe and the European neighbourhood, these laws are now part of the operating environment. They are designed to look like transparency regulations and to function as stigma machines. The label "foreign agent" is the point of the law, not its mechanism. The vague drafting is the feature, not the bug. The lack of judicial review is intentional.

What follows is not a legal guide. It is a campaign-craft guide for the practitioners doing the work, in jurisdictions where the work is becoming more dangerous. Take these as field notes from a wider conversation that civil society has been having for over a decade, from Moscow in 2012 to Budapest in 2026.

A note before we start: nothing here substitutes for proper legal advice in your jurisdiction. Some of these decisions need lawyers, and not the cheap ones.

#### Consideration 1: Assume the Law Is Coming Even Where It Has Not Arrived Yet

The campaign reality: foreign-influence laws travel through elite networks, not through legal evolution. The legislative text drafted in Moscow in 2012 has surfaced, with local edits, in Budapest, Tbilisi, Bishkek, Bratislava and elsewhere. The Kyrgyz 2024 law on "foreign representatives" was passed shortly after Russian officials publicly warned of "preventive measures" against Western-supported NGOs in Central Asia. These laws are not the spontaneous response of national legislatures to genuine concerns about sovereignty. They are an imported political technology.

The lesson: if your country is a candidate for the next adoption, the question is not whether the law will come, but how prepared you will be when it does. Most NGOs that struggled to adapt did so because the law arrived faster than their organisational change could move. The ones that adapted best had started preparing before the bill was published.

What this looks like in practice:

Track legislative developments across the region, not just at home. The signal is usually visible six to twelve months before adoption. Watch for sovereignty-themed parliamentary commissions, official statements about "foreign-funded networks", and bilateral elite contacts with countries that already have these laws. None of this is hidden. Most of it is announced.

Preposition your compliance and legal infrastructure now. Identify your potential lawyer, your potential donor-of-last-resort, your potential safe-haven hosting partner, your potential international communications channel. Do not wait for the listing notice. The window between bill publication and active enforcement is the only one you control.

Scenario-plan the typical escalation pattern. The standard sequence is: vague law, designated authority, public registry, financial restrictions, criminal liability, dissolution proceedings. Some countries skip stages. Some loop back. Plan for each stage as a separate operational state, not as a continuous slide.

#### Consideration 2: The Label Is the Weapon. Plan a Narrative Defence, Not Only a Legal One

The campaign reality: on the night before the Russian foreign agents law came into force in 2012, unknown individuals spray-painted "Foreign Agent! ♥ USA" on the buildings of three Moscow NGOs, including Memorial. The administrative procedures came later. The stigma came first. A decade later, Memorial was dissolved.

The function of these laws is not financial regulation. It is to attach a stigmatising label to organisations the government wants delegitimised, and to do so with the formal authority of the state. The label travels through state media, into public opinion, and back into the funding base of the targeted NGO, where domestic donors quietly disappear because nobody wants to be associated with a "foreign agent". The legal procedure is the delivery mechanism. The damage is reputational.

The lesson: defending against these laws is at least half a communications exercise. NGOs that prepare a legal response without a narrative response lose the public ground before the courts ever rule.

What this looks like in practice:

Refuse the law's framing in public. Do not call yourself a "foreign-funded organisation" in your own materials, even ironically. Call yourself what you are: a Hungarian women's shelter, a Slovak anti-corruption monitor, a Georgian environmental watchdog. The community you serve is the centre of the narrative. The funding is logistics.

Name the law for what it is, calmly and repeatedly. "Russian-style", "imported", "modelled on Moscow's 2012 law" are not insults. They are accurate descriptions. The Venice Commission, the OSCE, the UN Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Association and (as of February 2026) the Advocate General of the CJEU have all said the same thing. Quote them. Make the comparison the journalists' default frame.

Cultivate domestic media allies before you need them. The international press will cover your listing for forty-eight hours. The domestic press, the journalists in your own language with their own audiences, will determine whether your community thinks the listing is a scandal or a smear. That relationship is built over years of returned calls, not in the week after the gazette publishes.

Ground every public message in local work. The most effective counter to the foreign-agent label is the testimony of the local person whose problem your NGO solved. The single mother whose abuser was prosecuted because of your legal aid clinic. The journalist whose source was protected by your digital security training. The labelling regime relies on abstraction. Specificity is the antidote.

#### Consideration 3: Diversify Funding Before You Are Forced To

The campaign reality: in early 2025, the US administration dismantled USAID. Democracy and rule-of-law programmes that had been the backbone of civil society funding across Eastern Europe, the Balkans, the Caucasus and Central Asia evaporated inside ninety days. Hungarian and Slovak NGOs that had been preparing for a foreign-agent law suddenly faced a separate, parallel funding collapse driven by a country with no foreign-agent law at all. The lesson on funding fragility no longer needs a hypothetical example.

Russian environmental NGOs studied by Maria Tysiachniouk and colleagues survived the foreign agents law in proportion to how much of their funding came from inside Russia before the law was passed. The ones with diversified domestic bases adapted. The ones with single-source international funding closed.

The lesson: funding concentration is operational risk. The lower the concentration, the more options you have when one source disappears, whether by foreign-agent law, by donor government collapse, or by the routine fluctuations of philanthropic strategy.

What this looks like in practice:

Map your funding by source, currency and percentage of total budget. Any single source that exceeds 40% of your operating costs is a strategic vulnerability. Plan migration over twelve to twenty-four months, not as an emergency.

Build domestic individual giving capacity. This is unglamorous, slow, and the most resilient revenue stream civil society has. A thousand small donors at 50 euros a year is more politically robust than one donor at 50,000 euros. The diversification is also a narrative asset, since it is harder to label as "foreign-funded" an organisation visibly funded by its own community.

Develop fee-for-service revenue where it fits your mission. Training, consulting, research commissions, certification work. Not all NGO work can be charged for. Some of it can, and the revenue is yours to deploy with less political vulnerability.

Cultivate non-state European foundations and the EU Cross-Border Associations framework where it survives. The EU's response to the funding gap has been slower than the gap opened. Track which mechanisms reach deployment. Do not assume any single Brussels initiative will save your budget.

#### Consideration 4: Compliance Documentation Is Operational Infrastructure

The campaign reality: foreign-interference laws are drafted to be vague. The Hungarian Sovereignty Protection Office can request information from any organisation it suspects of "influencing public life with foreign support", a phrase whose definition has no statutory anchor. The Russian law's definition of "political activity" was expanded by ministerial guidance to include almost any public-facing work an NGO might do. Vagueness is the design choice. It allows arbitrary application against selected targets while preserving the form of legality.

The lesson: the defence against arbitrary application is documentation that takes the arbitrariness out of your reach. If you can show, line by line, what every euro of your funding paid for and which of your activities was financed by which source, you have removed several of the levers that the enforcing authority would otherwise use. This will not stop a determined regime from listing you. It will significantly raise the cost of doing so.

What this looks like in practice:

Establish a lawyer relationship in normal time, not in crisis time. Pro bono networks exist, but they have queues. The first call to your lawyer should not be made the week your name appears on a list. Brief your lawyer on your work, your funding structure, your jurisdictional setup, before there is anything urgent to brief them on.

Document funding allocations at activity level. Not at programme level, at activity level. Which grant paid for which event, which staff time, which publication. The compliance regimes targeting NGOs are designed to find the seams between programmes and exploit them. Pre-emptively flat documentation closes the seams.

Map jurisdictional options for sensitive functions. Your archive does not necessarily need to live in the same country as your office. Your most exposed staff do not necessarily need to be on the same national payroll. Treat geographic distribution as a security feature, not a corporate inconvenience.

Build the strategic litigation relationship now. The European Court of Human Rights, the CJEU and constitutional courts in jurisdictions that still have them are slow but real. Hungary dropped its 2017 anti-NGO law after a 2020 ECJ ruling. Bosnia's constitutional court struck down its foreign-agent law in May 2025. The cases that win take years to build. Start the file now.

#### Consideration 5: International Solidarity Has to Be Operational, Not Ceremonial

The campaign reality: there will be statements. There will be many statements. MEPs will issue concerned press releases. Foreign ministries will summon ambassadors for stern conversations. International organisations will draft resolutions that mention "deep concern" and "the rule of law". None of this stops legislation in the country that passed it.

What sometimes does work, on long timescales: targeted sanctions on the individuals architecting the laws (the European Parliament has begun discussing this). Emergency funding mechanisms for organisations newly cut off from regular sources. Evidence preservation networks that feed material into ECtHR and CJEU cases. Safe-haven arrangements for activists and journalists forced to leave. Sustained diplomatic pressure tied to concrete EU mechanisms, including infringement procedures, Article 7 processes, and conditionality on EU funds.

The lesson: international solidarity is useful in direct proportion to its operationality. Treat the ceremonial layer as the wrapper, not the contents.

What this looks like in practice:

Build international relationships before you need them. Brussels-based advocacy NGOs, embassies of friendly states, MEPs in your political family, regional civil society networks. The week your law passes is the wrong week to be making introductions.

Contribute systematically to international documentation efforts. ICNL, CIVICUS, Human Rights Watch, ARTICLE 19, Amnesty International and similar groups maintain trackers and submit reports to international bodies. Your information is part of that record. Submit it cleanly, in a format they can use, with dates and sources.

Brief MEPs and diplomats with specific asks, not with general updates. "Please raise the case of organisation X in your meeting with minister Y on date Z" is actionable. "Please support civil society in our country" is not. The diplomat's calendar is the constraint. Your job is to fit your ask inside it.

Do not outsource the narrative to international allies. The international voice supports the domestic story. It does not replace it. The communications that matter in your country are the ones in your country's language, from people in your country, addressing your country's voters. The Brussels statement is supplementary.

#### So, What Should Democracy NGOs Do This Month?

If you take only five things from a decade of European civil society's adaptation to these laws:

**Tip #1:** Run a foreign-agent-law stress test on your organisation. Pretend the law passes next quarter. Who is your lawyer? Where does your funding come from? Who speaks for you in the domestic press? What happens to your archive? Identify the gaps. Then close them, one a month.

**Tip #2:** Map your funding by source and percentage. Any single foreign source above 40% of your budget is structural exposure. Build migration paths. The diversification you achieve in calm time is the resilience you have in crisis time.

**Tip #3:** Establish your lawyer relationship in the next thirty days. Pro bono is fine. Free is not the same as fast. Make sure they know your work before they need to defend it.

**Tip #4:** Document your community impact in your local language with local voices. Build a library of named, specific, on-the-record testimonies. This is your narrative arsenal against the abstraction of the foreign-agent label.

**Tip #5:** Connect with peer NGOs in jurisdictions already living under these laws. Memorial-in-exile, Hungarian Helsinki Committee, Georgian civil society networks, Russian NGOs in Vilnius and Tbilisi. The most useful resilience guides have been written by people who survived the first decade of this. Use them.

#### A Game of Preservation and Perseverance

The honest summary of the foreign-interference-law era is that it is spreading because it works, and it works because the response has been organised around the speed at which European institutions normally move, which is not the speed at which authoritarian capture moves. The Advocate General opinion against Hungary's law arrived two and a half years after the law took effect. Memorial was dissolved in December 2021, nine years after the original Russian foreign agents law passed. The legal mechanisms exist. They are slow. The political mechanisms exist. They are slower.

The campaigners I have met in Budapest, Stockholm, Bratislava and elsewhere are not waiting for Brussels to save them. They are doing the work of self-preservation, narrative defence, funding diversification and quiet preparation, often without proper resources and without much public acknowledgement of how skilled the work is. They are also, with notable consistency, more clear-eyed about the threat than many of the international institutions formally mandated to respond to it.

For NGOs in jurisdictions where these laws have not yet arrived, the deeper question is whether you treat this as a problem for those countries over there, or as the operating reality that may be coming to your own jurisdiction next. The Slovak draft does not consider itself out of the question. The Hungarian expansion bill, if upheld, treats EU funding itself as foreign. The Russian model has been politically successful enough that the Brussels infrastructure has not yet caught up with how to defend the European model against it.

The work, in other words, is no longer about preventing the spread. It is about operating credibly inside it while the institutional response catches up. The democracy campaigners doing that work deserve more than statements. They deserve infrastructure, money, lawyers, lawyers' lawyers, and the assumption that they are the experts on what their own survival requires.

Until the institutional response catches up, the campaign continues. As campaigns do.
