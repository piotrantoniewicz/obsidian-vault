My favourite Lego set was a pirate ship. I built it on the floor of my room, or in the lounge when I could get away with it, and what I loved about it wasn’t the size or the sails. It was everything hidden inside. Trapdoors. A monkey. Cannons that actually fired. You only found half of it by poking around.

When it was finished, it went on display in my room, and it stayed a pirate ship. I wasn’t the kid who tipped the box out and turned it into a spaceship. I liked the kit. I liked what it gave me, and I liked finding the secret bits.

I’ve been thinking about that ship a lot this week, because I’ve just launched something I’m calling my playground: [playground.kylebehrend.com](https://playground.kylebehrend.com/). It’s a place where I spin up small experiments I build with AI. Some of them are useful. Some are just fun. None of them had to be perfect before they went up.

One of my favourites is a little game about a creek. It’s polluted, and you decide how to help. Put in nets, clean things up, make choices. What I like about it is that the moment you act, the picture changes. You can see the creek respond in real time. The AI generated the images, wrote the code and wires it all together. It’s the same feeling as clicking a brick into place. You do something, and there it is.

## From bricks to blocks

The line from that pirate ship to my playground runs through a lot of years at Edgar’s Mission, the animal sanctuary where I worked for thirteen years.

We had a complicated animal database that started in Salesforce and later moved to Airtable, with Slack as the thing trying to hold everything together. Somewhere in the middle of that, I found Make. I wasn’t trained in computer science but I was very comfortable with computers and Make was no-code and it was visual. You could see every piece and figure out how they plugged into each other, one by one. I thought it was the most amazing thing.

One automation became many. My favourite was simple. When someone made a donation, it checked the amount, and if it was over a certain threshold, it told the team so someone could pick up the phone and personally thank that donor. We didn’t know that was possible. And in a nonprofit, where there’s never enough time, taking that small job off people’s plates mattered. It freed staff to do the work that needed a human.

Building with AI now is different again. Make let me see every brick. With AI, a lot of the bricks are hidden. I’m not placing each piece anymore. I’m describing the outcome I want and shaping it as it comes together. Less brick by brick, more “this is what I’m trying to make.”

But it still feels like building. And I’ve realised that’s the part that matters to me. Not whether I followed the instructions or went freestyle as a kid. The act of building.

## Borrowed ideas, your own twist

People sometimes ask where my ideas come from. The honest answer is other people.

A friend of mine, Matt, vibe-coded an app with an eighties theme. I loved it, so I sat down with AI and asked what else from the eighties could be turned into something digital. We ended up with a typewriter tool, and another that sends secret codes through the sound of a dial-up modem.

That’s how most of the playground works. I see what someone else is doing, then ask how it could apply to my work, my projects, the organisations I care about. It isn’t about having original ideas out of nowhere. It’s curiosity, plus a willingness to try things without needing them to be perfect, plus your own expertise. Everyone is sharing what they build right now. That’s a huge pile of inspiration sitting there, waiting for someone with your particular knowledge to put a twist on it.

So my first suggestion is simple. Make yourself a playground. A place where small experiments can live. Borrow ideas freely and bring what you know to them. You’ll be surprised how quickly the imagination becomes the only real limit.

## An invitation, not a test

My second suggestion is for organisations. Ask your staff who played with Lego as a kid.

I don’t mean it as a hiring filter, or a way to sort the builders from everyone else. I mean it as a reminder. Most people built something as a kid. Lego, cubbies, cakes, a fort out of couch cushions. Asking the question reminds people that they already know how to build. They’ve just been told for a long time that building software is someone else’s job.

It isn’t anymore. I recently helped my wife rebuild her website with AI. I set up the accounts (GitHub, Vercel, Resend, Supabase), connected them to ChatGPT, and showed her how to use the desktop app. We got the AI to pull the content out of her old site. I gave her a few tips along the way, like taking screenshots to show exactly which element or wording she wanted changed. Then I stepped back. She picked it up quickly, and now she manages the site herself.

I’ve started having the same conversation with organisations. Instead of juggling five platforms that almost do what you need, what if we set up something built around how you actually work, and then you maintain it yourselves with AI? You get more flexibility and more control, and you’re not paying expensive consultants every time something needs to change. There’s a real opportunity now to help organisations get set up, then let them run with it.

## “We don’t have time to play”

I can hear the objection, because I’ve heard it many times. We don’t have time for this. We’ve got a funding gap. We’ve got a waitlist. Play is a luxury.

I think that’s the biggest mistake organisations make.

Making space for experimentation is how you find opportunities you didn’t know existed. I spoke with a nonprofit recently that was so fed up with their CRM that they’re building their own, designed exactly for how they work. My first thought was that other organisations probably need the same thing, and they could turn it into a product. I’m seeing organisations build games, lead magnets and interactive learning experiences that would have been out of reach a year ago because of cost and time.

It’s also where [Because of You](http://because-of-you.com/) came from, the donor retention platform I’ve been building to create personalised digital thank you experiences after someone gives. It’s the same idea as that Make automation from years ago, the one that flagged a donor so someone could ring and say thank you. Just with bigger bricks.

After thirteen years inside a nonprofit, I know how easy it is to become busy being busy. Most people don’t study to start a nonprofit. They start from passion, and they work incredibly hard, underpaid and under-resourced. I have a lot of respect for that. But so much of the sector lives in the same place. We know what we should do, but it’s too hard to do it. Every donor should feel thanked. Every volunteer should get a proper welcome. Every program should learn from its own data. We know. We just can’t get to it.

If you can make ninety percent of that easier, it stops being too hard. That’s the leverage point. Not a specific tool, and not a big transformation program. Just enough protected space for people to play, so they find the ways to make the hard things easy.

And I’d go further. If you’ve been handed tools that let you rethink how you do what you do, isn’t it the organisation’s duty to pick them up? The mission deserves everything in your power. Doing the same thing again and again and expecting different results isn’t loyalty to the mission. It’s just habit.

## You can’t skip to the end

What I keep telling every organisation I work with is that this is a journey. You can’t jump straight to the endpoint, because with these technologies you don’t know where the endpoint is. Some organisations will have a clearer idea than others. But it’s only once you start using the tools, see what’s possible, see what others are building and bring that back to your own work, that the path shows up. You take the first steps, and then you make your own path.

The pirate ship’s best features were hidden. You only found the trapdoor if you went looking. The same is true of what AI could do for your organisation. It’s there, but nobody is going to hand you the list. You find it by playing.

So this isn’t a time problem. It’s a prioritisation problem. You’re not going to know what’s possible until you walk down that path, and you need to make time to do that.

Start with a playground. And maybe start by asking who played with Lego.
