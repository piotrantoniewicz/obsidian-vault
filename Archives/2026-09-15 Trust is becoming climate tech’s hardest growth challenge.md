A perennial challenge for emerging climate technologies is making the transition from pilot project to commercial scale. While founders and their teams rightly focus on proving performance and impact, they’re often less prepared for the next challenge: earning trust.

As climate technologies move closer to deployment, scrutiny across audiences increases. Investors want confidence that impact and commercial claims will stand up; policymakers need evidence they can defend publicly; customers look for reliability and commercial viability; and local communities want reassurance that new infrastructure or technology will deliver benefits for them.

From carbon removal to AI-enabled climate solutions, success depends not just on technical performance, but on communication that builds trust.

#### Why trust is critical to scaling climate technology

Climate innovators operate in a demanding environment. Carbon removal companies face growing questions around permanence, measurement and environmental integrity. Nature tech businesses are increasingly challenged to prove that promised outcomes are measurable, durable and beneficial to local communities. Across almost every category of climate innovation, stakeholders are becoming more sophisticated and more sceptical.

That’s a good thing; climate, clean and nature tech needs scrutiny. But it also means that strong technical performance is no longer enough on its own. Organisations need to show that they understand the real-world context in which their technology will operate, communicate evidence clearly and build confidence with those who influence adoption.

We see this challenge regularly in our work with climate, clean and nature tech businesses. As companies move from pilot projects towards wider deployment, the communications challenge often changes from explaining how technology works to demonstrating why people should trust it.

#### What makes trust difficult to build?

The very complexity of climate innovation is part of the challenge. Founders and technical teams are often immersed in developing the solution, while their audiences have different knowledge and priorities.

Building trust requires a clear, consistent narrative that reflects what different audiences need to know. We’ve worked with organisations whose investor materials, website copy and media messaging all emphasised slightly different benefits. Individually, the messages were strong. Collectively, they created confusion about what the organisation ultimately stood for.

Emerging sectors face specific questions too. Alternative protein companies must show that their products can achieve consumer acceptance and scale affordably, while energy-storage businesses face scrutiny over safety, supply chains and end-of-life impacts. Addressing these issues openly builds credibility.

#### The communications mistakes that undermine credibility

Many climate and clean tech businesses understandably feel pressure to demonstrate rapid progress but overstating potential, making claims that cannot yet be substantiated or positioning an early-stage solution as a silver bullet can quickly and counter-intuitively undermine the confidence needed to scale. Trust is rarely damaged by acknowledging uncertainty, but it can be damaged when organisations aren’t upfront about it.

Another common mistake is assuming that evidence speaks for itself. Performance data matters, but audiences also need to understand what was measured, how the results compare with alternatives and what remains uncertain. Good communications does not remove these qualifications. It makes them accessible.

Engaging stakeholders too late can be equally damaging. If communities, policymakers or partners are approached only once decisions have been made, consultation can feel performative. Engagement is most valuable when it happens early enough for questions and experience to inform both the proposition and how it is communicated.

#### How trust is built over time

You don’t need all the answers to build trust. It’s about giving your key audiences confidence that claims are grounded in evidence from experts and that you are being transparent about what you know, what you’re learning and what is still uncertain.

We work with leadership and technical teams to identify where evidence is strongest and where assumptions are creeping into the story. The most credible narratives clearly distinguish between what has been proven, what is an ambition and what depends on future deployment, investment or policy change.

The evidence itself also needs to evolve as a business grows. At pilot stage, technical performance and learning may be the strongest proof points. As deployment scales, audiences increasingly look for customer outcomes, operational experience, commercial partnerships and independent validation.

#### Turning technical expertise into commercial momentum

At Forster, [we help climate innovators](https://forster.co.uk/climate-tech-pr/?) translate complex science and technical expertise into communications that support growth. That can mean helping leadership teams articulate a clearer story for investors, aligning technical and corporate messaging, developing evidence-led thought leadership, preparing organisations for increased scrutiny or creating communications strategies that build confidence before critical moments.

Lack of innovation is rarely a challenge we come across. More often, it’s helping the right people understand why that innovation matters, what evidence supports it and why it deserves their trust. The climate technologies that achieve widespread adoption won’t necessarily be those with the most impressive technical specifications. They’ll be the ones that can demonstrate evidence, answer scrutiny and build confidence among the people who ultimately decide whether new solutions succeed or fail.

Scaling climate innovation is not just a technical challenge. It’s a trust challenge too.
