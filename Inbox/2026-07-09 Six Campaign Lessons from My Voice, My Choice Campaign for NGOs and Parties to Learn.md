Power

06.07.2026

On 17 December 2025, the European Parliament adopted a resolution endorsing the European Citizens' Initiative "My Voice, My Choice: For Safe and Accessible Abortion" by 358 votes to 202, with 79 abstentions.

On 6 March 2026, the European Commission published its response, opening the possibility for member states to use European Social Fund Plus money to improve access to safe and legal abortion services. The Commissioner for Equality, Hadja Lahbib, called it groundbreaking. The ALDE Party called it historic. Even the campaign organisers, normally allergic to triumphalism, allowed themselves a moment.

\-

The numbers behind that moment: 1.12 million validated signatures collected across the EU, more than 300 organisations in coalition, the twelfth successful ECI since the Lisbon Treaty introduced the mechanism. The legal architecture behind that moment was rather more clever than the campaign's gentle public branding might suggest. Abortion is a national competence under Article 168 TFEU. The EU has no direct authority to legislate it. So the campaign did not ask for legislation. It asked for a voluntary opt-in funding mechanism through an instrument the EU does control. The political route around the legal wall was the entire game.

This piece is not a victory tour. The funding mechanism is voluntary, which means the member states most likely to use it are the ones whose citizens least need it, and the member states whose citizens most need it will not opt in. A Polish woman still cannot access abortion in Poland. The Commission response opens a door. It does not walk through it. The follow-through is the next campaign.

What the campaign already achieved, though, has lessons in it. Below are six, structured for two different audiences: three for campaigners working inside political parties, three for public relations officers in NGOs and corporate communications teams. The two audiences need different things from the same campaign.

#### Three Lessons for Campaigners in Political Parties

##### Lesson 1: When the Front Door Is Locked, Find the Back-Office Door

The campaign behind: the front-door demand on abortion access in the EU was always institutionally impossible. Health policy is national. Treaty change is hypothetical. A direct legislative ask would have collapsed on first contact with subsidiarity arguments. So the campaign did not make that ask. It identified the European Social Fund Plus, an instrument the EU does control, and asked that the regulation governing ESF+ be interpreted to permit funding for cross-border abortion care where a member state opts in. Same destination, different door.

The political lesson: institutional architecture has many doors. The main one being locked is information about that door, not a verdict on the building. Most policy areas have several adjacent EU competences (funding, standards, data, complementary action, internal market) that touch the same issue from different legal angles. Campaigns that map those adjacencies find routes the conventional advocacy literature misses.

In practice, before declaring an ask politically impossible, walk through every funding instrument, every existing directive, every soft-law mechanism in the neighbourhood of your issue. Some of them will surprise you. The opposition will be politically intense and legally weaker than on the main door, because the legal basis is solid.

##### Lesson 2: Cross-Party Coalitions Are the Campaign, Not a Nice-To-Have

The campaign behind: 358 votes in favour required Renew, S&D, Greens, the Left, plus a chunk of the EPP willing to break ranks on a contested social issue. That coalition did not assemble itself. It was built over months of careful negotiation, drafting compromises that gave the centre-right something it could vote for (the voluntary opt-in was that something), and refusing to let the campaign be captured by its most strident allies.

The political lesson: a contested social issue does not pass on your political family alone, even when your family is large. The campaigns that move in this Parliament are the ones that leave exit ramps for moderates of other groups, design the ask so that "yes" has plausible deniability for the centre-right voter at home, and accept that the price of breadth is precision.

In practice, write your ask twice. Once for the people who agree with you, once for the people you need to bring along. If the second version requires unacceptable compromise, the campaign is not yet ready for plenary. Test the second version on actual EPP and ECR staffers. Their lukewarm "I could probably get my MEP to abstain" is the indicator you are looking for.

##### Lesson 3: One Ask, One Name, One Rhyme

The campaign behind: "My Voice, My Choice." Three monosyllables (or near enough), an internal rhyme, a meaning that opponents struggled to caricature without sounding patronising. It fit on a placard, on a hashtag, in a headline. It dignified the rights-holder without lecturing the listener.

Compare with the formal title: "European Citizens' Initiative for the establishment of a voluntary financial solidarity mechanism, accessible to all EU member states, to ensure the provision of safe termination of pregnancy services." Also accurate. Also unrepeatable.

The political lesson: the campaign brand is the campaign asset. More time should be spent getting it right than most political organisers allow. A bad name is a tax you pay on every press hit for the duration of the file.

In practice, test campaign names on people who do not work in advocacy. Ask them to repeat the name 24 hours later. The ones they remember without prompting are the ones that survive into the news cycle. The ones they fumble are the ones quietly eating your budget.

#### Three Lessons for PR Officers in Corporate or NGO Roles

##### Lesson 4: Refuse the Opponents' Framing, Every Single Time

The campaign behind: opponents (the European Centre for Law and Justice, One of Us, conservative media) consistently tried to drag the debate back to first principles. Is abortion moral? What about the unborn? The campaign refused to be drawn. It kept the public conversation on access for women in difficulty, on cross-border solidarity, on practical care. The result is that even hostile coverage tended to litigate the campaign's chosen ground, not the campaign's deepest disagreements with its opponents.

The PR lesson: the side that chooses the question wins the answer. Communications discipline is not about saying nothing. It is about answering the question your campaign is built for, every time, even when the journalist's question is a different one.

In practice, write down the question your campaign is the answer to. Stick it on the wall. Brief every spokesperson on the bridging technique back to that question. When a hostile interviewer tries to pull you onto adjacent terrain, route back without rudeness. "That's a question some people are asking. The question I'm working on is whether women in Europe can access safe care."

##### Lesson 5: Coordinate 300 Organisations Without Diluting the Message

The campaign behind: a coalition of more than 300 organisations across 27 member states is a logistical achievement and a communications nightmare in equal measure. Every partner wants a small tweak for their national context. Every press officer wants to add the local angle. The default outcome is message mush. My Voice, My Choice avoided this by holding a tight central message architecture, with controlled national variations, distributed through a one-page narrative brief that every coalition partner used.

The PR lesson: coalition discipline is built by the people at the centre, not enforced by them. The partners need to want to use the central messaging because the central messaging makes their work easier, not harder. That is a brief-writing problem before it is a politics problem.

In practice, produce a living one-page message brief: the core line, three secondary lines, the lines you do not say, named spokespeople, and the bridging answers to the predictable hostile questions. Update it monthly. Treat any major deviation in coalition messaging as a coordination problem to fix, not a betrayal to punish.

##### Lesson 6: Ground Every Public Message in a Specific, Named, On-the-Record Voice

The campaign behind: the temptation in any rights campaign is to speak in categories. "Women", "patients", "those affected". The campaign resisted this. It featured named individuals, named cases, named circumstances. The 19-year-old Polish woman who travelled to Berlin. The Maltese health worker who could not refer a patient. The Hungarian gynaecologist navigating intimidation. Specificity, not abstraction, did the persuasive work.

The PR lesson: categories are forgettable. People are not. Every public-facing piece, from press release to social asset to written submission, should have at least one named human voice in it, on the record, with a story that the audience can hold in their head.

In practice, build a spokespeople roster from your community of impact. Brief them properly. Protect them properly (this matters in contexts where opponents harass campaign-affiliated individuals). Use them properly, which means coordinating their appearances rather than overusing the same two faces until the press loses interest.

#### Coalitions Conquer More Than Ego

The Commission's March 2026 response is a real win. It is also a partial one. The funding mechanism is voluntary, which means the member states most likely to opt in are the ones whose citizens have the fewest problems with access, and the member states whose citizens face the steepest barriers will exercise their right to do nothing. The structural inequality in access remains. The campaign won the argument that there is an EU role. It has not yet won the file that delivers consistent care.

What My Voice, My Choice did well, though, is the kind of thing that does not depend on the next legislative outcome. It built a 300-organisation coalition that held its message under pressure. It found a legal route around an impossible political wall. It made a complex demand repeatable in two words and a rhyme. It cultivated the cross-party majority that political reality required, without softening into nothing.

For political parties, the practical takeaway is that institutional creativity matters as much as ideological clarity, and most campaigns underinvest in the first. For PR officers, the practical takeaway is that discipline at the centre and specificity at the surface are what coalition campaigns are made of.

The legislative follow-through is the next campaign. The campaigners are already on it.
