### TEAMS! Newsletter | Mission Edition | September 17, 2026

### You don’t really know what kind of team you have until something goes wrong.

Teams worthy of the people we serve aren’t built during the easy stretches. They’re built when the plan falls apart. When someone leaves unexpectedly. When the deadline shifts. When funding changes. When a family needs an answer we don’t yet have. When everyone is already carrying too much, yet the work still has to get done. That’s when you find out what you’ve actually built.

> ***Every team I’ve led has eventually faced some version of the same test: What holds people together when the work gets hard?***

It isn’t the org chart. It isn’t the strategic plan. And it certainly isn’t the benefits package. It’s trust.

It’s not the kind of trust we talk about at retreats. It’s the kind earned when people go through something difficult together, tell each other the truth, keep their word, and come out the other side still willing to rely on one another, stronger and more committed to the team and the work.

In social services, this matters enormously. The people and families we serve already face enough uncertainty. They deserve organizations and teams that can absorb pressure without passing that uncertainty on to them. That doesn’t happen by accident. It’s built.

### In Today’s Edition of TEAMS!

Today we discuss why who is on the team matters so much. Talent, mindset, and shared values. Themes that help build strong teams:

- **Candor protects the mission.** Saying what needs to be said, clearly and respectfully, is an act of leadership and trust.
- **Trust is earned under pressure.** Hard moments reveal whether people pull apart or pull together.
- **The smartest person in the room is rarely in the room.** Better answers emerge when we use the collective wisdom of the people closest to the work.

### Building the Foundation for Strong Teams

**Get the Right People on the Team.** A strong team isn’t built by simply filling open positions. It’s built by finding people who make those around them better. Skills matter. Experience matters. But neither tells you everything about how someone will show up when things get difficult. We look for talent, certainly, but also for judgment, curiosity, drive, generosity, and a connection to the work. A résumé may get someone through the door. What they bring to the team determines what happens next.

**Say what needs to be said.** Organizations change. Jobs change. Expectations change. Sometimes the person who was exactly right for a role three years ago is no longer aligned with the role’s current requirements. Sometimes a team moves in one direction while one of its members moves in another. Pretending otherwise doesn’t help anyone. Too often, leaders mistake vagueness for kindness. We soften the message, postpone the conversation, and hope the problem resolves itself. Usually, it doesn’t.

> ***Vague feedback feels kinder today but becomes crueler tomorrow.***

The better choice is clarity delivered with respect. Misalignment is not a judgment of someone’s worth. Naming it early gives people a chance to understand, adjust, and succeed, and it protects the rest of the team from carrying a problem no one is willing to discuss.

**Pressure Reveals the Team.** Nobody really bonds when things are easy and everything goes as planned. Think about the teams you’ve trusted most. Chances are, you went through something hard together. A crisis no one expected. A deadline that suddenly shifted. A staffing shortage. A difficult decision. A period when everyone had to stretch beyond what was comfortable.

Those moments can fracture a team, but they can also forge one. Shared adversity creates something no ropes course, retreat, or team-building exercise can manufacture: the knowledge that when things get hard, the people beside you will still be there. That’s trust you can count on when it’s needed most.

**Say It Plainly. Hear It Back.** Confusion is expensive. In our work, it can be even more costly. It can affect services, safety, families, and the people counting on us. So strong teams communicate more than feels necessary, especially when something is new.

> ***Explain the why. Give the context. Say what needs to happen.***

Then make sure everyone heard the same thing. For mission-critical work, a ten-second read-back can prevent a ten-hour correction. That isn’t bureaucracy. It’s how people learn to depend on each other.

**The Room Is Smarter Than Any One Person.** Leadership experience can create a dangerous illusion: because you’ve seen a lot, you assume you’ve seen it all. You haven’t, and neither have I.

The frontline employee sees what the Leadership Team overlooks. The program manager notices what finance misses. A family understands what systems and KPIs don’t capture. A participant experiences what may never appear on a dashboard. Put those perspectives together, and the picture changes.

> ***Leadership is about building a team that can find a better answer than any one person could alone.***

### Easy Next Steps You Can Take Today

- **Hire for the team you need next.** Don’t automatically replace yesterday’s role. Ask what tomorrow’s organization requires.
- **Address name misalignment early.** If you see it, address it. A respectful conversation this week is better than a painful one in six months.
- **Debrief the hard stretches.** Ask what worked, what strained, who stepped up, and what you’d do differently next time.
- **Over-communicate when something is new.** During the first 90 days of a role, an initiative, or a major change, assume people need more context, not less.
- **Read back what matters.** For anything mission-critical, confirm that everyone heard the same message.
- **Ask the people doing the work.** Before finalizing the plan, talk to the frontline team members. They usually know where the plan will break before the rest of us do.

### You Don’t Build as Strong of a Team When Things Are Easy

We talk a lot about high-performing teams. I’m increasingly less interested in that phrase. A spreadsheet can tell anyone whether a team hit its numbers. It can’t tell us whether people trust one another, whether someone speaks up when something feels wrong, whether colleagues step toward each other when the pressure rises, or whether the people we serve experience the organization as steady even when things behind the scenes are anything but.

That’s the team we should all want to build. Eventually, every organization gets tested. Funding changes. Someone leaves. The plan fails. The call comes that nobody wanted. In that moment, the strategic plan can’t help you. The people around you can. That’s why pressure doesn’t just test a team. Pressure builds the team.

> ***One team. One purpose. Built for when it matters most: the communities we serve.***
