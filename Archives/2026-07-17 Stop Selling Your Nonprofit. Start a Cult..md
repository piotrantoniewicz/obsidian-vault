My friend [Erica Moore](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ericamoore-programs/) is the most dedicated fan I know.

She has spent thousands of dollars following My Morning Jacket around the world. Thousands of miles. Countless weeks of vacation time, all to stand in fields and amphitheaters with the same rotating cast of fellow obsessives.

Was it the music? The music is great. But you can stream the music for free from your couch.

Erica wasn't buying songs. She was buying her people.

Taylor Swift fans know this too. When you buy a Taylor Swift ticket, you're not buying the two hours she's on stage. You're buying an extension of your identity. The bracelets, the outfits, the strangers who become friends in the bathroom line. The concert is the excuse. The belonging is the product.

Here's what I've had a hard time articulating until now: when we use sales tactics for fundraising, we cheapen the experience.

Fundraisers at their best are not selling a product. They are building a community. And those two things stand in direct opposition to each other.

### The product pitch (you'll recognize it)

You know the sales model because you've probably run it. You show up with a deck. Mission slide, programs slide, impact numbers slide, budget slide. You walk the donor through your features like a car salesman walking a customer around the lot. Then you present the price options: $1,000 gets a name in the gala program, $5,000 gets a table, $25,000 gets a plaque.

You made a catalog. The donor became a customer. The gift became a purchase.

And customers behave like customers. They comparison shop, and there are a lot of good causes out there with shinier decks. They negotiate. They churn. When you sell a transaction, you have to re-sell it every single year: December comes and you're back out on the lot, re-closing the same gift.

That's the treadmill. That's why your development director is exhausted. You're not building anything. You're re-closing the same sale, over and over, forever.

### The reframe: what donors are actually buying

Donors give for their reasons, and one of the deepest reasons humans do anything is belonging. The need to be part of something. To matter to a group. To have an identity bigger than "person who wrote a check."

Nobody follows a jam band across three continents for the setlist. And nobody stays loyal to a nonprofit for the pie charts.

This is the part that changes everything about how you fundraise: in a real community, the member is the hero. The tribe doesn't exist to serve the band. The tribe is what allows Erica to live her values out loud: devotion, adventure, generosity, showing up for her people. The band is just the reason to gather.

Your donor community works the same way. It's the vehicle through which your people get to be who they say they are. The parent who values education. The survivor who values second chances. The neighbor who refuses to look away. You are not the hero of this story. You're the band.

Put the two models side by side and the difference gets stark:

Every icky feeling you've ever had about fundraising lives over there. The scripts, the pressure, the performance. Sales tactics feel gross in fundraising because they make the donor a means to your end. Community flips it: the donor is the hero, and your organization is the means to theirs.

The right column is the shift from petitioner to partner. A petitioner sells. A partner belongs.

### The math on 1,000 true fans

In 2008, Kevin Kelly, co-founder of Wired, wrote an essay that became gospel for creators: 1,000 True Fans. His argument: you don't need millions of customers to make a living. You need a thousand true fans. A true fan is someone who will buy anything you make, drive 200 miles to see you perform, and pre-order before the reviews come out. If each one spends roughly a day's wages on you per year, a thousand of them is a living. No gatekeepers, no mass market required.

Erica is a true fan. My Morning Jacket doesn't need her to love every album. She's flying to the show either way, because the relationship stopped being about any single product a long time ago.

Now run the nonprofit version of that math, because it's even better than Kelly's. You don't need a thousand. At a $2M to $10M organization, 25 true fans at major gift level rewrite your budget. A hundred true fans and your individual giving program carries the mission. The math maths, and it maths on a shockingly small number of people.

The catch: your mailing list does not contain true fans. It contains names. True fans aren't found. They're made, through belonging.

Which brings us to cults.

### The anatomy of a (benevolent) cult

Real talk: cults are terrible for their members and spectacular at retention. Strip out the exploitation and what's left is the most durable belonging machinery humans have ever built. Every group that inspires Erica-level devotion runs on the same bones. Steal the machinery. Skip the Kool-Aid.

**An origin myth.** Every cult has a founding story, told and retold until members can recite it. Your organization has one too: the kitchen table, the first family you served, the night your founder decided somebody had to do something. Tell it at every gathering until your donors tell it for you.

**A shared identity with a name.** Swifties. Parrotheads. Deadheads. What are your people called? If the answer is "donors," you've named a transaction. Give your inner circle an actual name and watch what happens when people start using it about themselves.

**Insider language.** Every tribe has words outsiders don't know. Your organization already has them: the shorthand your staff uses, the phrase your founder always says, the inside joke from the 2019 gala flood. Stop scrubbing that out of donor communications. Insider language is a gift. It says: you're in.

**An enemy.** Not a competitor. The injustice you exist to fight. Communities rally against something: the disease, the achievement gap, the broken system. When your donors can name the enemy in one sentence, they're not funding programs anymore. They've joined a fight.

**A ladder of commitment.** No cult opens with the big ask. There's a small first yes, then a slightly bigger one, each step deepening the identity. Done without consent, that's manipulation. Done with consent, it's just how humans deepen anything: the survey before the call, the call before the meeting, the meeting before the ask.

**Status that money can't buy.** In healthy tribes, standing comes from involvement, not just dollars. Spectators leave. Participants stay. Give people a job: making thank-you calls, judging the pitch competition, hosting the living room gathering. Then recognize it. Your ten-year monthly donor at $25 a month who does all three should feel like more of an insider than the stranger who dropped $10K once and vanished.

### How to create your 1,000 true fans (start with 25)

1. **Find the fans you already have.** Look for behavior, not capacity. Who gives every year regardless of amount? Who opens everything, shows up to everything, brings friends? A wealth screen finds money. Behavior finds belief. Pull your 25 and start there.
2. **Name your people.** Draft three options for what your closest supporters are called. Test them with five donors. Pick one.
3. **Create one ritual.** A founder's story night where the origin myth gets told. A first-gift welcome call from a board member within 48 hours. A members-only impact briefing every quarter. One ritual, repeated, becomes tradition. Tradition becomes identity.
4. **Give ten donors a job.** Not a gift ask. A job. If the only thing you ever ask a donor to do is write a check, you've told them their money matters and they don't. Pick ten people in your pipeline and invite each one to do something: advise, host, call, judge, introduce.
5. **Run a language audit.** Pull your last three appeals. Count how many sentences are about what you need versus what we are building together. If it reads like an invoice, rewrite it like an invitation.
6. **Tell insiders first.** Big hire, new program, hard news: your inner circle hears it before the public does, in a personal email or a call. Nothing says "you're one of us" like being told first.

None of this requires new software or a bigger team. It requires deciding that your donors are members of something, and then acting like it in every interaction.

### The math on belonging

Sell a product and you get customers. Customers negotiate, comparison shop, and churn.

Build a community and you get true fans. True fans renew without being re-sold. True fans upgrade because their identity is on the line. True fans recruit other true fans, which is the only donor acquisition strategy that gets cheaper over time.

Kevin Kelly said a creator needs a thousand. You need fewer. Twenty-five believers who see your organization as the place they get to live their values will do more for your mission than 25,000 names on a list.

Erica gives a jam band her money, her miles, and her vacation days, year after year, and nobody has ever sent her an appeal letter. Your mission fights for actual lives. It deserves at least that much devotion.

Stop selling. Go start your cult.
