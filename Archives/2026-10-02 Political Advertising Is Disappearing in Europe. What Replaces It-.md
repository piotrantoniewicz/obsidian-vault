Europe's political communicators are relearning a skill most of them thought they had already retired, which is how to reach people without simply paying a platform to interrupt them.

\-

The result is a fundamental shift in European political campaigning: from rented reach to owned distribution. Email lists, WhatsApp groups, volunteer networks, local organisers, creators and direct voter relationships are becoming infrastructure rather than supporting channels.The reason is a piece of EU law most voters have never heard of. Regulation (EU) 2024/900, known in the trade as the TTPA, entered into force in April 2024 and came into full application on 10 October 2025.

It requires every political advertisement to carry a clear label and a transparency notice naming who paid for it and how much, and it puts strict limits on using personal data for targeting, which now needs separate, explicit consent from each person whose data gets used for that specific political purpose. It also bans running political ads on behalf of anyone outside the EU in the three months before an election.

The targeting rules go further than most people outside ad tech would guess. Political advertisers can no longer build audiences from browsing history, location patterns or inferred political leanings, which had been the standard toolkit of programmatic advertising for a decade. They can only target with data someone handed over knowingly and specifically for that purpose, separate from whatever cookie banner they already clicked through without reading. For an industry built on lookalike audiences and behavioural retargeting, that was not a minor tweak.

Meta and Google both looked at the compliance burden this created and made the same call. Rather than build the systems the regulation demanded, they simply stopped running political, electoral and social issue ads across the EU from around October 2025 onward.

For campaigns that had spent a decade building their digital operation around targeted reach on exactly those two platforms, the easiest lever in the whole toolkit disappeared almost overnight.

The tidy version of this story is that regulation forced European politics back toward organising, canvassing and genuine relationships with voters, the things communications people always say they value and rarely fund properly. The real version, so far, is messier.

Hungary's parliamentary election on 12 April 2026 became the first serious live test of what happens when the paid lever disappears under real electoral pressure. Monitoring by researchers at Liberties.eu, running from late October 2025 through the run up to the vote, found that paid political content did not disappear from Hungarian social media. It just moved somewhere harder to see. Ads went undeclared. Campaigns worked through proxy pages rather than their own accounts.

Money moved toward smaller influencers who were not obviously running sponsored political content at all, and platforms did little to catch any of it. The regulation was built to make political advertising more transparent. In its first real test, it briefly made some of it less transparent, because the campaigns responding to it were solving the wrong problem. They were trying to keep buying reach quietly instead of learning to earn it.

That distinction matters. Campaigns now have three ways to reach people: buy distribution, borrow distribution from someone else’s audience, or build distribution they control themselves. The first has become dramatically harder in Europe. The second is vulnerable to platforms, influencers and regulation. The third takes longest to build, but becomes more valuable every time the other two become less reliable.

Poland offers the other half of this story, and it predates the regulation entirely. In the October 2023 parliamentary election, Poland recorded a turnout of 74.4 per cent, its highest since the fall of communism, ahead even of the first free election in 1989. Analysts and civil society groups both point to the same driver, a wave of grassroots get out the vote campaigns run by organisations such as Akcja Demokracja, built around door to door conversations, local community meetings and a simple, repeated message about showing up. A Batory Foundation survey afterwards found that late mobilisation efforts had lifted turnout by roughly seven percentage points in the final fortnight, and that around two thirds of voters said get out the vote activity had genuinely influenced their decision to cast a ballot. None of that ran through a targeted ad account. It ran through people talking to other people, at scale, because the infrastructure to do that already existed before anyone needed it.

Put those two elections side by side and the lesson is not really about Hungary or Poland specifically, or about who won which vote. It is about which kind of campaign the new rules actually punish. A campaign that treated paid targeting as a shortcut for organising it never quite got around to building is now stuck either finding workarounds or falling behind. A campaign that already had volunteers, local coordinators and a habit of showing up in person was never depending on that shortcut in the first place, so the rules barely touch it.

The regulation was written with elections in mind, but its definition of political advertising is broad enough to catch a good deal of advocacy and lobbying work too. Campaign groups, NGOs and corporate public affairs teams running issue based digital campaigns are increasingly finding themselves inside the same rules as political parties, which is a real headache for anyone whose entire model was a well targeted ad buy timed around a policy moment.

For anyone advising a party, candidate or advocacy campaign in Europe, that changes what should be built first. The priority is no longer simply audience size. It is distribution infrastructure: first-party contact data you have permission to use, volunteers who will actually knock on doors, WhatsApp and email lists that do not disappear when an algorithm changes, local coordinators who know their own patch, and relationships with creators and community figures whose audiences cannot simply be purchased through an ad dashboard.

These used to sit underneath the “real” campaign of digital reach bought at scale. That hierarchy has flipped. Owned distribution and organising are becoming the campaign infrastructure; paid reach, where it remains available, is the supplement.

None of this makes citizen driven communication easy. It is slower to build than an ad account, it does not scale on demand the way a media budget does, and it punishes anyone who only starts thinking about it once an election has already been called. But it is durable in a way that workarounds are not. A proxy page or an undeclared influencer deal can be caught, banned or simply stop working overnight. A volunteer network that has already had thousands of real conversations with real voters does not evaporate because a platform changed its policy.

For European campaign teams, the strategic question is therefore no longer simply how to replace Meta or Google ads. It is how to build enough owned and earned distribution that losing access to a paid channel stops being an existential problem in the first place.

That is the question at the centre of the session on citizen-driven communication and the end of the old advertising model at the first Political Communication Summit. On 7 October at Vienna City Hall, campaign and communications professionals will examine what political distribution looks like when targeted advertising can no longer be treated as the default.
