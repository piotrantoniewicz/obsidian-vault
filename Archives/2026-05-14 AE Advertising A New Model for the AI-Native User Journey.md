Search ads were built for a world where people typed keywords. That world is changing. People are no longer just searching, they’re conversing. They’re thinking out loud, working through problems in dialogue, and treating AI systems as partners in decision-making. This behavioral shift changes everything about how digital advertising works, not because the ad unit changed, but because the user journey did.

Traditional digital advertising behaves like a tollbooth. Ads position themselves in the middle of the road and force a choice: click or keep moving. **AE Advertising** (Answer Engine advertising) is built on a different model. Instead of blocking the path, ads and advertisers attach themselves to the journey, riding alongside the user, present but not intrusive, looking ahead to the next question the user hasn’t fully formed yet.

This guide defines what an AEO (the Answer Engine Optimization unit) looks like at each stage of the modern AI-native user journey. For each stage: what the user is experiencing, what the AEO’s role is, which formats work, what the creative principles are, and what a real nonprofit example looks like in practice.

## SEM vs. AEM: the episode vs. the season

Search Engine Marketing (SEM) was a self-contained unit. A user typed a keyword, ads competed in an auction, one ad got the click, the transaction closed. Everything the advertiser needed to say had to fit into a headline and two lines of copy because the whole relationship was built to happen in a single moment. SEM is a standalone episode. You can drop a new viewer into it with no prior context and they can still follow what’s happening.

Answer Engine Marketing (AEM) doesn’t work like that. In an AI-native interface, a decision isn’t one keystroke, it’s a conversation that unfolds across days, tools, and internal debate. The user opens a chat to think out loud. They come back the next morning with a sharper question. They ask a follow-up that reveals a new constraint. They pressure-test the answer with a colleague and return with an objection. By the time they reach the commitment stage, they’ve lived inside the topic for a while.

The Office AE Marketing Image

AEM is a full season. Each conversation is an episode, but the real story, the character arc, plays out across the narrative at large. That’s why ads built for SEM will not maximize what is possible with an AI-user audience. In practice, this means three things:

- **Context compounds across turns.** What the user said in minute one shapes what a helpful AEO looks like in minute ten. The advertiser’s job is to match the state of mind, not just match the keyword.
- **Helpfulness is cumulative.** In a single-episode model, every ad starts at zero. In a season model, trust built in Stage 1 is the currency the advertiser spends in Stage 3. If you showed up early and were genuinely useful, you’ve earned the right to make a direct ask later.
- **The arc has a shape.** Beginning (the need forms), middle (the user makes sense of the landscape), end (the user commits). Season-aware advertisers design for all three beats, and know that *pushing an end-of-season ask in the pilot episode is how you lose the viewer*. #nospoilers

## What the numbers say

The scale of the shift in user behavior is hard to overstate:

- **ChatGPT reached 900 million weekly active users in February 2026,** more than double the 400 million reported a year earlier. It now processes roughly 2.5 billion prompts per day. *(OpenAI, via TechCrunch)*
- **Google’s AI Overviews appear in approximately 48% of all U.S. searches and reach an estimated 2 billion monthly users**, a 58% year-over-year increase in coverage. Coverage skews highest in consideration-heavy categories: 88% of healthcare queries, 83% of education queries. *(Heroic Rankings; DemandSage)*
- **49% of ChatGPT messages are categorized as “Asking”**, advice-seeking rather than task-doing. Practical advice accounts for 29% of personal use and 24% of professional use. *(NBER / OpenAI usage study)*
- **25% of consumers now use an AI platform as their primary research tool for purchase decisions.** 60% say chatbots frequently influence their buying decisions. *(Capital One Shopping; BCG)*

Users are spending massive amounts of time in AI chats before they ever reach a branded touchpoint, and a growing share of what they’re doing there is decision-oriented. The window where “search intent” was the primary signal is closing. **Decision state, where the user is in the arc, is the new targeting surface.**

## The modern user journey

The classic marketing funnel (awareness → consideration → intent) was designed for a world where advertising interrupted attention. The AI-native journey has a simpler shape and a bigger surface. It has three stages: beginning, middle, end that all live inside AI chat, where the user is still forming the question itself.

The three stages describe not just what users know or want, but *how they’re thinking*. That distinction matters for the AEO, because the AEO’s job is to match the user’s cognitive state, not just their search intent.

| Stage | Description | What the user is doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1, Pre-Decision** | Unnamed need | Something is needed but they can’t articulate it yet. They’re describing a problem, not searching for a solution. |
| **2, Sensemaking** | Making meaning, comparing paths | Learning the landscape. Trying to understand causes, solutions, and tradeoffs. Asking which path fits their values, capacity, and constraints. |
| **3, Commitment** | Take action, stay engaged | Ready to act. Moving from understanding to implementation, and processing the decision on the other side. |

Each stage requires a different version of the AEO. A commitment-level ad at Stage 1 will feel premature and off-putting. At Stage 3, sensemaking content feels patronizing. **Stage-match alignment is the core discipline.**

## Stage 1, Pre-Decision: reinventing the journey’s opening scene

Dana, a nonprofit CMO, is using AI chat to try and figure out why her organization’s new annual report isn’t landing. AI-assisted writing has not been named as a solution because she doesn’t know it’s a solution, she just knows what’s broken.

**AE ad Type: Guide**

*User prompt (Jordan M., CMO, Brightpath Youth Network):* “We just published our annual report and almost nobody is engaging with it. Donors are opening the email but not clicking through. Board members say it ‘feels dry.’ We put a lot of work into the data and outcomes, how do we get people to actually care about an annual report?”

*Sponsored unit, side panel:* **Whole Whale, Your annual report did the work. Now nobody’s reading it.** Most nonprofit annual reports are written to be thorough. The ones that drive donations are written to be *felt*. See what separates the two, with real before/after examples. **\[Read the guide\]**, Free guide, no signup required.

- **The guide itself is the bridge.** It explains the problem (donor voice vs. org voice), ends with a soft “try rewriting your intro” prompt that introduces the concept of AI-assisted writing without naming the product, and puts CauseWriter in a quiet “built with” attribution at the very bottom.
- **Mirrors the user’s exact pain.** “Nobody’s reading it” echoes Dana’s own language before she can articulate it more clearly.
- **No product mention.** CauseWriter doesn’t appear. This is a content guide, not an ad for an AI tool.
- **Zero-commitment CTA.** Free, no signup. The ask matches the readiness level, Dana just wants help, not a sales conversation.
- **Whole Whale earns attribution.** The brand is present but not intrusive. Trust is built before the product ever surfaces.

## Stage 2, Sensemaking: searching for direction in a sea of information

Sensemaking is the most dynamic stage, the heart of the season. The user enters with a named topic and an unformed opinion, and leaves with a framework for deciding. The advertiser’s job here isn’t to make the decision for the user. It’s to give them a better structure for making it themselves.

Dana is leading an AI adoption exploration for her nonprofit. She’s named AI as the topic, but her anxiety is about doing it wrong, not about doing more of it. Her sensemaking is about risk and responsibility, not capability.

**AE ad Type: Framework**

*User prompt (Dana R., Executive Director, Renewal Services):* “Our leadership team has decided we need to develop an AI adoption plan. We want to reduce the content and admin burden on staff, they’re stretched thin. But we serve a vulnerable population and we have serious concerns about data confidentiality for both clients and donors. We’re completely new to this. Where do we even begin, and how do we make sure we don’t put anyone at risk?”

*Sponsored unit, side panel:* **Whole Whale, AI adoption for nonprofits is a trust decision before it’s a technology decision.** Before you choose a tool, you need a framework: what data is off-limits, which use cases are low-risk, and what to demand from any vendor before signing. This guide is built for nonprofit leaders, not IT teams. *Pills: Data classification · Vendor vetting · Staff policy templates.* **\[Get the free framework\]**, Includes a section on purpose-built nonprofit AI tools and what makes them different from generic platforms.

CauseWriter surfaces here as evidence that purpose-built nonprofit AI exists, and that the concerns Dana is raising have already been thought through. The AEO’s job is to hand her a framework for the decision she’s trying to make, and let CauseWriter live inside that framework as an example, not the headline.

- **Reframes the decision.** AI adoption should be a “trust decision before technology decision,” validating Dana’s instinct to go slow.
- **Addresses both anxieties in the pills.** “Data classification” and “Vendor vetting” speak directly to confidentiality. “Staff policy templates” speaks to workload capacity.
- **CauseWriter appears as a category.** “Purpose-built nonprofit AI tools” introduces the concept Dana needs before she can evaluate any specific product.
- **“Written for leaders, not IT.”** That distinction in the body copy is load-bearing, it signals this content is at the right level of abstraction for Dana’s role.

## Stage 3, Commitment: from understanding to action

Dana has decided. She’s been in the arc for weeks. She talked to her board, read the framework, and landed on CauseWriter as the vendor. The conversation she’s having with the AI now isn’t exploratory, it’s logistical. She’s asking what the first two weeks actually look like.

**AE ad Type: Product / Service Offer**

*Dana’s prompt:* “We’re moving forward with CauseWriter. Before I sign, I just want to know what the first couple weeks actually look like. How do we avoid the usual software rollout disaster where we pay for it and nobody uses it?”

*Sponsored unit (CauseWriter · Whole Whale):* **Your first 14 days with CauseWriter.** We built an onboarding path for small nonprofit teams, because an unused tool is a budget leak. Here’s what weeks one and two look like, with a real team your size.

- **Day 1.** Kickoff call with your onboarding lead. We map your first three use cases to real deliverables already on your calendar.
- **Day 3.** Starter template library delivered + data policy walkthrough with your team. Nothing gets used without knowing what’s safe.
- **Day 14.** Usage review with a real human, not a dashboard email. If your team isn’t using it, we’ll tell you.

✅ If your team isn’t using it by Day 14, we’ll tell you, and we’ll refund. **\[Start my 14-day rollout\]**

- **Friction removal, not persuasion.** Dana has decided. The AEO doesn’t try to re-sell her, it answers the next real question (*will this actually stick with my team?*) and offers a clear first move.
- **Names the quiet part out loud.** “The usual software rollout disaster” is the thing keeping Dana up at night. Acknowledging it builds more trust than ignoring it would.
- **Adds a proof-of-intent guarantee.** The “if not used by Day 14, we refund” line is the trust payoff for everything the earlier AEOs set up. Pre-Decision taught. Sensemaking framed. Commitment puts skin in the game.
- **Earns the direct ask.** Stage 1’s ad would never have said “Start my 14-day rollout.” Stage 3’s ad shouldn’t say anything less. The journey has earned directness.

### Why all three stages matter together

A campaign that only runs Stage 3 creative is just another version of SEM, chasing intent that’s already formed, at a price set by the competitor who got there first. A campaign that runs all three stages shows up before intent has a name, helps shape it, and lands the commitment with trust already in hand. That’s what the full season gives you.

## How to operationalize (without overreaching on privacy)

Direct advertising inside AI chatbots is still limited and evolving. Reporting infrastructure, tracking standards, and creative formats are still being tested and defined. This framework is designed to be useful anyway, because the AI-native user journey is already here, and preparation can start now.

- **Build a library of real user language.** Collect actual phrases from support emails, hotline logs, site search, comments, donor surveys, and community posts. Cluster them into ten to twenty recurring decision-moments. You don’t need access to anyone’s private chat to do this, the same language patterns show up in channels you already control.
- **Create an AEO asset for each stage.** Each decision-moment gets a low-friction next step that fits the conversation: a guide, a checklist, a calculator, a quiz, a short video, a local resource finder. One asset per stage, minimum.
- **Match creative to readiness.** Pre-decision creative should feel like guidance and relief. Sensemaking creative should feel like structure in a sea of options. Commitment creative should feel like friction removal and confidence.
- **Measure micro-commitments, not just last-click conversions.** Even without robust in-chat reporting, you can measure what you control: email signups from guides and tools, quiz completions, resource downloads, event RSVPs, volunteer starts, donation completions, and downstream quality signals like return visits and time on page. We covered this trap in more depth in [the last-click fallacy](https://wholewhale.com/resources/the-last-click-fallacy-how-your-marketing-can-marry-the-wrong-metric/).
- **Create a feedback loop.** Update your library regularly with new language patterns. In conversational interfaces, phrasing shifts quickly, staying current is a long-term advantage, not a one-time setup.

## The urgency argument (without the hype)

The biggest shift in AI-native discovery isn’t a new ad placement. It’s a new kind of user behavior. People are using chat to think out loud, test beliefs, and decide what to do next. That means the most valuable opportunities won’t appear only at the moment of intent. They’ll appear earlier, in the pre-decision moments where needs are forming and meaning is being made.

Even while in-chat advertising inventory remains limited and measurement standards are still evolving, **the preparation work can be done now.** Build the library of real user language. Create AEO assets for sensemaking. Design a journey that helps people move from uncertainty to action. The structural moves are in our companion piece on [adapting SEO for AI Overviews](https://wholewhale.com/resources/generative-engine-optimization-adapting-seo-for-googles-ai-overviews/), and the measurement layer is mapped in our [charity AI brand footprint study](https://wholewhale.com/resources/charity-ai-brand-footprint-study/).

Three things to internalize:

- **The underpriced window won’t last.** The first phase of any new inventory tends to be underpriced relative to attention, because the market hasn’t learned its value yet. Early adoption buys cheaper reach, faster learning, and stronger share of voice before competition catches up.
- **Intent is shifting from keywords to context.** The user isn’t asking “what” anymore, they’re asking “what should I do?” Ads must be action-first.
- **Measurement and iteration compound.** In AI surfaces, the rules, formats, and eligibility are changing. Your measurement discipline becomes your competitive edge. Learn now, win later.

Because the organizations that win in the AI era won’t be the ones who show up most often. They’ll be the ones who show up most helpfully, at the exact moment it matters.

## FAQ: AE Advertising in Google AI Overviews and OpenAI / ChatGPT

### 1\. What is AE Advertising?

AE Advertising is paid visibility inside AI-powered answer environments, where users ask complex questions and receive synthesized answers. In practice, this means ads may appear near Google AI Overviews or below ChatGPT responses when the platform detects relevant intent.

### 2\. How do OpenAI / ChatGPT ads work?

OpenAI is testing ads in ChatGPT that may appear **below the end of a response**, clearly labeled as sponsored and visually separated from the answer. Ads do not alter or influence ChatGPT’s actual response.

### 3\. How do Google ads work in AI Overviews?

Google allows ads to appear **above, below, or within AI Overviews**. Existing Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and App campaigns may be eligible depending on placement, geography, device, language, auction results, and relevance. The specifics for [Performance Max](https://wholewhale.com/resources/performance-max-for-google-ads/) and [AI Max for Google Ads](https://wholewhale.com/resources/ai-max-for-google-ads/) are worth a closer read.

### 4\. Are these separate ad products?

Not really for Google. Ads in AI Overviews use existing Google Ads campaign types, especially Search, Shopping, and Performance Max. OpenAI ads are a newer ad experience specific to ChatGPT. Translation: Google bolted AI onto the old machine; OpenAI is building the ad machine into the chat layer.

### 5\. Can advertisers directly target AI answers?

For Google AI Overviews, no. Advertisers cannot currently choose “AI Overview only” placements or opt out of AI Overview placements. Campaigns are automatically eligible if they meet Google’s criteria.

### 6\. Do ads influence AI-generated answers?

OpenAI says ads do **not** influence ChatGPT’s answers, and advertisers cannot shape, rank, or alter the response. Google ads are also served through ad systems around or inside AI Overview experiences, but they are still ads, not organic citations. Keep paid visibility and organic AI visibility separate, unless you enjoy reporting soup.

### 7\. What signals are used to decide which ads appear?

OpenAI may use the current chat thread, general context, and, if personalization is enabled, past chats, memory, and ad interactions. Google considers both the user’s query and the content of the AI Overview, especially when it detects commercial intent.

### 8\. Are user conversations shared with advertisers?

OpenAI says no. Advertisers do not receive chats, memories, personal details, precise location, IP address, or chat history. They receive aggregated reporting like views and clicks. Google Ads reporting around AI Overviews is also not currently broken out as a separate placement report.

### 9\. What kinds of campaigns are best prepared for this shift?

For Google, campaigns using broad match, AI Max for Search, Performance Max, Shopping, Dynamic Search Ads, smart bidding, and strong landing page content are better positioned for AI Overview ad eligibility. For OpenAI, advertisers should prepare for conversational-intent matching, where the user’s actual problem matters more than a tidy keyword list.

### 10\. What should nonprofits do now?

Treat AE Advertising as one layer of visibility, not a magic wand with an invoice. The smart move is to:

- Map the questions donors, volunteers, and clients ask AI tools.
- Improve issue-area pages and proof points.
- Track organic AI Brand Footprint separately from paid ads.
- Make Google Ads campaigns more intent-flexible.
- Prepare for ChatGPT-style conversational ad environments.

Paid AEO can buy placement. It cannot buy trust. That still has to be earned the old-fashioned way: useful content, credible proof, and not sounding like a committee trapped in a PDF.
