## Introduction

During political campaigns, the American electorate becomes inundated with fundraising appeals. Roughly 6% of Americans respond to these appeals by making political contributions (Bouton et al., 2022). If political contributions are close substitutes for charitable contributions, then increases in political donations should reduce donors’ willingness to give to charity. Recent work has found that political contributions crowd out charitable contributions at a rate of $0.33 per dollar (Yildirim et al., 2024), and has interpreted this as evidence that donors substitute between these two categories of gift. In a similar vein, Cagé and Guillot (2022) also finds substitution between political and charitable giving, in which €352.2 in charitable donations is lost per additional €1 of political donations. The magnitudes of these estimates are quite large, suggesting that private charitable giving is at least half as sensitive to political donations as it is to government grants (Andreoni and Payne, 2011).

If charitable donors are this eager to substitute between political and charitable giving, then one of two cases must hold. Either the implied drop in charitable giving is spread out across so many charities that no single organization notices the loss, or charities do notice that political campaigns are siphoning off some of the revenue they would otherwise enjoy. A rational charity which understands that it faces increased competition for donated funds will adjust its fundraising expenses accordingly.

It follows that the observed loss in private charitable giving attributable to political donations conflates two separate effects. The first is the change in charitable donors’ willingness to give to charity, stemming from increases in political donations. The second is the change in charities’ fundraising expenses, induced by increases in successful solicitations by political campaigns. If the second effect is nonzero, then omission of charitable fundraising will bias estimates of the substitution between charitable and political giving. Until and unless the second effect can be ruled out, the extent to which donors substitute between political and charitable giving will remain an open question.

This paper therefore estimates the cross-elasticities of both the fundraising expenditures charities make, and the private donations charities receive, to political contributions. It is the first to estimate the elasticity of charitable giving to political giving using charity-level data. This can help us to understand how consequential political contributions are, from the perspective of the charity. After all, political donors are rare compared to charitable donors (Osili et al., 2021); even if political giving crowds out charitable giving to a substantial degree, it does not necessarily follow that the average charity notices the loss.

This paper also provides the first measure of the elasticity of charitable fundraising to political donations. By recognizing the charity’s active role in determining the relationship between political and charitable giving, this paper connects the nascent literature on political dimensions of charity to the well-established literature on charitable fundraising.

The close connection between political and charitable giving is rooted in donors’ pro-sociality: both categories of giving can be viewed as ways to influence aggregate public good provision. However, the relationship between the two types of gift is not purely altruistic. Each gift can generate “warm glow” utility, in which a donor derives particular benefit from their own contribution to the public good. Indeed, political and charitable giving each confer nearly identical reputational benefits and social opportunities to the donor. Since these two categories of giving can serve such similar functions, donors are likely to allocate their gifts to political and charitable recipients simultaneously.

Since these giving decisions are made simultaneously, an instrumental variables strategy is necessary to produce unbiased estimates of these cross-elasticities. The chosen instrument is the number of elections to federal or gubernatorial positions, held in the set of states where the charity fundraises, during its fiscal year. When more elections are held, the charity faces more competitors for donated funds. This drives up the dollar amount of political contributions made by people the charity might solicit for donations.

The count and timing of elections is exogenous to both sides of the market for charitable funds, in which charities solicit contributions from the donors who supply these funds. The number of elections held in a given time and place is also a strong, exogenous predictor of the flow of donations to political campaigns, thus solidifying both the relevance and exogeneity of the instrument. The identifying variation in this instrument comes from the geographic distribution of elections, as well as their timing relative to the charity’s fiscal year.

On average, a 10% increase in political contributions reduces charitable fundraising by 0.92%, and lowers private charitable giving by 0.78%. These estimates imply that political contributions crowd out private charitable giving by 37 cents on the dollar. These gifts are lost, not delayed: the drop in contributions does not reverse itself in the subsequent fiscal year.

Heterogeneity analyses explore how these elasticities vary with charity size, cause area, and fundraising archetype. While both the fundraising and contributions elasticities are more pronounced for larger charities, they are negative and statistically significant throughout the size distribution of charities. Some cause areas are more sensitive to competition from political campaigns than others: charities in the Health, Human Services, and Other National Taxonomy of Exempt Entities (NTEE) sectors lose donations to political contributions, whereas giving to charities in the Arts and Education sectors appears invariant to the volume of political donations. The final heterogeneity analysis separates charities into three groups, based on the frequency with which they report positive fundraising expenditures. The elasticity of private charitable giving to political contributions is most precisely estimated among charities which always fundraise. Among charities which never report fundraising expenses, this elasticity is smaller in magnitude and indistinguishable from zero.

The paper then decomposes the overall degree of crowd-out between political and charitable giving into “direct” and “indirect” components. Following Andreoni and Payne (2011), this decomposition recognizes that charitable contributions depend on political contributions both directly and indirectly. The indirect relationship exists because charitable contributions are an increasing function of the charity’s fundraising expenses, which in turn may depend on political contributions. The indirect relationship explains 133% of the total crowd-out between political and charitable contributions. This implies that if fundraising expenses were held constant, political contributions would crowd charitable giving in, rather than out.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents institutional context on charitable and political contributions in the United States. It proceeds to describe how this context has shaped the existing literature, and to discuss the paper’s contributions to that literature. Section 3 outlines the empirical strategy used to estimate the relationship between political and charitable giving. These estimation strategies are implemented using data presented in Section 4. Section 5 presents the estimates, along with a variety of heterogeneity analyses. Section 6 decomposes total crowd-out into direct and indirect components. Section 7 concludes.
