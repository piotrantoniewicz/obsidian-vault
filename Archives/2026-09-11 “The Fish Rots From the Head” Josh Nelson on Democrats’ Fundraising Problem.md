“The Fish Rots From the Head”: Josh Nelson on Democrats’ Fundraising Problem

The pervasive problems around Democratic fundraising — the endless spam, the [recurring donation scandals](https://insidepoliticalmoney.com/news/do-more-recurring-donation-scandals-lie-ahead/), the [shady super PACs](https://insidepoliticalmoney.com/news/a-lot-of-money-was-being-diverted-steve-schear-on-scam-pacs/), and the many, many annoyed donors (or *former* donors) — have gotten a lot of attention in recent years.

Most recently, the political scientist Adam Bonica published an in-depth investigation, “ [Hook and Squeeze](https://data4democracy.substack.com/p/hook-and-squeeze),” that described how “Democratic consultants, committees, and party leaders built a fundraising spam pipeline that funnels money from a captive pool of elderly donors into their own operations.” This fundraising model, Bonica wrote, “is a parasite on the party, enriching outside consultants while steadily starving the host.”

Josh Nelson — the CEO of [Civic Shout](https://civicshout.com/partners), an ad platform that works with over 1,000 progressive causes — has been saying much the same thing for years now, sounding the alarm about the dangers of spam fundraising to the Democratic Party and helping organize efforts to crack down on these practices.

Nelson started working in digital advocacy and fundraising for nonprofits, Democratic campaigns and super PACs some two decades ago. Over that time, he watched email and text messaging evolve from powerful tools for mobilizing supporters into an ecosystem of spammy and often deceptive appeals.

In a recent conversation, Nelson and I discussed how Democrats got here, why abusive fundraising practices persist, and what it may take to change the system. The interview has been edited and condensed.

## What drew you to the problem of deceptive political fundraising?

I’ve been doing digital mostly for nonprofits, but working with Democratic campaigns and PACs as well over the years, for about 20 years now. I’ve seen firsthand how powerful email, in particular, can be for raising money and for mobilizing people in advocacy campaigns, getting them to volunteer, to show up at hearings, getting them to call their elected officials at scale. It’s what I spent most of my career doing.

During that same time, I saw this sort of trajectory of campaigns realizing how powerful a tool this could be for fundraising, getting really good at that, and then going way too far, and getting this sort of tunnel vision of starting to think that your grassroots program or your email program, your text messaging program should only exist to raise money.

Dan Pfeiffer has written some pretty [interesting stuff](https://www.messageboxnews.com/p/the-high-cost-of-spam-how-dem-fundraising) about the damage that’s been done to the Democratic Party’s brand. He and I have talked about how Democrats have all these very carefully crafted messages. They’ve done their focus groups and their message testing, and then they go on MSNBC where 40,000 people see it or whatever, and then they blast out just some hyperbolic nonsense bullshit to 10 million people that’s just designed to raise money. Most people’s primary interaction with the Democratic Party then is these scammy texts and emails.

I don’t think this is a sustainable, long-term way to interact with your supporters and raise money. You’re raising a bunch of money in the near term at the expense of your long-term brand and your long-term relationship with supporters you’re going to need not just in 2026, but in ’28, ’30, ’32, etc.

## How did Democratic digital fundraising develop into the system we see today?

The Dean campaign was the first to use the Internet effectively. Campaigns had websites in ’96 and 2000. Of course, nobody was raising any money online. So the Dean campaign was the first to do the Meetup thing and get some success with fundraising. Obama then supercharged the fundraising, and in particular email fundraising.

Obama shattered all the fundraising records, primarily with email, and that’s what got the attention of the power brokers in the Democratic Party, the DCCC in particular. That was how they always talked about it at the time, when people started complaining about these tactics that the DCCC was the main one using in, like, 2010, 2012. They would say that, “We’re A/B testing everything. We’re doing more tests than anybody’s ever done on email fundraising,” and “We’re just optimizing for dollars raised.” Always optimizing: What’s the most we can raise with this email, without regard to what it does for anything else downstream?

The DCCC got very good at that. They “innovated” — I’ll put in quotes there — many of the tactics that are still in use today. A couple of their employees left to start Mothership Strategies, which became the firm that further innovated on that model, basically turned that grassroots fundraising model into a business model, and got a bunch of campaigns and PACs and leadership PACs and some of the top Democrats in Congress to engage in that sort of thing. All kinds of other copycat firms came out of that.

## Where does ActBlue fit into this story?

ActBlue makes it a lot easier, but they were around — they started in 2004, 2005 — so they were around throughout this and starting to get more and more traction with Democratic campaigns over the years.

In 2024, I helped organize an [open letter](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1o5k4vCCZ7lsofuw79lFS7_Juekf0tqvQA8uU2CGC2k0/edit?tab=t.0) to ActBlue. The 100-and-something people who signed onto the letter — people who had raised something like 7% of all the money ever raised on ActBlue — basically urged it to acknowledge the problem and take a leadership role in fixing it by strengthening and actually enforcing its account use policy.

About nine months later, ActBlue implemented some of the reforms we suggested. Our open letter included an appendix with dozens of specific recommendations for things they could do better, both on the product side and on the account use policy side. They ended up implementing some of those, not nearly enough, but what I considered at the time a significant step forward. Definitely the largest action they had taken to date to protect donors from these exploitative fundraising practices.

They’ve done some to enforce them, but enforcement has been, I think, insufficient and uneven. And in particular, they have proven unwilling or unable to enforce their terms on the big committees. The DCCC violates ActBlue’s account use policy just about every day. Some of the big, sketchier PACs that are not party committees, like the Progressive Turnout Project. And for whatever reason — we could speculate — they’ve declined to enforce their own terms.

## Why has ActBlue been reluctant to crack down?

My sense — I want to think about how to say this — is that they’re feeling pressure on one side from those of us who think this is a problem and want to protect donors and want the Democratic Party and Democratic campaigns to do better. I think they are also feeling pressure from the big entities themselves, including the party committees, who are pressuring them to maintain the status quo.

So I think there are implicit and maybe explicit threats to leave the platform, launch our own fundraising platform, things like that, if ActBlue were to actually tell the DCCC, “No, you can’t lie about donation matches. No, you can’t constantly do fake midnight deadlines and made-up numbers in your emails and things like that.”

## But let’s kind of consider the case for these tactics — which is that they work — and that without them, Democrats wouldn’t raise as much money.

I would say that it works if you’re defining success very narrowly. So if you’re measuring success on the basis of what’s the most possible money we can raise, and you’re not looking at any externalities or unintended consequences of that. You’re not looking at: What can we raise next cycle? What does this do to our relationship or standing with our grassroots donor base? What does this do for the Democratic Party’s brand and for our candidates’ reputation and people’s propensity to vote for them and volunteer for them?

I think there are a lot of people out there who, if they think about the Democratic Party, it’s, “Okay, those are the people that send me those shady text messages.” That might be their first association, which is not good for a party long-term.

## Yet Democratic fundraising has continued to grow. Where is the evidence that these practices are doing harm?

We don’t know the counterfactual and how many trillions of dollars Democrats would be raising if they hadn’t spent the last 20 years treating their donors like shit. For the record, I said that with a smile. I do think it’s extremely likely that Democrats would be raising more if they hadn’t — you know — exploited people at scale for decades.

There’s no question Democrats are raising lots of money. Generally, you would expect that to go up cycle over cycle, regardless of what’s going on. One of the things that may be happening is that Democrats are raising more money from fewer donors, which aligns very closely with Adam Bonica’s research. It’s this relatively small group of donors carrying more and more of that weight, and it adds up a lot when Grandma chips in $150,000 accidentally and stuff like that. That’s the trend we were seeing in ActBlue’s quarterly reports before they stopped reporting total unique donors per quarter in late 2024. So more dollars, I think, in some ways we’re seeing more dollars from fewer people, which is probably less sustainable and not what you would want. You’d rather have that broader base of support.

The best thing for political fundraising — there are two things that work really well for that. One is having a very charismatic hero, like Obama. The other is if you have the most evil villain possible to raise against. So I think Trump being there and being such a prominent part of Republican politics over the past, what, 11, 12 years now, is kind of lifting up Democratic fundraising and maybe obscuring some of the harm the party has done with its fundraising base.

## Fundraisers say they have tested more positive, straightforward appeals and found that they don’t work as well. What’s your response?

The defenders of these tactics, they’ll say, “Oh yeah, we’ve tested. We tested a more positive message. It doesn’t work.” Well, you’ve built a community of people who are responsive to the spam and scam tactics. You’ve got an email list of people who are responsive to the spam and scam tactics. Everybody else tuned you out years ago. Of course they don’t work, right?

The people who would respond to the fact-based, straightforward appeal that’s not trying to trick people stopped reading the DCCC’s emails years ago. They blocked them. They reported them as spam. You’re not reaching those people.

## If ActBlue cannot or will not solve this, who can?

There was a time when I thought that our best opportunity to make progress on this was moving the big vendors, and so that’s why we pushed NGP in a big way in 2021 and ActBlue in a big way in 2024. I think ActBlue is essentially captive to the DCCC and its big clients at this point.

If the CEO of ActBlue woke up tomorrow and said, “We’re going to do everything we can to protect donors, and we’re really going to crack down on this stuff,” I do think the big party committees and the scam PACs and all those folks would leave ActBlue that same day and would go somewhere else. Now it’s so easy to spin up a payment processor. They can vibe code one on Stripe in 45 minutes and start accepting donations on their own website without a vendor.

The fish rots from the head, and the people in charge of the party committees — the people that run the DCCC, DSCC and DNC, have to decide that this is a problem for the Democratic Party. It’s a problem for their candidates, and they need to take ownership of the problem. I don’t think we’ll fix it until committee leaders decide it’s their responsibility to help fix it.

## Why haven’t party leaders taken ownership of the problem?

I think in many cases they’re too disconnected from the grassroots donors. These are people who spend their time in rooms with wealthy donors and who probably are just very narrowly focused on hitting the fundraising numbers. They have come to see their job — yes, I guess broadly their job is electing Democrats — they have come to see the primary piece of that being to raise as much money as possible as quickly as possible.

They’re evaluating their success solely on the basis of dollars raised. At the end of the day, they are a little more accountable to, okay, do Democrats win or lose? But the fundraising numbers are a hard thing they can point to. So if the DCCC raises tons of money and the Democrats still don’t take the House, the DCCC can still say, “Well, we did our part.”

There’s no hard number we can tie back to the brand damage and the fact that people are avoiding their phones because they’re afraid Democrats are going to text them, and they’re not excited to go vote for them because they’re annoyed by all the texts and emails.

## Isn’t this fundamentally a collective-action problem, with each campaign rewarded for maximizing its own short-term return?

I think that’s why the party committees, which are supposed to take a broader view and care about what happens next cycle and the cycle after that, and care about the party’s reputation, should play a more active role here. Now, in order for them to actually be leaders and help encourage campaigns to do better, they have to clean up their own house first, so they’ve got a long way to go. They continue to be part of the problem on a day-to-day basis right now.

But yeah, it is absolutely a collective-action problem. It’s absolutely related to the short-term incentives of campaigns and PACs. In their view, if you lose the election, it doesn’t matter anyway. Winning is all that matters, so we have to just raise the money and get across the finish line.

## What pressure could realistically change the party committees’ behavior?

The high-dollar donors reaching out to their contacts at the DNC, DCCC, DSCC and telling them to cut the shit out would be extremely helpful, because I think the party committee leadership is far more likely to listen to those folks than they are to listen to you or me.

If big donors were to push the committees on this and threaten to withhold donations if the party doesn’t clean up its act, that’s probably one of our best opportunities. It maybe doesn’t totally fix it, but it would go a long way.

I think the other potentially interesting opportunity would be if there were legal ramifications. A lot of this stuff — I’m not a lawyer, but I can read — is wire fraud. So if there were people getting in trouble for that, if there were civil suits, if there were state attorneys general tracking down on this, that’s the other potential avenue right now.
