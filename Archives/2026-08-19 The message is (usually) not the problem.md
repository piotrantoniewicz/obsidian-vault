It’s increasingly clear that the biggest challenge progressive communicators face is not actually crafting a compelling message: it’s how to effectively distribute that message. Andre Banks wrote about this recently in his fantastic essay, *Built for a Different War* on Substack. He said that progressives waste time polishing the message, when their focus should be on how to actually get that message in front of people’s eyes and into their ears.

I agree with this analysis, but I want to push it a bit further to outline some actionable steps progressive campaigners can take to increase our impact.

### The Machine God and the Algorithm

The biggest shift in the communications and information environment in the last decade has been the move from chronological feeds to algorithmic ones. So today, as soon as you publish a piece of content on a social media platform, whether it be YouTube, Meta, or TikTok, you lose all control over who sees that content. The algorithm takes over, a machine god, dictating the flow of information to the community without regard for your strategy or chosen audience.

> Ben Thompson put it best: when distribution costs fall to zero, content gets commodified, and power moves to whoever controls the discovery of content.

In short, organic social media is wholly dependent on algorithms because it is free. Free distribution mechanism, no control. Paid distribution mechanism, more control (like paid ads).

### Is there a way out of this trap?

There are two ways we might deal with this as progressives, to build on Banks’ work. The first is to simply get better at leveraging the algorithm. This means leaning into emotional, polarising content and building audience relationships to overwhelm the algorithm itself. Second, to build owned media. Email newsletters, blogs, SMS, and podcasts via RSS feeds are all mechanisms we can use to deliver messages to our audiences without the mediating effect of the algorithm.

The thing is, both of these approaches have significant issues too. If we dive into mastering the algorithm, we will see wider distribution of our campaign messages. But it could be illusory. Relying on algorithms is like building a house on rented ground: it’s fine until the land is sold or you want to move. Gaming the algorithm also has a cost to our values, and it’s a resource-intensive beast, draining our ability to do much of anything else.

> If we try to move off algorithmic platforms in favour of wholly owned audiences, we lose the network effects gained through scale.

There is simply no way to grow an audience as quickly as social media. An email list is a slow, steady investment that pays off in the medium/long term. But it is no use to you if you are launching a new campaign: on issues, or in an election. The scale-up is too slow. Additionally, we miss the opportunity to directly engage in the political discourse of the day on these platforms, potentially undercutting the effectiveness of our organisations and diluting our power to persuade and engage with real people.

### Resilience versus reliance in progressive communications

The point isn't to escape the platforms; it's to not let them dictate the terms of the relationship. The solution goes like this: rented platforms should be top of funnel, attracting and engaging people. Organisations should then look to bridge audiences to spaces we own and control: email lists, online communities on Discord, podcasting, and (shock) in-person events and written publications.

This might seem radical, but it’s already happening. New media outlets, often started by founders who split from legacy media, have been using this two-step process for years. Defector, 404 Media, Crooked Media. Multi-issue advocacy groups are also built on owned infrastructure: GetUp, Leadnow, MoveOn.

There is a lot of low-hanging fruit for progressives in this strategy. Unions have email lists of potentially hundreds of thousands of people. Political parties, the same. These aren’t administrative databases. And they aren’t purely fundraising lists (looking at you, political parties…). They are distributional infrastructure.

And infrastructure makes us resilient. I’d rather us be resilient than reliant, especially not reliant on big tech and the almighty algorithm of the Machine God.
