### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | June 24, 2026

***A version of this piece first ran in my other newsletter, THE BIG PICTURE. I wasn't planning to cross-post it, but the more I thought about it, the more I realized its real subject isn't AI at all. It's how we lead our people through it. So, I reworked it for the TEAMS! family, because I thought it was worth sharing here. — Bryan***

This past week, I started jotting down the latest wave of AI news: the gigawatt superclusters, the IPO aftershocks, the agentic roadmaps, and the now-routine drumbeat of disruption. Then I spent an afternoon among the paintings at an exhibition of young creators at the Royal College of Art. I didn't go to escape the "AI frontier." If anything, I went in search of a different kind of signal, one that never shows up on a dashboard.

What I expected from a show of emerging artists in 2026 was a bleak portrait of the world we're handing them. Who could blame them? We are living through a massive, disorienting transformation, as roles dissolve into autonomous workflows and entire categories of work bend to the pace of machine execution. It would be the most natural thing in the world for the generation inheriting this moment to paint it in the colors of anxiety, grief, and the quiet loneliness of a hyper-connected, strangely disconnected world.

### That is not what I found.

What met me instead was a defiant, forward-leaning optimism. There were darker works, yes, and they did important work, holding up a mirror to how easily we grow numb to one another's pain. But the dominant note was alive, hopeful, and resilient.

> ***"We have art," Nietzsche wrote, "so that we do not perish from the truth." These young artists seemed to know that in their bones.***

The afternoon carried me back to my senior year at Santa Clara University and to a history professor who taught that art and culture are the truest leading indicators of where a society is headed. Artists feel the tremors of the future first. Carl Jung thought this was no accident. He described a collective unconscious, a deep, shared layer of the human psyche.

> ***"The artist," Jung wrote, "is not a person endowed with free will who seeks his own ends, but one who allows art to realize its purposes through him."***

If the collective unconscious of our young creators is leaning toward hope, perhaps a deeper human confidence is quietly gathering beneath all the noise and uncertainty.

### Here is where it turned, for me, from an essay about art into a lesson about leadership.

When I asked these artists about their process, not one of them used the words prompt, parameter, or optimize. Every single one spoke of flow, of reaching for something buried deep inside, drawing it up, and giving it away on the canvas. They described moments of visceral necessity, surges of creative energy that simply had to be expressed.

> ***A language model does not feel a burst of energy. It executes an algorithm and computes a probability.***

That distinction is easy to lose in the noise, and it matters enormously for those of us who lead organizations and the people within them. AI is, by its very architecture, a mirror, an extraordinary, tireless one. It can synthesize every style we've ever invented and reflect our feelings back to us. But it has data without a narrative. It can parrot our suffering and joy without ever having suffered or rejoiced itself. That act of transmission, something lived and then given away, is the one thing the mirror cannot originate. It can only reflect.

Which brings me to the real risk of this *Great Transformation* we’re living through. It was never about AI becoming human. The risk is that we make ourselves mechanical to keep pace with AI, compressing and flattening our organizations, teams, and lives into ever-tighter loops of metrics and efficiency, until we forget what efficiency was meant to serve.

> ***Efficiency is the proper genius of machines. Meaning is the province of human beings. We confuse the two at our peril.***

I keep returning to a concept from Pope Leo XIV's recent encyclical on AI, *Magnifica Humanitas*: ***cura personalis***, care for the whole person. Treat every individual as a unique being with their own spiritual, intellectual, and physical needs, not as a metric to be measured. Not a worker, not a user, not a unit of output, but the whole, unique person. It is an old idea, and it may be exactly the focus and discipline this very new technology demands of us.

For those of us in this work, it lands close to home. The heart of what our teams do, which is sitting with a person, earning trust, noticing the small things nobody else noticed, and being fully present, is work no mirror can do. It is the frontier of the soul, and for our people, it is the daily job.

Here is the work I believe falls to leaders. Aim the genuine, society-changing power of agentic AI at friction and gaps, the forms, the queues, and the grinding overhead that quietly steals our people's hours and pulls them away from the people they came to serve. Let the code carry the drudgery, freeing our teams to do what only people can do: create, connect, and be fully present for one another and for those who count on us.

That is what those young artists gave me on an ordinary weekday, a moment off the AI treadmill and a glimpse of the leading indicator my old professor taught me to watch for.

> ***The signal from the frontier is not despair. It is hope. And it is unmistakably human. This is the future I want us to scale toward, one with soul.***
