Last week, I sat down with our technology lead and our leadership team and we named exactly which documents a staff member may paste into an AI tool and exactly which ones would get them a phone call from our IT guy. The policy had to list document types rather than state principles.

This is part two of a four-part series on getting a nonprofit started with AI. [Part one was about building your own literacy first](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-tell-answer-ai-just-gave-you-any-good-wendy-clow-isixf/?trackingId=NdBVvpYCSva9nS%2FvBa6LZg%3D%3D), on the argument that you learn to judge these tools by running them on work where you already know the right answer. This part is about the same problem one level up, which is how an organization decides what may go into the tool at all.

### The values question, briefly

I want to deal with the ethics question honestly and then move past it, because I have written the long version already and repeating myself would waste your time.

The worry usually arrives in one of three forms. There is a fear about authenticity, since donors gave to people rather than to a machine. There is a fear about integrity, since automating part of the work can feel like cutting a corner. And there is a fear about identity, since if a tool can do a piece of the work, you start to wonder what that says about the work.

My answer, worked out over about a year, is that **staff time in a nonprofit is the scarcest resource on the balance sheet and the one most directly tied to the mission**. A business that automates routine work turns the saved hours into profit. A nonprofit that automates routine work turns the saved hours into mission. Every evening a program manager loses to reformatting a report is attention the families never receive, and we have simply grown used to absorbing that cost without calling it what it is.

If you want that argument in full, it is in my newsletter from June under the title about [AI belonging in mission-first work](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-belongs-mission-first-work-reason-mission-itself-wendy-clow-zu1lf/?trackingId=%2FG4uiBhISVikOZcbvIZyfg%3D%3D).

I am moving past it here because the values conversation has a comfortable property, which is that it never has to be specific. You can hold it for eighteen months. I have watched organizations do exactly that, and at the end of it their staff are still using AI, just without any rules, on personal accounts, with nobody able to say what went where.

### Writing it down

The moment you have to write the policy down, the abstractions stop.

Our first draft said something close to "be careful with client data." I read it back and realized I had written a hope rather than a rule. Careful is not a decision procedure. A housing counselor sitting at her desk at four in the afternoon with a case note she needs help structuring cannot act on careful. She needs to know whether this specific document, the one in front of her, is allowed.

So the policy we ended up with does one job. It tells you which bucket the thing in your hand falls into before you touch it.

### The three buckets

This was approved by our technology lead and leadership on August 6, and it is now the rule at Habitat for Humanity of Michigan. I am publishing it in full because I think it is more useful to you than a blank template would be. Take it, change the specifics to match your own funders and systems, and put your own name on it.

**Green: go ahead.** Work that contains no information about a specific identifiable person.

- Program descriptions, process documentation, and standard operating procedures
- Templates and form letters with names and details removed
- Meeting notes and agendas about internal operations
- Grant narrative drafting where the content is about the program rather than about individuals
- Communications about process, deadlines, and requirements
- Marketing copy, newsletters, social posts, and web content
- Aggregate and de-identified numbers, for example "we closed 63 homes this year"
- Anything already public, including your own published materials and public funder guidance
- Learning how a system works, drafting a question, or figuring out how to word something

**Yellow: strip it first, then go ahead.** Work where the underlying task is fine but the document carries identifying details the task does not actually need.

- A case note you want help structuring, with the name, address, and any account or case number removed first
- A client letter you want to draft, written with placeholders and filled in afterward in Word or Outlook
- A tracking spreadsheet you want analyzed, with the identifying columns deleted and only the ones the question needs kept
- An email thread you want summarized, with signature blocks, phone numbers, and addresses removed

The test is one question. Is this specific person's identity necessary to answer the question I am asking? It almost never is. You want help with structure, wording, or logic, and none of those require knowing who the client is.

**Red: stop.** Do not put this in.

- Client case files, income documentation, pay stubs, bank statements, tax returns
- Credit reports, credit scores tied to a named person, debt-to-income worksheets for a specific household
- Anything carrying a Social Security number, date of birth, or account number
- Donor records, giving histories, and contact lists
- Signed agreements, closing documents, and deeds
- Photographs or scans of identity documents, including driver's licenses
- Anything received from a federal or state housing agency about a specific household
- Login credentials or passwords for any system

Three buckets is the whole design. A staff member can hold three categories in their head while looking at a document, and they cannot hold a two-page policy. That is why most AI policies get read once during onboarding and then stop affecting what anybody actually does.

### The four decisions underneath it

Writing the buckets took a couple of hours. The decisions underneath them took considerably longer, and they are the ones I had not seen discussed anywhere when I went looking.

**Where the connected folder lives.** These tools work by being pointed at a folder on your computer, and the natural instinct is to point them at the folder where the files already are, which for most of us is a synced cloud drive. The consequence is that everything in that folder becomes reachable by default, including whatever syncs in next month that nobody consciously decided to expose. We chose a separate local working folder that staff copy files into deliberately. The extra copying step is the point of it, because copying a file forces you to look at it and decide, and that decision is exactly what the three buckets are asking for.

**Whether sessions run on your machine or in the cloud.** On our plan there is an organization-wide setting controlling whether the work executes in the vendor's cloud or locally, and it defaults to the cloud. This one went against my recommendation. I had a weak preference for turning it off, and leadership decided the convenience was worth it, which is a defensible call. With the safeguards listed in the next two paragraphs in place, letting our AI tool run in the cloud is safe. The training module now says plainly that sessions run on the vendor's infrastructure.

**The feedback buttons.** I wrote about this in a LinkedIn post yesterday. The thumbs up and thumbs down control is the single route by which your content can reach model training, and it carries a five-year retention window. We disabled it for the whole organization rather than training people to avoid it.

**System connections.** Whether the tool may be connected to your database, your file storage, or your email. Our answer for this phase is no, written down plainly so it is a rule rather than a judgment call, and the training tells staff to assume the answer is no and not to attempt a connection. We will revisit it once the rollout has a track record.

### The two decisions about people

I expected the technical decisions to be the hard ones. These two have mattered more in practice.

**Who does someone ask when they don't know?** A three-bucket policy still produces edge cases, and an edge case with no owner becomes a guess. We named a person for judgment questions and a second person for technical ones, and we committed to answering within twenty-four hours. The response time is the part that does the work, because if asking takes three days then guessing is the rational choice and your policy is at risk to stop applying.

**What happens when somebody gets it wrong?** Somebody eventually will, given a new tool, real deadlines, and a document that looks green from one angle. We made the reporting procedure blameless and specific: delete the conversation, tell me what happened, and we log it and look at whether the rule needed to be clearer.

That framing is self-interested rather than generous. A mistake nobody reports is one you cannot correct, and it is also the one that surfaces eighteen months later in a funder conversation with no warning. A policy that frightens people produces the same number of mistakes and costs you the ability to hear about them.

### What the policy costs us

Earlier this year I ran an AI audit across five departments at Habitat Michigan and interviewed staff about their actual daily work. The two largest time savings that audit found are both in the red bucket. Case note drafting after counseling appointments currently runs thirty to forty-five minutes per appointment. Assembling a closure portfolio means manually converting Word files, photographs, and PDFs into a single submission document. Both involve client information that is not going anywhere near this tool under the policy I just wrote.

So the policy blocks the best opportunities on the list. I approved it anyway, and I would encourage you to do the same thing in your own organization.

The reasoning is that the safe work is substantial on its own. Affiliate correspondence, process documentation, grant narrative drafting, class attendee reporting, and internal communications are all sitting in the green bucket and all of them consume real hours today. Starting there builds a track record. In a few months I can go back to our technology lead with evidence about how this has actually been used rather than with a proposal about how it might be. That is a considerably stronger conversation than the one I could have this month.

The organizations that get into trouble with this are usually the ones that started with the hardest and most sensitive use case because it had the biggest number attached to it.
