The result is a market that is simultaneously high‑risk, fragmented, and existentially important.

A market democracy cannot live without, and investors still don’t trust

The investment case for political tech ought to be obvious: campaigns and causes pour hundreds of millions into digital outreach; voters live online; and data has become the raw material of power. Yet the sector remains undercapitalised, especially in Europe, where campaign finance caps, multi‑party systems and strict regulations keep the market small, fragmented and hard to read.

Two structural problems dominate. First, the “total addressable market” is almost invisible when sliced across 24+ political and regulatory landscapes, each with different rules on spending, donations and data. Second, political tech rarely promises the scale‑fast, exit‑soon model familiar from SaaS; it cycles with elections, relies on trust, and is hemmed in by compliance. In France, for example, individuals are capped in what they can donate to parties, and past scandals, including a former president jailed over illicit campaign financing, have reinforced a culture of tight controls.

That caution protects democracy from oligarchs and offshore cash, but it also depresses the “willingness to pay” that makes software markets grow. In this landscape, classical venture capital simply does not map: fund maths that demand 10x returns in five years are out of step with products whose “ROI” is measured in institutional resilience, not margin.

#### When the business case is democracy itself

To understand who steps into that gap, look at the people now building the ecosystem. One founder in Georgia runs the country’s only business angel network and has pushed her members into “democracy tech” because high‑quality, for‑profit products started showing up at the dealflow table, even if grant‑dependent, donor‑oriented teams still dominate the field. Her message is blunt: treat political tech like fintech, except with a longer runway and more regulatory landmines, there are still scalable B2B and B2C plays that deserve early‑stage capital.

Elsewhere, a Portuguese entrepreneur has turned his experience building startup accelerators into an academy for young politicians, an “accelerator of politicians” designed to seed legislatures with trained, pluralist, digitally literate leaders rather than leave the field to populists. In a few years, his academy has produced nearly a hundred active figures spread across government, opposition, local politics and NGOs, at the precise moment an extreme‑right party has vaulted from non‑existence to the second force in parliament.

In Paris, a health‑tech founder spearheaded an endowment fund after the dissolution of the National Assembly pushed France to the brink of a far‑right breakthrough. Her vehicle, explicitly non‑partisan and philanthropic, has raised millions of euros in under a year and is racing against the 2027 electoral clock to shore up electoral integrity. It backs organisations working on three pillars: access to reliable information, spaces for dialogue in polarised societies, and citizen engagement through voting and running for office.

What unites these efforts is not a shared revenue model but a shared analysis: political tech is an infrastructure for state performance and legitimacy, not a niche campaign add‑on. It is, as one German innovation agency argues, a “trust and efficiency play” in systems where the real bottleneck is not money or talent, but decision‑making that is too slow, fragmented and disconnected from reality.

#### Participation as infrastructure, not a ‘nice‑to‑have’

That German agency, a federal body for breakthrough innovation, has quietly begun treating digital participation like ERP software for the state. In its view, participation is now a data‑processing endeavour: a decision‑support stack that helps institutions detect friction early, prioritise across complexity and execute better policy.

Its flagship experiment borrows from Taiwan’s presidential hackathon and digital democracy playbook. A nationwide programme invites thousands of citizens to surface pain points in bureaucracy and regulation, clusters those inputs with AI, and then channels them into civic tech teams that co‑create solutions alongside civil servants. The best prototypes receive funding and multi‑phase implementation, turning complaints about “what sucks” into a pipeline of digitally enabled reforms.

This is political tech as core infrastructure: a participation stack that upgrades state performance while giving citizens visible feedback loops, input, output and “outflow” of decisions they helped shape. The political theory is simple. If citizens don’t participate, governments still get data, just bad, late and expensive data, often filtered through lobbyists or media uproars. A digital participation system that is assistive, scalable and decision‑relevant by design can both de‑risk policy and rebuild self‑efficacy in societies where trust has become brittle.

The investment implication is more radical. You are not funding a gadget for the next election; you are funding a layer of the state’s operating system. That kind of asset rarely spins off unicorn exits. It does, however, produce public‑goods returns, more responsive bureaucracies, fewer implementation failures, lower long‑term costs, that can justify a mix of public money, patient private capital and mission‑driven funds.

#### Agentic AI and the end of the big campaign stack

If regulation and fragmentation hold the sector back, AI is blowing it open. Across the panel, there was a shared conviction that the “SaaS era” of political tech, big, centralised stacks, long sales cycles, armies of consultants, is already dying.

New tools like code‑generating models and agentic systems make it possible for tiny, highly specialised teams to spin up campaign or civic tools in weeks rather than years. One investor described peer‑to‑peer organising apps being built by teams of one or two people, particularly in Eastern Europe, where democracy is under direct pressure. Another pointed to a Swedish AI “unicorn” built with a minimal headcount as a taste of what happens when even politically focused products can scale at software speed.

This cuts both ways. Far‑right actors, unburdened by concerns about truth or regulation, are often first to exploit new platforms, hacks and grey zones. They move fast, break norms and treat content moderation as an obstacle to route around, not a framework to respect. Campaigns on the progressive or liberal side, by contrast, are entangled in compliance, slow decision‑making and a legacy tech stack that in the US was described as “20 years old and not working anymore.”

For investors, the lesson is to shift focus from “big tech teams” to “problem‑holders”: practitioners with deep experience in specific political markets and constituencies who can use AI to iterate solutions rapidly. Capital, in this thesis, should back small, embedded teams that know their terrain, whether that is fundraising, mobilisation, counter‑disinformation or youth organising, and then give them the runway to adapt over multiple electoral cycles.

#### The uncomfortable politics of money

All of this plays out in an emotionally charged domain where reputational risk can be lethal. Corporate boards, donors and even family offices hesitate to fund explicitly political projects for fear of blowback, even when their long‑term business interests align with democratic stability.

Campaign‑linked tools also face an awkward reality: the perception that a billionaire can move an election by dropping a fortune into platform ads undermines the case for painstaking, technology‑driven field work. Yet recent races show that money alone cannot guarantee victory; it amplifies messages, but does not fix bad ones. That nuance is hard to communicate in a media environment primed to equate spending with power.

The more profound risk is complacency. Across the session, European speakers pointed to the speed with which extreme‑right forces have risen from fringe to governing contender, fuelled by digital mobilisation and narrative discipline. The message was stark: if mainstream investors and institutions shy away from “political risk,” they simply cede the terrain to actors who do not.

That places a heavier burden on angels, syndicates and philanthropic capital, which are typically more values‑driven and willing to accept lower financial returns in exchange for impact. But it also suggests a role for corporates and family offices that see autocracy, corruption and instability as existential threats to their long‑term operations. Defending the basic conditions of open markets may be the most rational “defensive investment” they make.

#### From sector to safeguard

The most striking refrain from the panel was about time. Europe cannot afford to build its political tech ecosystem on Silicon Valley timelines while its political landscape shifts month to month.

In Portugal, in France, across Central and Eastern Europe, the next two to three electoral cycles will likely determine whether liberal democracy remains the default or becomes the exception. That urgency is forcing a redefinition of risk and return. The right question is no longer “can this tool reach a billion‑euro valuation?” but “can this tool help prevent democratic backsliding at a national or European scale?”

That demands an investment vocabulary closer to climate or health security: blended finance, public‑private partnerships, long‑term grant‑plus‑equity models, and regulatory frameworks that recognise participation and information integrity as critical infrastructure. Political tech, understood this way, is less a niche and more a safeguard: the connective tissue between citizens, institutions and the data that now mediates almost every decision in public life.

If capital continues to treat it as a curiosity, democracies will fight 21st‑century battles with 20th‑century tools, while their opponents do not.
