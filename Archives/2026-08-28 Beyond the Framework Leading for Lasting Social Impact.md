### TEAMS! Newsletter | Stewardship Edition | August 28, 2026

For many leaders today, including those in social services and nonprofits, the weight of responsibility can feel overwhelming. In the social services sector, we need to stretch every donor dollar, navigate unpredictable and shifting community needs, and keep our teams together and motivated amid the uncertainty many organizations face. It is understandable why so many leaders default to survival mode, focusing on maintaining current programs rather than fundamentally rethinking how to fulfill the mission in the future.

The old approaches were not built for today’s realities. Organizational stewardship can no longer be just about “holding the line”; as hard as it is, we need bold adaptation to meet the future. Recent insights from Bob Sternfels, Global Managing Partner at McKinsey, offer a powerful blueprint for how organizations need to transform to survive and thrive. When we apply these corporate insights to the social sector, one truth becomes clear: preserving our mission's integrity requires modernizing governance, reinvesting in our workforce, and shifting our focus from activities to outcomes.

### Executive Summary

Today’s social sector stewardship requires a shift from traditional, transactional service delivery to shared, outcome-driven impact. By integrating human-centered AI, building resilient organizational structures, developing adaptive talent, and implementing rigorous governance standards for technology use, nonprofit leaders can ensure long-term sustainability and nurture community trust.

### Key Takeaways

- **Services to Outcomes:** Shift from measuring outputs (like beds filled or meals served) to underwriting long-term, systemic community outcomes.
- **Human + Agentic Workflows:** Leverage AI to automate administrative overhead while elevating irreplaceable human judgment and empathy.
- **Resilience-Based Talent:** Prioritize continuous learning and collaborative aptitude over static credentials when hiring for social impact.
- **Proactive Stewardship:** Implement rigorous compliance and centralized oversight to safeguard community trust and public integrity, especially when implementing new technologies.
- **Unlocking Internal Potential:** Eliminate structural bottlenecks and tap into frontline staff insights to drive mission-aligned innovation.

### A Framework for Lasting Social Impact

**From Program Delivery to Outcome Partnership.** For decades, the standard nonprofit funding model has rewarded volume, measured by the number of clients served, hours of counseling provided, or packages delivered. Of course, scaling impact matters, but it can often overlook individual experiences and become a “homogenized” metric. Community stewardship starts with one person’s results, which requires aligning our organizational success directly with individual transformation, ultimately improving the entire community's experience.

Imagine a community housing nonprofit shifting its operational narrative. Instead of contracting for "number of shelter beds occupied per night," the organization enters into an outcome-based performance agreement tied to "sustained independent housing stability at 24 months." By underwriting the outcome alongside government and philanthropic partners, the agency moves from a simple service provider to a co-creator of long-term social stability.

**Integrating Human Creativity with AI Agents.** In the social sector, technology is often viewed as either a costly burden or a magic fix. In reality, half the secret sauce to adopting new tools like AI lies in organizational redesign. Leaders face a choice between conservative budget preservation and radically improved efficiency; the middle ground is to leverage AI to flatten overhead so frontline staff can focus on high-touch human care.

Consider a child and family services agency integrating AI agents into its daily workflow. Rather than caseworkers spending 15 hours a week manually entering client notes, summarizing case files, and drafting routine compliance reports, autonomous agents handle the administrative legwork. This frees caseworkers to do what AI cannot: exercise deep human judgment, set high aspirations for struggling families, and build trust-based relationships.

**Hiring for Resilience, Judgment, and Aspiration.** The credentials that defined leadership in the past will be less effective at driving future social impact. With the rapid pace of change, subject-matter expertise has a shorter half-life now. Social sector organizations must stop screening purely for yesterday’s resumes and start screening for the future, with skills such as resilience; people who have faced setbacks, adapted, and recovered; and a “lifelong learner” mindset.

When hiring program directors for a youth workforce program, an organization should move beyond predefined degree requirements and prioritize candidates who demonstrate high learning agility, strong interdisciplinary thinking, and the ability to collaborate across multiple partners. Artificial intelligence can handle linear problem-solving, but people are required to set bold aspirations, make non-linear creative leaps, and exercise moral judgment in complex community settings.

**Safeguarding Trust Through Governance.** Nonprofits often pride themselves on a mission-driven ethos, assuming that good intent naturally yields good behavior. Yet without rigorous controls, ethics alone cannot guarantee operational integrity. As scale increases and community dynamics grow more complex, organizations must adopt governance and compliance standards comparable to those of publicly traded entities to protect their reputational standing; this is especially true when adopting new technologies, such as AI.

A regional food bank expanding across multiple counties cannot rely on informal regional autonomy. By modernizing internal audits, establishing strict partner selection criteria, and standardizing financial controls, leadership preserves the institution's integrity. Relinquishing a small degree of local autonomy is a worthwhile trade-off to ensure stronger transparency, avoid conflicts of interest, and maintain public trust.

**Speed, Flattened Structures, and Empowered Innovation.** Bureaucratic bottlenecks silently kill social impact. Nonprofit leaders often struggle with slow decision-making that delays frontline response times. Furthermore, executives often look externally for solutions when the best ideas are already embedded in their frontline teams.

Consider a health equity nonprofit working to improve neighborhood access to preventive care. Rather than waiting for multi-layered executive committees to approve a new outreach pilot, leadership flattens decision-making and allocates seed funding directly to frontline community health workers. Moving fast and iterating based on direct community feedback yields far better results than waiting for a slow, perfected “corporate strategy”.

### Redoing Your Operating Framework

- **Review Your Current Metrics:** Review your top three programs and identify how to pivot key performance indicators from outputs (units delivered) to long-term impact outcomes.
- **Conduct an AI Workflow Mapping Session:** Identify one high-volume administrative process (e.g., intake routing or grant reporting) that can be delegated to automated tools to reclaim staff time for direct client engagement.
- **Revise Interview Scoring Protocols:** Update your talent assessment criteria to explicitly evaluate candidates on resilience, continuous learning capacity, and collaborative problem-solving.
- **Strengthen Governance Controls:** Convene your board’s audit or governance committee to review your criteria against modern risk standards, specifically including AI governance guidelines.
- **Host a Frontline Listening Session:** Skip middle-management layers this week to ask frontline staff: "What bottleneck is preventing you from serving our community faster, and what solution do you already have?"

### Closing Thoughts

To honor our mission and the people who rely on us, we must be brave enough to dismantle outdated structures, leverage modern tools, and lead with accountability. The work we do matters too much to settle for traditional, slow-moving frameworks. Stewardship today means leading into the future, one with many unknowns, with the courage and determination to change and adapt, without ever losing sight of our destination: serving our communities.

> ***Stewardship is not about keeping an institution comfortable; it is about keeping it capable.***
