***Making AI something you can trust for mission work is no small feat. Here is what it entails and how a team might go about it.***

I've spoken with more than a few nonprofit leaders who have run into the same issue with AI. They will put one of the big-name tools to the test and get a first draft that is surprisingly well done; they are left thinking this could be a game changer. But then they put it to a more exacting test, asking it to parse an organizational program policy or a funding rule, and it will give an answer with all the confidence in the world and yet be completely off base. There is a name for that kind of disconnect.

> ***The difference between general capability and domain reliability,***

**Which is how** they put it at the recent AWS Marketplace workshop on building domain-specific AI agents. The workshop was intended primarily for engineers, but the takeaways are relevant to any organization, ours included. I have put my own spin on them for the nonprofit sector, and we are putting them to use here at AbilityPath.

### A Capable Generalist Is Not a Reliable Specialist

You would be hard-pressed to find an AI model today that isn’t broad and fluent. They can reason their way through almost anything. What they lack is an understanding of your world: the people, the regulations, the policies. And when they err, it is not always easy to spot. The mistakes can be subtle and plausible, yet dead wrong.

The workshop made clear that you cannot simply flip a switch to turn an AI generalist into an AI specialist. It has to be done deliberately. For a mission-driven organization steeped in judgment and clear rules, that is welcome news. We don’t view specialization as a way to curtail what AI does, but as a way to focus it where it needs to perform in support of our work.

### Four Levers Your Organization Can Pull

The presenters laid out four levers for tailoring AI to your domain. No technical expertise is required to use them.

- **Instructions.** Think of this as the AI's job description. Before it gets to work, you should tell it who it is, what its responsibilities are, how to carry them out, and what is off-limits. A good job description won’t turn AI into an expert overnight, but without one, confusion is inevitable. We’re developing an AI Prompt Playbook for our staff, so they know to set clear boundaries and context for the AI tools they use.
- **Knowledge.** This is what the AI is permitted to draw from. It should analyze your actual program data and policies, not a generic notion of how things are done. That information must be up to date and thorough; otherwise, it will fail. For those of us under state regulations and shifting funding rules, this is essential. At AbilityPath, we have gone to some lengths to unify the data from our various programs because AI is only as sound as the information it is built on.
- **Tools.** In other words, what it can do. Grant it access only to the systems a task requires. A narrow scope limits harm if something goes wrong and tends to yield better results. Be sure to ask: what can this tool see and what can it alter?
- **Guardrails.** Or what is forbidden. I found this to be one of the key lessons. An AI can talk its way around instructions. When safety, privacy, or legal liability are at stake, the system itself must enforce the rule. For example, with protected health information, we don’t just include it in a prompt. We have Business Associate Agreements with our AI and software providers and other partners, documented security protocols for every tool the team uses, and have evaluated many platforms to ensure HIPAA compliance.

### Decide How Right Is Right Enough Before You Build

The workshop presenters put forward a practical idea: set your reliability target before an AI project even gets off the ground. For internal productivity tools where staff can correct any mistakes, 80 to 85 percent is fine. But for anything the public will see, aim for 90 to 95 percent, since errors quickly erode trust. And if medical, legal, or financial information is involved, as with our human-facing work, we’d aim for 99 percent.

In a nonprofit context, the line of sight is clear. An AI an employee uses to put together meeting notes doesn’t have to be flawless. One that touches donors or families must clear a much higher hurdle. For matters of a participant’s health or safety, a trained human should remain firmly in control. At AbilityPath, we distinguish between exploratory questions, where speed is key, and metrics for the board or compliance, which must be exact. We may use the same tools, but the standards differ.

### Make Sure There Is a Handoff to a Human

Don't let a customer-facing AI go live without a policy for when to involve a person in the process. The workshop stressed this point. Whether it is an upset individual, a request beyond scope, or a lack of confidence in the system, the handoff needs to be smooth and carry all the context so the person doesn't have to rehash their story.

For us, serving families in trying times, this is perhaps the most critical design choice. A parent calling about their child must not get the runaround. The technology should connect them to the right person promptly, with the background already known.

### Put It to the Test with Actual Questions

An AI can look good on a few selected examples but falter when faced with what people actually ask. The key is to have experts review results from a test set of genuine inquiries and edge cases. Nonprofits are well positioned to do this; our front-line staff knows the hard and odd questions that come from partners and families. They should write the test cases before the tool goes public.

### A Smarter Chatbot Won’t Solve Everything

Some problems call for the right tool, versus just any LLM. Language models are not up to the task of precise math, such as plotting the best route across a dozen stops with time windows; leave that to purpose-built routing software. We will keep that in mind as we update our transportation program.

And if you are going to serve more than one language, it has to be done properly. If a family is speaking Spanish, the AI’s knowledge base and responses need to be in Spanish, not a half-measure translation of the opening line. That is the standard we will apply to any tool, given the number of Spanish-speaking families in our programs.

### Speed Comes from Good Governance

You don’t need a deep technology budget for this, just leadership. Our ability to move with confidence at AbilityPath comes from the governance foundation we have in place: a Board AI Governance Charter, an oversight working group, and a curriculum to build staff AI literacy.

I would tell any nonprofit leader to start with clear rules. Write the job description for the AI, limit its access, curate the knowledge, and determine what level of accuracy is sufficient. Start small and keep the people closest to your mission at the heart of the process.

> ***An AI can give a generalist the veneer of an expert. It is on us to ensure it is one before anyone we serve is relying on it.***
