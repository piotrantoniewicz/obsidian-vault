***Connection, choice, and confidence: the foundation of stewardship that lasts***

### TEAMS! Newsletter | Stewardship Edition | October 2, 2026

The longer I do this work, the more convinced I am that every donor seeks the same three things, whether the gift is $50 or $500,000. **They want to be seen**: known by name and connected to the people and purpose behind the work. **They want to be free:** giving because they choose to, on their own terms, without a sense of debt. And **they want to be sure:** confident that what they gave made a real difference.

Psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan would recognize that list. Their self-determination theory holds that:

> ***People thrive when three basic needs are met: relatedness, autonomy, and competence.***

The needs that keep a staff member engaged are the same as those that keep a donor engaged. That raises a practical question for any nonprofit: can we meet those needs for hundreds of donors and still have each one feel personally known? I think a good tailor answered it long ago. Every garment starts with a standard pattern, and the fitting is what makes it yours.

### Learnings at a Glance:

Before we get to the pattern itself, here are the four ideas this edition rests on:

- **Donors have needs, too.** Connection, choice, and confidence turn a first gift into a lasting relationship.
- **Proximity beats reporting.** A donor who sees, hears, and feels the impact remembers it long after the numbers fade.
- **Small rooms build big relationships.** Salons, Jeffersonian dinners, and giving circles spark conversations a gala cannot.
- **Personalization can scale.** Standardize the timing of the giving journey and personalize what fills it.

### Five Pillars of Lasting Stewardship

The first three pillars follow the needs every donor brings to a gift: connection, choice, and confidence. The fourth is about bringing donors close enough to feel the work, and the fifth is about doing it all at scale. Together they describe what a donor experiences when stewardship is done well.

**Connection Before Contribution.** Relatedness comes first and is built in small rooms. A salon in someone's living room, a giving circle that pools its gifts and decides together, and an invitation to lend professional expertise as well as money: each one tells a donor they belong to something.

The Jeffersonian dinner may be the best example. Jeffrey Walker developed the format while chairing the foundation that runs Monticello. He and Jennifer McCrea describe it in their book *The Generosity Network*. Eight to twelve people share a table and a conversation, opened by a question sent in advance. No one is asked for a gift. People leave knowing each other and why the cause matters to them.

**Choice, Not Obligation.** A gift given freely is one a donor wants to repeat. A gift given under pressure is one they hope to be finished with. Autonomy means the donor decides how, when, and how deeply to engage, and "not now" is always an acceptable answer.

It also changes how we read a first, modest gift. Many donors begin with a test gift, watching how the organization responds and whether a connection to the mission takes hold. The donor is quietly interviewing us, and our response is the answer.

**Confidence the Gift Mattered.** Donors want to be good at giving. That is the “competence” need, and it is met when they can see the impact of their generosity. Every donor carries two quiet questions after a gift: Do you remember me? Did it make a difference?

Strong stewardship answers the question before it is asked. The answer is specific: what changed, for whom, and why.

**Proximity to Impact.** A report can tell a donor how many people you served, but it cannot convey what the gift meant to those people. Impact has to be more than metrics. It has to be real and human, and it has to reach the senses and the emotions.

So bring donors close. A morning spent with students or an afternoon meeting with families does what no dashboard can; it makes it real. The donor stops reading about the mission and feeling it.

**A Standard Pattern, But a Personal Fit.** This is where the tailor comes in. We can standardize the giving-journey pattern: the moments when every donor hears from us, and what kind of moment each one is. That part scales. Here's one version of the journey pattern:

- A personal thank-you within 48 hours of every gift
- One human story at 90 days
- One invitation a year to see the work up close
- A real choice offered before any next ask

The “fitting” is what fills those moments: the story chosen for this donor, the invitation that aligns with their interests, and the note from someone they know. The calendar is the same for everyone. The content is for them alone.

### Your Playbook:

The Five Pillars describe what good stewardship looks like. The playbook is how you and your team can start practicing it. None of these steps needs a new system or a bigger budget. Any development team can begin the first one this week.

- **Map the first twelve months.** Write down every moment a donor hears from you during the year after a gift. That map is your pattern.
- **Treat every first gift as a test gift.** Respond to a $100 gift with the same care you would give a $10,000 one. The donor is deciding whether to come closer.
- **Build in a choice at every stage.** Offer a visit, a conversation, a volunteer role, or simply an update, and let the donor choose.
- **Close the loop with one story and one number.** Pair a single human story with a single result and link both to the donor's gift.
- **Put proximity on the calendar.** Schedule regular opportunities to see the work in person and invite donors by name.
- **Convene a small group once a quarter.** Host a salon, a Jeffersonian dinner, or a giving circle gathering, and ask donors what they know and what they can give.

### "The Fitting" Is the Point

What people remember about a good tailor is the fitting: the attention, the adjustment, the sense that this was made with them in mind. Stewardship works the same way. The system keeps our promises on schedule, and the people inside it make each promise personal.

When we get this right, donors stop being a list we manage and become valued friends who walk alongside the mission. They are seen for who they are. They can give in ways that fit their lives. And they are sure, having stood close enough to the work to feel what their generosity made possible.

> ***Seen. Free. Sure. That is stewardship worth scaling.***
