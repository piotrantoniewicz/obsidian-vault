Nonprofits have adopted AI faster than almost any technology in the sector's history. Ninety-two percent of organizations now use it in some capacity.\[1\] And yet only 7 percent report a major improvement in what their fundraising program can actually accomplish.\[1\] That gap is the real story. It is not a story about whether AI works. It is a story about what fundraisers are asking it to do, and whether that is the right question.

I have spent 40 years building donor relationships across independent schools, healthcare, senior living, and national foundations. I have raised more than $100 million doing it. So I come at this question from a specific place: philanthropy is a human transaction of identity and meaning before it is a transaction of dollars. My Twelve Principles of Transformational Philanthropy start from that premise. Principle 4 holds that relationship is the infrastructure. Principle 5 holds that the ask is a moment, not a strategy. Principle 10 holds that donor motivation is intrinsic, not transactional. None of that changes because a large language model entered the development office. But something else has changed, and it is worth being precise about what.

### The gap is real, and it has a shape

The 2026 Nonprofit AI Adoption Report, released by Virtuous and [Fundraising.AI](http://fundraising.ai/), surveyed 346 nonprofits and found a sector that has adopted AI broadly but shallowly.\[1\] Seventy-nine percent report small to moderate efficiency gains. Eighty-one percent describe their use as ad hoc, run by individuals rather than embedded in team workflows. Forty-seven percent have no governance policy at all.\[1\] The picture is one person on staff using a chatbot to draft an appeal letter while the rest of the department still works the way it worked five years ago.

This is happening against a backdrop that makes the stakes higher than a productivity question. The Fundraising Effectiveness Project's Q4 2025 data shows overall giving dollars up 5 percent year over year, the strongest growth in five years, but donor counts down 3.6 percent, extending a decline that started in 2021.\[2\] The growth is coming from fewer, larger gifts. FEP's own analysis calls converting a first gift into a second the most consequential unsolved problem in the donor pipeline, since new donor retention has stayed essentially flat even as repeat donor retention slowly improves.\[2\] The sector is retaining the donors it already knows well and still has not cracked how to hold on to the donors it just met.

That is precisely the terrain where AI's proven strengths and philanthropy's core work either reinforce each other or collide.

### Where AI genuinely closes a capacity gap

Three uses have real evidence behind them, and none of them require pretending a model can build a relationship.

**Finding the right prospects faster.** CCS Fundraising uses predictive modeling combined with wealth screening to surface unassigned major gift prospects who match the affinity and capacity profile of an organization's best current donors, then filters by life stage and existing portfolio assignment so gift officers get a short, high-quality list instead of a spreadsheet of noise.\[3\] DonorSearch's predictive modeling clients include Life Teen International, which has consistently exceeded its five million dollar annual goal after rebuilding its screening approach.\[4\] This is research acceleration. It replaces hours of manual list-building, not the conversation that follows.

**Optimizing the portfolios gift officers already carry.** CCS also applies predictive scoring to existing major gift portfolios, evaluating both portfolio quality, whether the right donors are assigned, and portfolio performance, whether the available capacity is being captured, so leadership can reallocate an officer's limited time toward the prospects most likely to respond.\[5\] The scarce resource in any development shop is not data. It is a gift officer's calendar. AI's honest contribution here is protecting that calendar for the moments that matter.

**Personalizing stewardship at a scale humans cannot sustain manually.** AFP's own 2026 trend analysis is blunt that filling in a name field is not personalization anymore.\[6\] Real personalization means referencing a donor's specific last gift, connecting it to the program they said they cared about, and doing that consistently across a file of thousands rather than dozens. Allison Fine, president of [Every.org](http://every.org/), describes the emerging model well: a fundraiser logs into their CRM and finds a ranked list of donors ready to hear from them, paired with draft messages that reflect each donor's actual journey, so the fundraiser's time goes to engaging as a partner rather than assembling the briefing.\[7\]

In each case, the pattern is the same. AI compresses the research and drafting work that used to eat the hours a gift officer needed for the visit, the call, the handwritten note. That is not a small thing. It is also not a transformational thing. It is table stakes.

### Where the evidence says AI cannot go

Donors have already told us where the line is, and the data is more nuanced than either the enthusiasts or the skeptics usually admit.

The Donor Perceptions of AI study, an independent survey of 1,006 donors led by Nathan Chappell and Cherian Koshy, found only 16 percent of donors actively appreciate personalized AI-driven approaches, while 39.8 percent express discomfort about how their data might be used behind the scenes.\[8\] Transparency changes the calculation. Another 28.1 percent say they would accept AI-driven personalization if the organization is upfront about how it works.\[8\] Donors are not rejecting AI. They are rejecting AI they cannot see.

There is a biological reason a chatbot draft, however well personalized, cannot substitute for a real relationship. Paul Zak's research at Claremont Graduate University found that narratives following a real person through a real, unresolved tension trigger the release of oxytocin, which is directly linked to trust and giving behavior.\[9\] A face and a voice activate neural pathways that text alone does not reach.\[9\] This is not a preference donors could be talked out of. It is how trust gets built in the human brain, and no model output changes that mechanism.

The Donor Relations Group states the boundary in operational terms that I think every development office should post somewhere visible: AI can draft, it can outline, it can summarize, but it cannot feel gratitude, it cannot understand the nuance in a donor's lifetime relationship, and it cannot replace the relational capital built over years of stewardship.\[10\] Industry guidance across the field, including AFP's own 2026 recommendations, converges on the same instruction: AI augments human relationship-building, it does not run it, and organizations that let it "run" donor communications are the ones losing trust rather than earning it.\[10\]

### What this means for how we actually work

Read against my Twelve Principles, the research points somewhere specific rather than somewhere vague.

Principle 2 holds that moral biography precedes the gift. A predictive model can tell you who has the capacity and the giving pattern. It cannot tell you the story that will make a specific donor say yes, because that story lives in a life, not a database. Principle 6 holds that transparency builds the trust that sustains giving. The donor perception data says exactly this about AI specifically: disclosed use is tolerated and often accepted, undisclosed use breeds distrust.\[8\] Principle 12 holds that stewardship is a covenant, not a communication strategy. A covenant cannot be automated end to end, though the research and drafting that supports it can be.

The organizations the 2026 Adoption Report identifies as getting real impact from AI, rather than the 79 percent stuck at incremental gains, share one trait: they embedded AI into team-wide workflows tied to strategy, rather than leaving it to individual staff experimenting alone.\[1\] That distinction, structural integration versus scattered personal use, tracks almost exactly onto the distinction my framework draws between transactional and transformational practice. A tool used transactionally speeds up the wrong work. The same tool, deployed with intention around where the human belongs, protects the hours that make transformational philanthropy possible.

AI can close the capacity gap. It can find the right prospect faster, protect a gift officer's calendar, and personalize outreach at a scale no team could sustain by hand. It cannot close the relationship gap, and the data on donor trust and the neuroscience of giving both say we should stop expecting it to. The gap AI cannot close is not a limitation to engineer around. It is the actual point of the work.
