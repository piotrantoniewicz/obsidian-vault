## Change 2: Why More Emails Could Actually Hurt Your Nonprofit Fundraising Campaign in 2026 — And What to Do Instead

I know. This one is counterintuitive coming from *me*.

For years I have been the person telling nonprofit leaders to send more emails — because more emails, sent consistently to a warm audience, genuinely does produce more donations.

That is still true.

But here is what has changed in the current email marketing for nonprofits landscape: with the explosion of AI-generated content flooding inboxes everywhere, sending more emails has never been easier. Which means inboxes have never been more crowded. Which means the bar for what gets opened, read, and acted on has never been higher.

Emails that feel generic get deleted.

Emails that feel like they came from a real human being with something genuinely worth saying get opened — and they get replied to, forwarded, and donated to.

So the question for your nonprofit fundraising email strategy is no longer just how many emails. It is which emails, when, and in what sequence.

This year-end giving season we are experimenting with something we are calling the inverted bell curve approach — and here is what it looks like in practice:

- **The week of Giving Tuesday:** 4–5 emails — high frequency during the highest-urgency window when donor intent is strongest
- **The weeks of December 7, 14, and 21:** 1 email per week — giving donors breathing room and making each email feel like an event rather than noise
- **The week of December 28:** 4–5 emails — a final urgency push to close the nonprofit year-end fundraising campaign strong before December 31

![Black and white hand drawn inverted bell curve example](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c25449df8370ab0e18e201b/aed25fde-2ffe-4d43-823e-229426cea428/Inverted+Bell+Curve?format=2500w)

Black and white hand drawn inverted bell curve example

The total email volume across the thirty-day campaign period is roughly the same as what we have always sent. What changes is the distribution.

Here is why this matters psychologically for nonprofit donor cultivation: when donors receive four or five emails in the first week of a campaign, they engage or they do not — and then they expect that pace to continue. When the frequency drops in the middle weeks, each email that arrives carries more weight. It does not feel like another blast in a blizzard. It feels like something worth opening.

Then when the final push arrives in the last week of December, the urgency is real — and the donors who have been warming throughout the campaign are primed to act.

High quality over high volume. Intentional sequencing over relentless sending. Every email in your nonprofit year-end fundraising campaign earning its place in the inbox rather than taking it for granted.

That is the shift.

## Change 3: Match Donors Are No Longer Optional in Your Year-End Giving Campaign — They Are Required

For years I have *strongly* recommended that our clients secure a match donor before launching their nonprofit year-end fundraising campaign.

Because they work. Dramatically, consistently, and for a reason rooted deeply in donor psychology — a matched gift makes the donor feel like her contribution is worth twice as much, which makes the decision to give twice as easy.

This year we are no longer recommending match donors for nonprofit fundraising campaigns.

**We are requiring them.**

Here is what that looks like in practice:

Ideally, before your year-end giving campaign ever goes public, you have already raised 25% of your fundraising goal privately — through a match donor, a major donor, or a combination of both.

Which means if your nonprofit fundraising goal is $100,000, you walk into the campaign with $25,000 already secured before a single email goes out.

That money does two things simultaneously. It gives the campaign immediate credibility — donors can see that serious supporters are already behind this. And it creates urgency — because a matched gift has a deadline, and deadlines move people to act in a way that open-ended campaigns simply do not.

It can be a Giving Tuesday match donor.  
It can be a year-end match donor.  
It can be a match that supports both campaigns.

But it needs to exist before your nonprofit year-end fundraising campaign launches — which means the conversation with your potential match donor needs to happen now. Not in October. Not after Giving Tuesday. Now.
