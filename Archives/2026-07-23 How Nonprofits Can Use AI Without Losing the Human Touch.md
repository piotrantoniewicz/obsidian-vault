> Nonprofits can use AI safely by handing it the mundane work (first drafts, flyers, data cleanup, summaries) while keeping the human work human (real donor relationships, authentic stories, and final judgment). Treat AI as an exponent to your relationships, not a replacement for them.

Here is the question almost every development team is working through right now: how can nonprofits use AI without losing the human touch that makes their work matter? The answer is that you draw a clear line. Some tasks should be handed to AI today. Others should never leave human hands. Knowing the difference is the whole skill.

AI should be an exponent to the human relationship, not a replacement for it. Used well, it does not make your work less personal. It gives you back the hours to be more personal.

## How can nonprofits use AI without losing the human touch?

The simplest rule is this: automate the mundane, protect the meaningful. AI is excellent at the repetitive, low-judgment work that drains your week. It is dangerous when it stands in for the trust, voice, and relationships that donors actually give to.

You are not alone in figuring this out. Roughly eight in ten nonprofits report using AI in some form, but a 2024 study from the Center for Effective Philanthropy found that most use is still informal, concentrated in internal productivity (about 35 percent), marketing and communications (about 31 percent), and fundraising (about 24 percent). The opportunity is real. So is the need for a few simple guardrails, which we will get to.

## What should nonprofits hand off to AI?

Start with the tasks that are repetitive, low-stakes, and easy to check. These are the jobs where AI saves real time and a mistake is cheap to catch. The Nonprofit Communications Trends Report found the most common uses are exactly this kind of work: checking grammar and spelling (53 percent), brainstorming headlines and subject lines (53 percent), and drafting first versions of content (39 percent).

Good candidates to delegate today include:

- First drafts of emails, social posts, and appeals that you will edit before they go out.
- Design starting points like flyers and graphics, using your own brand colors, fonts, and logistics.
- Summarizing long meeting notes or a messy call into a few clear bullet points.
- Cleaning up and standardizing data, like fixing formatting before an import.
- Repurposing one piece of content into several, such as turning a newsletter into social posts.
- Brainstorming subject lines, headlines, and event names when you are stuck.

Notice the pattern. In every one of these, a human still makes the final call. AI gives you a faster starting line, not the finished product. ## What should nonprofits keep human?

Some work is the relationship, not a task around it. Hand these to AI and you will quietly erode the very trust your fundraising depends on.

Keep these in human hands:

- The actual donor relationship. The coffee, the call, the handwritten note. AI can remind you to do it and draft the first version, but it should not be the one doing it.
- Authentic words from donors and the people you serve. Never let AI rewrite, polish, or invent a quote. If a beneficiary said it, share it exactly as they said it.
- Impact claims and stories. Do not let a tool generate what your program achieved. Report what actually happened.
- Final judgment on anything a donor will read. Your name is on it, so your eyes should be the last ones on it.

This is where Jackie draws her hardest line, and she is right to. The authenticity of a girl's own voice is the product. A donor can feel the difference between a real story and a dressed-up one, even if they cannot name it. Protecting that voice is not a limitation on your AI use. It is the point of it.

## How do you set guardrails for ethical AI use?

Most of the risk in nonprofit AI comes from moving fast without a few simple rules. The gap is wide: TechSoup's 2025 State of AI in Nonprofits report found that 76 percent of nonprofits have no AI policy at all. Among staff already using these tools, a 2024 survey found 70 percent were concerned about data privacy and security, 63 percent about accuracy, and 57 percent about bias and fair representation, while 69 percent had received no formal AI training.

You can close most of that gap with a short, plain set of guardrails:

- Write a one-page AI policy. It does not need to be fancy. Name which tasks are allowed, which are off limits, and who reviews AI output before it ships.
- Protect donor data. Never paste private donor information, financials, or personal stories into a public AI tool that may use it for training.
- Keep a human in the loop. Treat every AI output as a draft that a person must read, fact-check, and approve.
- Be honest about impact. Never let AI invent a statistic, a quote, or an outcome. If you cannot verify it, do not publish it.
- Disclose when it matters. If AI played a meaningful role in something donors might assume was personal, lean toward transparency.

## What is a simple way to start this week?

Here is a starting sequence any growing nonprofit can run in a few days.

- Pick one mundane, recurring task that eats your time, like drafting your weekly social posts or formatting an import file.
- Use an AI tool to produce a first draft of that one task, then edit it the way you always would.
- Write your one-page AI policy while it is fresh, based on what felt safe and what did not.
- Share both the time you saved and the guardrails with your team, so adoption is intentional rather than accidental.

Done this way, AI earns trust task by task. You prove the value on something low-risk before it ever touches a donor relationship.

## Will AI replace fundraisers?

No, and the fear that it will often gets the relationship backward. Fundraising runs on trust, and trust is built between people. A donor does not give because an email was well written. They give because they believe in your mission and feel known by your organization. AI cannot manufacture that, and it should not try.

What AI can do is remove the quiet weight of administrative work that pulls fundraisers away from people in the first place. The goal is not fewer people. It is a team that spends its energy on relationships instead of formatting.
