### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | September 16, 2026

**AI can give people time back. But we should be careful not to take away the judgment that took them years to build.**

Ray is a job coach who has helped people find and succeed in competitive jobs for nearly two decades. Ask him what fills his day. Along with the work he enjoys, he’ll give a list of the “other” part of the job: paperwork, transportation logistics, employer check-ins, forms, schedules, and follow-up. But watch him work, and you’ll see one of the most important parts of his work that never appears in his job description.

A young man sorts inventory when he suddenly grows nervous, and his hands begin to shake. Ray notices. For maybe half a second, Ray has to decide: do I step in quickly, or give him a little more time to work through it himself?

**Ray waits.**

That pause is part of his job, too. In fact, it may be the most important part. Nobody taught it to him; it wasn’t in a training manual. Ray learned it over years of standing beside people, making mistakes, reading expressions, noticing patterns, and figuring out when to step in and when to step back so it doesn’t get in the way of people learning on their own. Some will call it instinct, which is understandable. I call it human judgment. As AI finds its way into more of our work, we need to pay much closer attention to human judgment.

### The Debt We Don’t Measure

In a May 2026 Harvard Business Review article, researcher Guy Champniss examined the psychological cost of adopting AI at work. He surveyed more than 1,200 full-time employees in the United States and the United Kingdom and found something that rarely appears in an AI business case. The technology may improve productivity while creating a different kind of cost for users. Champniss calls it psychological debt. It doesn’t appear on a balance sheet. You see it later: in motivation, confidence, collaboration, professional identity, and, eventually, burnout.

He identifies six forms of that debt, which are especially relevant to those of us working in the human services sector because our work depends so heavily on something technology can’t provide: a lived understanding of another person.

### When Assistance Becomes Substitution

![Treść artykułu](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQG3WPaPxhxb-A/article-inline_image-shrink_1000_1488/B56aCX2zJeHIAI-/0/1789254120338?e=1791417600&v=beta&t=4HhDEWCwmLQ_KGIMnemwTuBF_ENqeNgn1S6uJSvWTFg)

**Cognitive debt** begins when the tool starts thinking for us before we have done enough of the work ourselves. Imagine a family support specialist feeding notes into an AI tool and asking it to draft an intake summary before she has stopped to form her own impression of the conversation. The document may be good, but something important can still be lost if the act of writing the report was an important part of applying judgment.

**Autonomy debt** appears when the technology begins narrowing choices rather than supporting them. A schedule, recommendation, or care plan arrives looking finished. It is efficient, polished, and perhaps even statistically sensible. But the person closest to the work no longer feels like the author of the decision.

**Competency debt** matters deeply in our field. What happens when a direct support professional spends years becoming skilled at something, only to watch a machine produce an answer in five seconds? The question beneath it is: *Did all those years I spent learning how to do this still matter?* They matter enormously.But we shouldn’t assume everyone will automatically experience AI that way.

**Relatedness debt,** asChampniss describes it, is when people start turning to a tool instead of one another. That may sound minor until you consider how much learning in our field happens informally. You ask the veteran staff member what she thinks. You pull someone aside after a difficult interaction. You compare notes after a home visit. You say, “Something felt off in there. Did you notice it too?”

Those conversations are not inefficiencies. They are part of how people become good at their work and how we stay connected to one another.

**Credibility debt** is the awkwardness of wondering whether colleagues will think less of your work if they know AI helped produce it. Sometimes, when dealing with mounds of paperwork, AI is a helpful tool that can significantly lighten caseworkers' and administrative teams’ workloads. For someone like Ray, this means more time to coach and support the people in his employment program.

**Identity debt** may be the deepest. If someone chose this profession to become a skilled therapist, teacher, job coach, or direct support professional, what happens when new technology makes them feel less like one? That isn’t resistance to change. It is a legitimate question about professional identity that every organization must seriously consider and address.

### Pay Attention to the Newest People on Your Team

One finding in Champniss’s research should attract the attention of anyone responsible for managing talent. The overall psychological debt was highest among employees new to the workforce and lowest among those with twenty years of experience. At first, I thought he had it backward, but after further reflection, the pattern made sense.

Someone who has spent twenty years developing judgment has a much firmer sense of what they know. They can argue with the machine, ignore it, or use part of its answer and discard the rest. A person three years into a career is still figuring out what expertise feels like. Now, put an extraordinarily confident machine beside them, and you can imagine the self-doubt creeping in.

In direct care and social services, these are exactly the employees we are working so hard to recruit, develop, and retain. We should *not introduce* AI in a way that leaves a 25-year-old DSP wondering whether the technology is there to help her improve in her profession or to ultimately make her profession require less of her. Those are very different futures.

### The Better Question

None of this is an argument against AI. I believe these tools can eliminate enormous amounts of accumulated administrative work in the human services sector. Documentation, summarization, scheduling, searching, routine communication, and dozens of other tasks are ripe for improvement. If we can give people back hours in their day, we should. But the most important question we should ask is:

> ***What do we want our people to become better at because the technology is here?***

This is a very different design principle. Champniss points to organizations that are already thinking this way. One organization required employees to form their own hypothesis before consulting AI. Another created simple “nutrition labels” explaining what each model could and couldn’t do and where people should be cautious when using it.

Others built peer communities so employees learned from one another rather than receiving technology as another top-down mandate. Others had teams review AI-generated work together, turning the tool into a catalyst for more conversation rather than less. The common idea is simple: keep people actively engaged.

### What It Could Look Like for Your Team

It might mean introducing a documentation tool that explains what it sees and what it misses. It could mean asking a small group of DSPs to shape a scheduling tool before anyone considers scaling it. It could mean making room in team meetings to ask:

- *Where was AI useful this week?*
- *Where was it wrong?*
- *What did you notice that AI didn’t see?*
- *What did your lived experience tell you that AI couldn’t?*

Those may become some of the most valuable learning conversations your team has. Good AI adoption should not make people more dependent on it. It should help them become more capable without relying on it.

### Protecting the Pause

Former U.S. Surgeon General C. Everett Koop famously observed that medicines don’t work for patients who don’t take them. Technology has its own version of this truth. The most sophisticated AI system we deploy will accomplish very little if the people using it don’t trust it, understand it, or see how it helps them do the work they choose to do.

At AbilityPath, our 2030 framework calls us to build a community where every person, including those we serve and those who serve them, is valued, honored, and able to grow. This promise applies to technology, too.

We should use AI to eliminate work that doesn’t require a person, then reinvest that time in the work only people can do.

- *Listening.*
- *Noticing.*
- *Coaching.*
- *Building trust.*
- *Being present.*

Which brings me back to Ray. A young man’s hands begin to shake. Ray notices. For half a second, he waits. AI may someday see the shaking. It may know what happened the last ten times. It may offer a recommendation. Sometimes it may even be right.

> ***But Ray’s judgment came from building a relationship. This is the part we should never automate.***
