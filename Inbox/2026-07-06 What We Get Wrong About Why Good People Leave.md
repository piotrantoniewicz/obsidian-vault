### TEAMS! Newsletter | Talent Edition | July 6, 2026

Let me start with something every one of us has said, probably in the last month: “We just can’t compete with corporate wages.”

And here’s the thing. It’s true. The wage pressures we face in this sector are real. I won’t pretend otherwise, and I won’t insult anyone doing this work by suggesting that a heartfelt thank-you card pays the rent. Our direct support professionals are chronically underpaid for some of the hardest, most important work in our communities. That’s a fact, and we should keep fighting to change it.

But it’s not the whole story. Too often, we let it become the whole story. We point to the wage gap, shrug, and stop looking. It becomes the explanation that requires nothing of us, no hard questions about how we lead, how we structure teams, or whether we keep the promises we make on day one. That’s the comfortable answer. This edition is about the uncomfortable one: the hard introspection that can actually move the needle on retention, even when the budget can’t.

When you look at the organizations beating our sector’s roughly 50% average turnover rate, an inconvenient pattern emerges: the highest payers aren’t always the ones winning. People don’t walk away from hard, meaningful work just because the pay is modest. They walk away when we send them into intense care environments alone, under-led, and unsure whether anything we promised them will actually happen.

### Executive Summary

Our frontline turnover crisis is, in large part, a trust and belonging problem that we’ve been misdiagnosing as a compensation problem alone. Wages matter, but blaming them solely lets us avoid the leadership work that’s actually within our control. Here’s what that work looks like:

- **Belonging beats isolation.** Moving staff from solo shifts into small, named teams of 6–10 peers is the single most powerful, lowest-cost retention lever we have.
- **Supervisors are the engine.** First-line leads with a manageable span of 6–10 people and real management training drive team longevity more than almost anything else.
- **Kept promises compound.** Staff stay when the organization’s word is reliable, on schedules, on growth opportunities, and on the small stuff. Broken promises make leaving the rational choice.
- **Growth can’t require a goodbye.** A visible career lattice, clear tiers, credentials, and pay steps give people a way to advance without leaving to find one.

### The Five Pillars of Frontline Retention

**Build Care Pods, Not Solitary Shifts.** Here’s a pattern I’ve watched unfold over decades in both corporate and nonprofit settings: people quit isolated jobs, but they are remarkably slow to leave a tight-knit team that visibly depends on them. Yet our sector’s default staffing model, with solo shifts and rotating assignments, practically manufactures isolation.

The fix is structural and not expensive. Group staff into small, named teams of 6 to 10 peers who share a client roster and cover for one another. When someone has a brutal day and, in this work, brutal days come they aren’t absorbing it alone in a parking lot at 7 p.m. They have people to catch them. That’s not a perk. That’s a retention system.

**Professionalize the First-Line Lead.** We all know how this movie goes. Our best direct-support professional gets promoted to supervisor on Friday, inherits 20 direct reports on Monday, and receives exactly zero management training in the interim. Then we act surprised when their team churns.

I spent 30 years working at technology companies, and I can tell you that no serious organization would hand a first-time manager 20 people without support. We shouldn’t either. Cap the span of control at 6 to 12. Train for the role. And make the supervisor’s explicit, measurable job the growth and welfare of their team, not compliance paperwork. When supervisor quality goes up, longevity follows. Every time.

**Design a Cohort-Based Welcome, Not a Compliance Binder.** The first 90 days account for most of our turnover. An identity formed in week one prevents a resignation in month nine. This is where I keep returning to the “gardener’s approach”: you don’t yank on a seedling to make it grow faster; you build the conditions and cultivate patiently.

Too many of us treat Day 1 as a lonely march through binders and training videos. Flip it. Bring new hires in as a cohort. Give them a shared identity and shared milestones. Celebrate the 30-day mark out loud, in front of their colleagues. A witnessed moment of belonging anchors someone’s professional identity in a way no automated welcome email ever will.

**Run a Reliability Engine.** This is the pillar that demands the most honesty from us as leaders. When someone leaves for fifty cents more an hour, we call it a wage problem. But behavioral science, and frankly, common sense, says something different: people defer rewards and endure hard days when they trust the institution’s word. When growth opportunities are hinted at and then vanish, when schedules shift without warning, when small promises quietly break, leaving for fifty cents isn’t impulsive. It’s rational.

So make reliability a core strength: proactive stay interviews on a calendar, small promises kept visibly, and advancement triggered by objective milestones rather than a manager’s memory or mood. Every kept promise is a deposit. Every broken one costs more than we think.

**Establish a Clear Career Lattice.** If the only way to earn a meaningful raise or title in your organization is to leave the frontline for administration, you will lose your best practitioners, either to a desk they don’t want or to a competitor. Map clear, credential-anchored tiers with real pay steps, including technical tracks for people who want to go deeper, not just up. Retention, at its core, is giving someone a concrete way to say: “I can influence my future here.”

### One Size Fits No One: The Five Motivations

None of these pillars work if we treat our frontline staff as a monolith. Every person on our teams is doing emotional math that doesn’t always add up on a spreadsheet. Across the sector, most people fit one of five motivational profiles, and what they’re actually “buying” with their labor determines how you keep them:

***Each of these deserves a closer look:***

- **The Mission-Rooted Caregiver** sees this work as an identity, not a job. They’ll take on the hardest shifts and leave the moment they feel like an interchangeable cog. Meaning and direct recognition hold them; nothing else will.
- **The Springboarder** is often a student building hours toward graduate school, temporary by design, for 18 to 36 months. Don’t fight the departure; make the most of the tenure. Promise (and deliver) strong recommendation letters and real exposure, then celebrate their exit like a graduation. Done right, they become lifetime referral sources.
- **The Stability Provider** is focused on household survival, and yes, this profile is primarily held together by money and predictability. Guard their role reliability fiercely. Erratic expectations are the fastest route to losing them to a competitor offering fifty cents more.
- **The Second-Act Seeker.** They come to us from somewhere else, often from a corporate career, looking for work that means more to them personally. I know this profile well; I lived it. They bring deep experience, and they aren’t chasing a title or a raise. What they’re looking for is to make an impact and the autonomy to make a difference. Bury them in bureaucratic red tape and approval chains, and they’ll conclude they can find meaning somewhere with less friction. Give them room to run, and they become some of your most durable, valuable people.
- **The Community Bridge.** They often live in the neighborhoods we serve, share language and culture with our clients, and carry credibility no outside hire can replicate. What they’re seeking is a sense of local belonging and identity. Retain them by making that identity official: specialized titles, localized career pathways, and visible recognition that their community connection is an organizational asset. When you lose a Community Bridge, you lose a piece of your standing in that community.

### Putting It into Action: Your Week One Playbook

A full overhaul takes time, and like anything worth building, you’ll find that some approaches won’t work along the way. But you can start building institutional trust this week with these five concrete steps:

- **Pull your exit-curve data.** Map exactly when people leave. A cliff in the first 90 days means investing more in onboarding; a cluster at 12 months means your career pathways aren’t legible. Let the data pick your focus.
- **Audit your manager spans.** If any frontline lead is carrying an over-scoped roster, map a plan to split it into units of 6–12. This one change touches everything else.
- **Start 90-day stay conversations.** Don’t wait for the exit interview to learn the truth. Sit down with people at the 90-day mark and ask one direct question: “Has a promise we made you during hiring not been fulfilled?” Then honestly address what you hear.
- **Close hard weeks with a 15-minute reflection.** Four questions: What was supposed to happen? What actually happened? What went well? What will we do better? That small ritual builds psychological safety, keeping a hard day from becoming a path to burnout, isolation, and a “turnover” statistic, and helping teams bounce forward from setbacks rather than just bounce back.
- **Guard the human dividend against automation.** As AI takes on more of our administrative load, and it should, double down on face-to-face relational touchpoints. Never automate recognition or stay in conversations. An algorithmic “thank you” doesn’t just fall flat; it actively erodes trust.

### Closing Thoughts

Frontline human services will always be highly rewarding yet demanding work. That’s not the problem. Our people don’t walk away because the work is hard; they walk away when we make them carry the challenges alone and when we hide behind the wage gap instead of doing the leadership work in front of us.

Keep fighting for better wages. That fight matters. But don’t let it become the excuse that keeps us from looking in the mirror. When we ground our organizations in small-team belonging, professionalized leadership, and a word that’s reliably good, we stop fighting a wage war we can’t win and start building places where people make it their mission.
