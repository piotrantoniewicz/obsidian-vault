Bill Gates’ AI manifesto has received a great deal of attention. The founder of Microsoft points out some of the fantastical opportunities and existential threats AI poses. He emphasizes the need for new national and international institutions to ensure humanity reaps the benefits and avoids the real potential for catastrophe.

Rightly, Mr. Gates notes that we cannot leave the future of AI to technology executives. But he also lets business leaders off the hook when he describes the new structures we need. Leaders have agency, today, to decide whether AI will make the lives of their employees and the communities in which they operate better or worse. And these decisions do not need to wait for new institutions, frameworks, and rules.

Leaders could build AI today around a culture where every significant business decision is tested against the company’s stated values, with AI examining how a decision advances or undermines them, and suggesting modifications before the decision is finalized.

What if individual leaders defined their own values, and used AI to figure out how to execute business decisions, from hiring and firing to benefits to capital investments, in ways consistent with those values?

What if AI gave executives, who are often far removed from the daily lives of front-line employees, a clear picture of how a C-suite decision lands on the people and communities that give a company its license to operate, along with options that support both employees and financial results?

Chicago could be the test kitchen for this model. New York may be the city that never sleeps and Silicon Valley the place where ideas scale; Chicago can be where capitalism grows up. Its tradition of civic involvement by the business community makes our city the natural city to test whether AI can deepen the integration of business and community.

A credible model must show that employers can expand opportunity and grow; that community impact and business performance reinforce each other; that capitalism works better when communities do the same.

Imagine Chicago employers and investors using AI to ask, before a layoff, benefits change, capital investment, or site decision: Who absorbs the cost? Which neighborhoods gain or lose opportunity? What alternative protects long-term business performance while reducing human harm?

None of this is science fiction. These are just use cases, and companies choose their use cases.

AI will scale whatever culture, and whatever version of capitalism, we hand it. Hand it the layoff, and it will perfect the mass layoff. But hand it a culture where decisions answer to values, and it could build business practices far more valuable. Now that’s an AI — and a capitalism — to be excited about.
