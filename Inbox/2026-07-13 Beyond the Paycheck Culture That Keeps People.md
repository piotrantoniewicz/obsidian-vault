### TEAMS! NEWSLETTER | TALENT EDITION | July 13, 2026

A few editions back, in “The Paycheck Excuse,” we pushed back on the comfortable, incomplete belief that frontline turnover is fundamentally a compensation problem, solvable if we can just find the budget for one more raise. I recently read four pieces of research on this topic from *CQL*, *The Guild for Human Services, Relias*, *Mind Share Partners*, and the *ASU Lodestar Center*. Read together, they tell a more complicated and ultimately more hopeful story. Pay matters. It is nowhere near the whole answer.

Here is the challenge every one of us in human services faces right now: we hire people who believe deeply in the mission, then quietly ask that belief to substitute for an environment that sustains their passion. Nationally, half of direct support professionals say they have left a job because of a supervisor, not because of the work itself or the population they serve. At AbilityPath, our DSPs, early intervention paraprofessionals, pediatric therapists, and classroom staff face the same daily challenges: crisis calls, behavior plans, families running on empty. While our retention rate is much better than the national average, we are not exempt from that math. We still face turnover rates that impact our work. Wages fix the top of the funnel. However, they do very little for the middle, where a stretched supervisor, a missing career ladder, or an unspoken permission to be human quietly decides who stays and who walks.

> ***As Kelly Greenwood, founder of the workplace mental health nonprofit Mind Share Partners, has put it, the cost of looking away shows up “in dollars and cents and, more importantly, in people’s lives.”***

### Executive Summary

- **Supervision, not salary, is the biggest lever leaders control.** Half of frontline staff nationally have quit over a supervisor, not the mission, the work, or the pay.
- **Staff development is an investment in outcomes, not a compliance checkbox.** Ongoing, practice-oriented learning builds confidence and directly improves care quality. It should be viewed as an investment, not as a requirement to be tolerated.
- **The career ladder is missing where it matters most.** Nearly a third of organizations offer no career advancement program to DSPs at all, even though real advancement, not recognition, is what staff say they want.
- **Passion is not a substitute for permission to be human.** Staff serving vulnerable populations routinely minimize their own needs; the culture must explicitly grant permission to share those challenges and to listen to them.
- **Culture is built, and it starts at the top.** Leadership behavior measurably affects staff health and satisfaction; the organization chart is a culture document, whether we treat it that way or not.

### The Five Pillars

**The Supervisor Effect.** CQL’s research and the national DSP Survey Report point to the same lever: the single-supervisor relationship determines whether a frontline employee stays, grows, or leaves. That is not a soft finding; it is the difference between a program that retains institutional knowledge and one that is perpetually retraining. For AbilityPath, this means treating supervisor capability as a program investment, not an HR afterthought: coaching, feedback, and mental-health-conversation skills for every program manager across preschool, therapy, EI, and adult services, recognizing that a good supervisor is the most cost-effective retention tool we have.

**Development as Investment, Not Compliance.** Carli Friedman’s work for CQL makes a case that’s easy to nod along to and hard to build toward: ongoing staff development isn’t a mandatory training box to check; it’s a strategic investment in both service outcomes and staff identity. The findings link continuous development directly to greater confidence, self-efficacy, and the health and safety of the people we serve. In practice, that argues for tiered learning pathways shared across all services, with foundational modules on trauma-informed care and communication, followed by role-specific advanced tracks, paired with peer practice labs where staff from different programs work through difficult cases together. None of us gets this right on the first try. Keep working on it until you find what works. A learning culture has to leave room for staff to do the same.

**The Career Ladder.** The Guild for Human Services and Relias surveyed DSPs directly and heard something leaders too often assume rather than ask: staff want real advancement, not just recognition. Yet nationally, nearly a third of organizations offer no career advancement program at all. The fix isn’t complicated. Co-design ladders that let people move between services (DSP to lead DSP to program coordinator to assistant director, for example), paired with paid certificates or degree pathways. When staff can see the next rung, the job stops being a placeholder and becomes a career.

**Permission to Be Human.** Mind Share Partners identifies a dynamic that will be uncomfortably familiar to anyone in this sector: staff who serve vulnerable populations often compare their struggles with those of the people they support and quietly conclude they should “tough it out.” The fix is not turning supervisors into therapists; it’s training them to recognize distress, respond without over-functioning, and know when and how to refer. For AbilityPath, where our EI teams, preschool teachers, therapists, and DSPs face daily exposure to families in crisis, this means normalizing self-care and boundary-setting through leadership storytelling, explicit encouragement of PTO, and manager training on trauma exposure, so staff hear, in plain terms, that their well-being is not in competition with the mission.

**Leadership as Culture Architect.** The ASU Lodestar Center makes the sharpest case for this argument: leadership behavior is not a soft variable; it shows up in people’s health. Top performers rarely leave over a single pay review; they leave because managers never taught them how to grow, or because organizations have a ceiling on growth opportunities. With multiple service lines and professional identities under one roof, our job as leaders is to prevent “program islands” through open communication, collaborative decision-making, explicit recognition tied to mission impact, town halls, structured feedback loops, and shared governance, where staff from every program see us model the transparency we’re asking of them.

### The Action Playbook:

- ***Upgrade supervisor training.*** Build or require modules for coaching, feedback, and mental health conversations for every program manager. This is the single highest-leverage retention investment available.
- ***Replace one-off compliance training with a learning pathway.***Foundational modules for everyone, role-specific advanced tracks by program, and recurring peer practice circles where staff work through real issues together.
- ***Co-design a cross-program career ladder.***Involve frontline staff in the design process and, wherever possible, link the ladder to a paid certificate or degree pathway.
- ***Train managers to recognize distress without becoming clinicians.***Give them the skills and explicit permission to normalize self-care, encourage PTO, and refer rather than diagnose.
- ***Open a structured, recurring feedback loop.***A town hall, survey, or shared forum that staff can count on to listen and share.

**Say the connection out loud.** Tie every one of the above explicitly to mission language, so investment in staff reads as investment in the people we serve, not overhead.

### The Close

None of this is a single fix, and we won’t get it all right on the first try. That’s fine. Bouncing forward has always been part of how we build things that last, in the same patient, tend-it-daily way many of us practice for our own growth. Culture works the same way. What we can’t keep doing is asking passion to cover for the environment we haven’t built yet.

As we build toward AbilityPath 2030 and the future we’re shaping, centered on innovation, adaptability, and lifelong growth, the people doing that work every day are the plan and the heart of our strategy. In the words of Frances Hesselbein, the legendary nonprofit leader,

> ***“Organizations exist to make people’s strengths effective and their weaknesses irrelevant.”***

**That’s the job. Let’s build the culture that does it.**
