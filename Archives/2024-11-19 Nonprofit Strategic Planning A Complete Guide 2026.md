Nonprofit strategic planning is the process of creating a documented blueprint that guides your organization toward its mission-related goals over a defined time period, typically three to five years. Done well, a [strategic plan](https://bloomerang.com/nonprofit-glossary/strategic-plan) helps your nonprofit raise more money, allocate resources effectively, and respond to change with confidence.

That confidence matters more than ever: [48 percent of nonprofit leaders reported a worse outlook](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/nonprofit-leaders-reported-growing-uncertainty-2024-early-2025) heading into 2025 compared to the year before, according to the Urban Institute's National Survey of Nonprofit Trends and Impacts — while only 19 percent saw things improving.

Organizations with a [**strategic plan**](https://kindful.com/nonprofit-glossary/strategic-plan/) report significant advantages: one survey found that [**86 percent of respondents**](https://www.nonprofitpro.com/post/how-having-a-strategic-plan-will-help-you-raise-significantly-more-money/) believed their strategic plan positively impacted revenue generation through grants, donors, events, and other avenues.

Whether you’re [**starting a new nonprofit**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/how-to-start-a-nonprofit), crafting an emergency plan to react to unexpected external circumstances, or creating your strategy for the next five years, this guide will help your nonprofit get the most out of its strategic plan. ## What is nonprofit strategic planning?

**Nonprofit strategic planning** is a structured process where organizational leaders identify three to five priority goals, define the strategies to reach them, and assign clear ownership to ensure accountability. The result is a living document — a strategic plan — that your board and staff use to make consistent, mission-aligned decisions.

A good strategic plan answers three core questions:

1. Where are we now?
2. Where do we want to go?
3. How will we get there?

### How often should you update a nonprofit strategic plan?

Most nonprofit boards and executive directors revisit and rewrite their strategic plan **every three to five years**. Between major rewrites, the plan should function as a living document, reviewed quarterly or annually to reflect new opportunities, challenges, or organizational changes.

### 3 common misconceptions about nonprofit strategic planning

Many nonprofits hesitate to invest in strategic planning. Here are the three most common misconceptions — and why they're wrong:

1. **"We shouldn't spend money on planning — we should focus on saving it."** Strategic investments in tools, consultants, and planning processes directly improve your fundraising return on investment. You must spend money wisely to raise more money effectively.
2. **"Nonprofits shouldn't take risks."** Calculated risks fuel innovation. Organizations that experiment with new fundraising strategies, programs, and outreach methods are better positioned to grow their mission impact.
3. **"Our board should lead the strategic planning process."** The board's role is to *oversee* organizational strategy, not to *set* fundraising priorities. Staff leaders, with input from stakeholders, are better positioned to drive the planning process.

### How do you begin the nonprofit strategic planning process?

You’ll start by discussing measurable objectives for your team to reach and drafting the priorities for each of these objectives. You may begin with a SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis. This exercise can help you define goals and consider which strategic planning model will best work for your organization.

## 5 types of nonprofit strategic planning models

Different circumstances and goals will require different types of nonprofit strategic plans. Choose a strategic planning model based on your nonprofit’s current circumstances, opportunities and threats.

Carefully examine the following strategic model plans to decide which one will best suit your nonprofit’s needs:

### 1\. Standard (goals-based) strategic planning model

**Ideal for:** Stable organizations in calm external environments

The standard strategic planning model, also known as the basic planning model, vision-based model, goals-based model or conventional model, is the most common nonprofit strategic planning model.

It’s best to use this model when your organization’s external surroundings are generally calm. When you start using this model, the economy is probably stable, your community and country are at peace and your organization is well-established in the community.

#### How it works:

- Define your organization’s mission and goals.
- Set specific, short-term goals you would like to reach to get you closer to those larger goals.
- Create a clear plan to reach short-term goals, including who is responsible for each goal’s success.
- Write these actions down and create a timeline to complete each one.

**Example**: Let’s say your organization is an animal shelter. In the next year, your specific short-term goal is to increase the shelter’s capacity by 50 animals and invest in the materials to do so.

To do this, your organization will need to increase fundraising revenue by $10,000 by finding new outreach opportunities and strengthening relationships with existing supporters to increase donor retention.

You may decide to ask one team member to post to social media every day to [**engage your online audience**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-engagement). Meanwhile, you may assign another person to call new donors to thank them and [**increase your new donor retention**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/10-ways-to-find-new-donors-for-your-nonprofit-organization).

This model is the most common because the climate in which your organization resides is, more often than not, fairly stable. When it does become unstable, that means it’s time to switch to a different model.

### 2\. Issues-based strategic planning model

**Ideal for:** Organizations experiencing internal turbulence

An issues-based strategic planning model can be used when your organization’s internal operations are in more turbulent conditions. For example, if you’re undergoing frequent staff turnover, a change in leadership or are understaffed, you may find an issues-based strategic planning model to be the best choice.

This nonprofit strategic planning model helps organizations get back on track if they have strayed from the path to success.

#### How it works:

- Brainstorm the elements that are holding your organization back from success.
- Decide how to address each of those elements to get your organization back on track.
- Carefully monitor your progress and adjust the strategy accordingly.

**Example**: Your nonprofit has limited staff and struggles to increase fundraising revenue. You may decide to address this by working with an external fundraising consultant or directing your staff’s attention to building relationships with your most engaged donors who are likely to increase their giving amounts.

An issues-based nonprofit strategic planning model is a living plan. Instead of setting it in stone, set check-in milestones and make adjustments based on your progress and results.

### 3\. Organic (nonlinear) strategic planning model

**Ideal for:** Organizations facing uncertain or rapidly changing external conditions

The organic or nonlinear nonprofit strategic planning model is best when there are uncertain external factors that threaten your nonprofit’s situation.

Using this model, your team members will come together to solidify their understanding of the organization’s mission and goals. Each person then comes up with actionable next steps to help get closer to that goal by the next time the group meets.

#### How it works:

- You and your team members go on a retreat to unify your understanding of the organization’s big-picture goals.
- Each team member examines their own strengths and decides on an actionable goal they can achieve based on that strength by a certain date.
- The team meets together again either quarterly or annually (or as frequently as you’d like) to discuss your progress toward each goal and mission impact.

**Example:** A staff member named Theo excels at face-to-face communication. After the retreat, he sets a goal to use your [donor database](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-database) to cultivate major donor relationships and grow major giving by 10% within the year. His goal fits his strengths and supports the organization's broader objectives.

This model never looks the same for two organizations. Each team member has inherent strengths, so this model is designed to help your nonprofit make the most of your unique strengths.

### 4\. Real-time strategic planning model

**Ideal for:** Nonprofits in active crisis

The real-time nonprofit strategic planning model is useful when your nonprofit is in the midst of a crisis, like an economic recession or national/global catastrophe. The situation could also be limited to your organization. For instance, you might have been the victim of a cyberattack or your headquarters might have been severely damaged in a tornado.

This model relies on an extreme focus on short-term goals that aim to help you weather the storm. Your nonprofit staff members might meet as frequently as every week to discuss your progress toward these short-term goals.

#### How it works:

- Your organization frequently meets as a large group to define short-term objectives for individual team members.
- In these team meetings, you discuss whether you’ve met these goals, your day-to-day progress and any roadblocks your organization members face.
- After the crisis period, your organization takes inventory of the progress made or damage done, thanks team members for their hard work and creates a new strategic plan using a different model.

**Example**: You discover that your nonprofit was the victim of a cyberattack that potentially left some donors’ information at risk. In response, you meet with your team to define and align on urgent next steps.

You assign several team members to assess the extent of the attack and summarize their findings into a clear report. Then, you designate other team members to notify impacted donors as quickly as possible about the breach. You outline the steps you’ll take to keep donors’ information more secure in the future and prevent future attacks.

As you can see, these goals are small and manageable in a short time. Goals are created as responses to the direct impact that external forces have on your organization’s internal operations.

### 5\. Alignment strategic planning model

**Ideal for:** Organizations with strong individual departments but poor cross-team communication

### How it works:

- Your team members meet to learn about the issues each individual faces in their position.
- You re-establish the common mission that everyone on your team is working towards.
- You outline tweaks that your team can use to improve internal communication processes.

**Example:** Your nonprofit has an excellent grant writer, a skilled major gift officer, and capable fundraisers—but they rarely communicate. The alignment model creates a shared fundraising goal and regular cross-department check-ins with the executive director to ensure everyone is working in the same direction.

The alignment nonprofit strategic planning model is a great way to set new communication standards and processes to incorporate moving forward as a team.

## 6 steps of strategic planning for nonprofits

Use these steps to launch the strategic planning process:

### 1\. Set fundraising targets.

We recommend starting strategic planning by envisioning the end result you want to achieve. What will your nonprofit look like when you’ve achieved your goals? How will your ability to achieve your mission evolve over time?

Use your [**organization’s budget**](https://www.jitasagroup.com/jitasa_nonprofit_blog/nonprofit-budgeting-understand-the-basics/) to determine how much you need to raise to achieve your philanthropic goals. Then, outline the strategies you’ll use to acquire that funding.

For example, you may decide to raise:

- 50% of funding from individual contributions to your annual fund
- 20% of funding from your [**planned giving**](https://www.qgiv.com/blog/nonprofit-needs-planned-giving-program/), legacy, and [**endowment**](https://www.qgiv.com/blog/nonprofit-endowment/) programs
- 20% of funding from corporate giving programs
- 10% of funding from grants

Every nonprofit will have a different fundraising goal breakdown based on its current fundraising initiatives and donors’ giving capacities.

### 2\. Get input from key stakeholders.

Next, ask your stakeholders for input on your fundraising goals. Provide context for your goals and philanthropic objectives, explain how each will impact your mission, and ask for feedback about the plan.

The different stakeholders you should reach out to include:

- Board members
- Staff members
- Key corporate and community partners
- A fundraising consultant

Running the plan by everyone will ensure your goals are achievable and manageable for your team. Stakeholders might raise the alarm if your fundraising amount is drastically different from last year’s or if you’re relying on strategies that have been ineffective in the past.

For example, let’s say your nonprofit has a good track record with grant writing. You’ve won 80% of the grants you’ve applied for, so you decide to dedicate a large portion of your fundraising revenue plan to be raised using grant money. Seems reasonable, right?

Well, your grant writer may bring up the valid point that you’ve only been that successful because you’re incredibly picky about the type of grant you apply for. While the percentage looks impressive, there aren’t enough grants out there to meet the goal you’ve set. This insight enables you to switch up your strategy to be as successful as possible.

### 3\. Determine your key fundraising and marketing strategies.

You may consider doing a SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) analysis of your past fundraising to better understand what areas you’re already strong in and what could be improved.

This chart can help you outline your strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats clearly and concisely:

![This image shows a breakdown of a nonprofit SWOT analysis, which you can use during your strategic planning process. ](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6996de43eb5bbc35466bf590/6a0329a635da8d3b243ac058_Nonprofit-Strategic-Planning-SWOT-copy.png)

This image shows a breakdown of a nonprofit SWOT analysis, which you can use during your strategic planning process.

Some of the fundraising and marketing strategies your organization should take into consideration include:

- [**Major gift fundraising**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/top-strategies-to-kickstart-major-gift-fundraising)**.** Major gift fundraising likely makes up a large portion of your proceeds. If it doesn’t already, this is one area of your strategy you’ll want to improve.
- [**Donor-centric stewardship**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-stewardship)**.** Donor stewardship leads to better retention rates and better fundraising results down the line. This ties in well with your marketing strategy and ensures you’re communicating regularly and effectively with donors.
- [**New donor acquisition and retention**](https://bloomerang.com/retention)**.** The second donation a donor gives is the “golden donation” because most donors lapse after the first gift. After the “golden donation,” there’s about a 60% chance they’ll give again.
- [**Online fundraising**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/online-fundraising)**.** While more revenue probably comes from in-person conversations with major donors, most of your donors probably prefer to give online. It’s convenient so long as your fundraising page is well-optimized.
- [**Peer-to-peer fundraising**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/peer-to-peer-fundraising)**.** Peer-to-peer fundraising is a great strategy to attract new donors while raising additional funds from your committed supporters. Leverage the power of your social networks to raise funds using this avenue.
- [**Monthly giving**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/monthly-giving)**.** Recurring gifts are essential because they’re a consistent source of revenue. If someone sets up a recurring gift, you can probably count on that gift being given for an extended period and account for that in your future budget.

Assign areas that need improvement a lower fundraising target than the aspects of your strategy where you already know you’re strong. This way, you can try out different strategies to make these elements stronger without as much pressure.

For example, if you know your nonprofit has an incredible major giving program, specify that a larger portion of your fundraising will likely come from this avenue. If you know that you could use improvement on your new donor retention rates, you might set that at a lower goal and use this as an opportunity to try out new things like [**calling new donors**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/actually-calling-donors-to-thank-them-does-make-them-more-likely-to-give-again-and-give-more) and setting up a [**welcome email series**](https://www.qgiv.com/blog/email-welcome-series-with-templates/).

### 4\. Establish SMART objectives.

When you set your fundraising goals, make sure they’re SMART:

- **Specific:** Target a specific area for improvement
- **Measurable:** Quantifiable
- **Attainable:** Achievable based on your past successes
- **Realistic:** Reasonable based on your available resources
- **Time-based:** Aligned with a specific time frame

For example, let’s say you have a specific goal for increasing individual contributions to your annual fund.

Here are a few examples of SMART goals that target this objective:

- Acquire 500 new donors through your online fundraising page within a year.
- Call 100% of the new donors who give within 90 days of their gift.
- Acquire 200 new donors through a 2-week peer-to-peer fundraising campaign.
- Expand your monthly giving program by 100 donors within a year.
- Retain 70% of donors from last year.

These goals include specific numbers and time frames to help orient your strategic planning around quantifiable metrics.

### 5\. Assign tactics and team responsibilities.

Use reporting tools in your [**donor management system**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-management-software) and marketing platforms to help keep your team accountable for reaching your goals.

Assign each team member an individual role that they’re responsible for. Here’s an example of a chart that clearly defines each activity, the staff member accountable for the task and the deadline:

![This image shows how to plan out activities that support your nonprofit strategic plan and assign staff members to complete each task. ](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6996de43eb5bbc35466bf590/6a0329a635da8d3b243ac07b_Nonprofit-Strategic-Planning-Activities-copy.png)

This image shows how to plan out activities that support your nonprofit strategic plan and assign staff members to complete each task.

When everyone is clearly aware of their role at the organization and how their actions will impact the mission at large, you’ll make sure everything gets done. Plus, everyone will have a sense of purpose as a part of the team.

In addition to assigning team member roles, you can also automate certain processes to free up more staff time. For example, if you used to send out the monthly newsletter manually or personally manage every social media post, consider investing in new marketing software as part of your strategic plan.

Ask yourself some of the following questions.

- What areas of our work do we need more time for?
- What can we automate?
- Who at our organization has repetitive tasks that take time away from more important activities?

Sometimes the answer to these questions leads you to invest in new nonprofit software like a [**new CRM**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/nonprofit-crm) to automate donor engagement efforts or [**volunteer management tools**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/volunteer-management-software) to streamline volunteer scheduling.

### 6\. Measure your success.

Track key metrics relevant to your goals to assess the success of your strategic plan. These key performance indicators may include:

- [**Donor retention**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-retention)
- New donor acquisition
- Monthly giving program participation
- Return on investment (ROI) for every fundraising tactic
- Cost per dollar raised
- Average gift size

Evaluate these metrics as a team at regular intervals, such as monthly or quarterly, to mark progress toward your goals. Identify performance decreases, investigate root causes, and adjust your strategic plan as needed.

Remember the SWOT analysis process as you evaluate your fundraising results, and keep in mind that not every threat is entirely within your nonprofit’s control. For example, a decrease in average gift size could be due to an economic downturn. However, there are measures your organization can put in place to mitigate threats like these, such as diversifying your revenue sources or [**focusing more on donor stewardship**](https://bloomerang.com/blog/donor-stewardship).

## Nonprofit strategic planning FAQs

### What is the difference between a nonprofit strategic plan and a fundraising plan?

A strategic plan sets the organization's overall mission-aligned goals across all departments for three to five years. A fundraising plan is a subset of the strategic plan that focuses specifically on how your organization will raise the revenue needed to achieve those goals. Your [fundraising plan](https://bloomerang.com/blog/the-essential-guide-to-writing-a-fundraising-plan) should be directly informed by your strategic priorities.

### Who should be involved in nonprofit strategic planning?

The process should involve staff leadership (executive director, program directors, development director), board members in an oversight capacity, key community and corporate partners, and optionally an external fundraising consultant. Frontline staff who implement programs often provide the most grounded feedback on what's achievable.

### How long does the nonprofit strategic planning process take?

Most organizations take **three to six months** to complete a full strategic planning process, from initial stakeholder input gathering through final board approval. Smaller organizations with simpler structures may complete the process faster; larger or more complex organizations may take longer.

### What is a SWOT analysis in nonprofit strategic planning?

A SWOT analysis is a structured exercise that examines your organization's internal **Strengths** and **Weaknesses** alongside external **Opportunities** and **Threats**. Nonprofits use SWOT analysis during strategic planning to identify where to invest resources, where to course-correct, and what external risks to plan for. It is typically completed before setting strategic goals.

### How do you measure the success of a nonprofit strategic plan?

Track KPIs aligned to your SMART goals at regular intervals — monthly or quarterly. Common metrics include donor retention rate, new donor acquisition, average gift size, cost per dollar raised, monthly giving participation, and program impact data. If a metric declines, investigate root causes before adjusting strategy.

### What is the most common nonprofit strategic planning model?

The standard (goals-based) model is the most widely used because most nonprofits operate in relatively stable environments most of the time. However, organizations should be prepared to shift to an issues-based or real-time model when internal or external disruptions occur.

### Can a small nonprofit benefit from strategic planning?

Yes. Even a simple one-page strategic plan with three clear goals, assigned owners, and a 12-month timeline provides enormous value for small nonprofits. Strategic planning helps small teams prioritize limited resources, avoid reactive decision-making, and communicate direction clearly to donors and board members.
