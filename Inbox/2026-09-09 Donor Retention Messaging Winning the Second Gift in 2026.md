You already know retention beats acquisition. You have probably sharpened your donor retention messaging, too. Maybe you send the thank-you faster, make it warmer, add a handwritten note, pick up the phone, or share a video from your program director.

Those are smart moves, and they deserve plenty of credit. But the data suggests that the message itself may need your attention, too.

In [The Giving Signals Report](https://bloomerang.com/resources/giving-signals-report-2026), Bloomerang’s research study fielded in March 2026, feeling like part of something ranked as a top-three motivator for 46% of donors considering a first gift. Among donors deciding whether to give again to an organization they had already supported, that number dropped to 28%.

The donor may be the same, but the decision has changed. The pull of belonging drops by nearly half.

**A note on the data:** This piece also draws from a deeper analysis of the same Giving Signals Report survey, with donor responses broken out by region, household income, and gender. Those figures go beyond what appears in the published report. You can find more information about that additional analysis in the Sources section at the end.

Belonging is one of the most powerful acquisition signals in the data and one of the clearest [drivers of a first gift](https://bloomerang.com/blog/what-donors-are-actually-telling-you-giving-motivations-from-the-2026-giving-signals-report). It is also the signal that fades fastest once someone has already given.

That creates an opportunity for your next appeal. When your second, third, and fourth appeals keep leading with “join us,” “be part of something,” and “you’re one of us,” they keep answering a question the donor may have already settled. Your retention message can move the relationship forward by showing donors what their generosity made possible.

## Key findings from the Giving Signals Report 2026

- Belonging drives 46% of first-time gifts but only 28% of repeat gifts, per the Giving Signals Report. It is the top acquisition signal and also the fastest-fading one.
- When tested head-to-head, donors chose a specific dollar-amount appeal over a generic one by 88 percentage points (94% to 6%).
- High-capacity donors, women, and Midwest donors respond to different kinds of proof, which means one retention message may not resonate equally across your entire donor file.
- Trust determines whether a renewal appeal gets believed at all. The highest-trust region also has the highest share of donors planning to give more this year.

## What should a donor retention message say instead of belonging?

Once a donor has given, their question can shift from “Do I want to be part of this?” to “What did my last gift make possible?”

The data gives fundraisers a clear answer. Nearly every donor, 94%, says they are motivated to give when an organization tells them exactly where their money will go (Giving Signals Report, 2026). Another 90% say they are motivated when they are told the impact of their donation.

When donors were asked what most clearly signals that a nonprofit is effective, they put transparency at the top. Sixty-nine percent chose clear reporting on finances and programs, while 58% chose impact data and statistics.

For retention messaging, specificity earns its place front and center.

The report put two versions of the same appeal in front of donors:

| Appeal version | What donors read |
| --- | --- |
| Generic appeal | “Your gift today helps us continue our vital work serving families in need throughout the community. Every dollar makes a difference.” |
| Specific appeal | “$50 buys a week of groceries for a family in our pantry program. $200 keeps the lights on at our family resource center for a day. Where would you like your gift to go?” |

Donors chose the specific version by 88 percentage points, 94% to 6% (Giving Signals Report, 2026).

The generic version asks donors to trust that their gift matters. The specific version gives them something concrete to believe in. It connects their generosity directly to an outcome they can picture.

For a donor considering another gift, that connection can be powerful. Instead of asking them to rediscover why they joined your community, show them what happened because they did.

## Why doesn’t what nonprofits send match what keeps donors giving?

The report also asked nonprofit leaders what they prioritize most heavily in donor communications, and their answers reveal an important gap.

Mission and organizational needs came first at 52%. Belonging to a community came second at 49%. Recent impact reports, which provide the closed-loop proof repeat donors say they want, landed at 41%.

That gap matters because a message that helps inspire the first gift may have a different job once the donor relationship is underway. After someone gives, your communications have an opportunity to connect their generosity to visible progress.

Fundraisers have good reason to emphasize belonging. The data shows that it works. But retention gives you the chance to build on that sense of connection with something more tangible.

[Building the fuller retention journey](https://bloomerang.com/blog/first-time-donor-retention-strategy/) means giving impact reporting a meaningful role throughout the relationship instead of saving it for the annual report.

## How does donor proof preference differ by income, gender, and region?

A deeper analysis of the Giving Signals data (Giving Signals Report, additional segment analysis, 2026) breaks donor responses out by region, household income, and gender instead of treating donors as one group.

The takeaway is useful for fundraisers. Donors broadly want proof, but different donors place different value on different kinds of proof.

### High-capacity donors validate before they give.

Households earning $150K or more are considerably more motivated by third-party ratings such as Charity Navigator and CharityWatch than lower-income donors, at 41% compared with 29%. They are also notably less motivated by the argument that the government is not helping enough, at 68% compared with 80–86% for every other income band.

Those differences point to a more evidence-forward approach for major-donor retention. Financial transparency, independent validation, and measurable results can give these donors more of the information they use to make giving decisions.

That opportunity matters because these donors are also the most likely to say they plan to increase their giving this year. Sixty-one percent plan to give more, compared with 42% of households earning less than $50K.

### Women and men take different roads to the same trust.

Women are more motivated by several proof-of-impact signals. They are more likely than men to cite transparency as a signal of effectiveness, at 74% compared with 65%. They also respond more strongly to matching gifts, at 81% compared with 74%, and to being told the impact of their donation, at 93% compared with 88%.

Men show stronger responses to relevance and endorsement. Thirty-three percent are motivated by a recommendation from their personal or professional network, compared with 24% of women. Thirty-four percent are motivated by being reached through their preferred channel, compared with 25% of women.

The practical lesson is that your retention strategy has more room to work when donors have multiple paths to connection and trust. Transparency, impact, peer validation, and channel preference can all help supporters see a reason to stay engaged.

### The region changes the dialect.

Midwest donors sit at the bottom of several identity and belonging measures. They are the least likely to expect to give more this year, at 45% compared with 62% in the West. They are also the least motivated by being made to feel part of something, at 28% compared with 43% in the Northeast.

For fundraisers, that points toward a different emphasis. Renewal messages to these donors may have more room to lead with concrete need, visible outcomes, and demonstrated results rather than relying heavily on community language.

Taken together, the data makes a strong case for a more flexible retention strategy. Your donors have different reasons for believing, connecting, and giving again. Your messaging can make room for those differences.

## How does donor trust affect annual renewals?

The West is both the most trusting region and the most likely to expect to give more this year, according to the Giving Signals Report and its additional segment analysis: 86% trust nonprofits at large to use donor funds effectively, against a 79% national average, and 62% expect to give more this year. The Midwest sits low on both, trust and growth moving together in the same direction.

Trust decides whether everything you send after gets believed. Even a well-written renewal appeal underperforms in a low-trust relationship, because the donor doesn’t believe the money will do what you say it will.

That’s why closing the loop matters more than most nonprofits treat it. It makes the next ask believable, not an afterthought you get to once the campaign wraps. Underinvest there and the shortfall shows up as lost revenue.

## Frequently asked questions

### What’s the difference between a donor acquisition message and a donor retention message?

An acquisition message asks someone to join a community for the first time, using belonging and identity to earn a first gift. A donor retention message asks someone who already gave to trust that their last gift worked, using specific proof like where the money went and what it accomplished. The Giving Signals Report found belonging drives 46% of first-gift donors but only 28% of repeat donors, so the two messages need different proof, not just a warmer version of the same appeal.

### Why do donors respond differently to belonging messages after their first gift?

Once someone has already given, their focus can shift from deciding whether they want to be part of the cause to understanding what their gift accomplished. That shift helps explain why belonging, a top-three motivator for 46% of first-time donors, drops to 28% among donors deciding whether to give again (Giving Signals Report, 2026). Showing donors the results of their generosity can give them a compelling reason to keep that impact going.

### What should a donor renewal appeal include instead of a generic thank-you?

A donor renewal appeal should connect a specific dollar amount to a specific outcome while still expressing genuine gratitude. When the Giving Signals Report tested a generic appeal against one that explained exactly what $50 or $200 would buy, donors chose the specific version by 88 percentage points. Nearly every donor, 94%, also says they are more motivated when an organization explains exactly where the money will go.

### Do all donors respond to the same kind of proof?

No. High-capacity donors from households earning $150K or more respond more strongly to third-party ratings such as Charity Navigator. Women respond more to transparency and matching gifts, while men respond more to peer recommendations and preferred communication channels. Midwest donors respond less to belonging language than donors in the West or Northeast. These differences give nonprofits a strong reason to tailor retention messages around the signals that matter most to different supporters.

### Should nonprofits ask donors to add a tip at checkout?

A tip prompt deserves careful consideration. The Giving Signals Report found that a tip prompt may cause 70% of donors to reconsider giving at all, even though 44% of nonprofits still include one at checkout (Giving Signals Report, 2026). Turning the prompt off or setting it to zero can create a smoother giving experience. Unexpected fees present an even greater risk, with 79% of donors saying they may reconsider giving entirely because of a fee they did not expect. Clear disclosure before checkout can help donors know exactly what to expect.

## So, what should you do with this data?

You do not need a new mission or a bigger budget to put these insights to work. You can start by giving the donor you already have more of the proof they are asking for.

Show them where their gift went. Show them what changed. Give them outcomes they can picture and progress they can feel proud to have helped create. Then use what you know about your supporters to make that proof more relevant to the person reading it.

Your donors have already chosen your mission once. Your retention message can show them just how much their choice made possible and give them a reason to keep the impact going.
