## The Currency You Can’t Budget For: Building Trust in Communities of Stakeholders

  

**Key Takeaways**

- **Trust is not a byproduct. It’s infrastructure** – The strongest strategies, programs, and partnerships struggle when trust isn’t present. Trust isn’t built after the work. It makes the work possible.
- **People trust what they experience consistently** – Reliability isn’t about never making mistakes. It’s about communicating honestly, following through, and showing up when things get difficult.
- **Power shapes trust, whether we acknowledge it or not** – Even well-intentioned leaders can undermine trust when decisions are made for communities rather than with them.
- **Authenticity, reliability, and mutual respect are daily practices** – Trust grows when people feel heard, respected, and included in shaping what comes next.
- **Listening is one of the most practical trust-building tools available** – Creating space to hear stakeholder experiences without defending, explaining, or fixing can reveal more than any survey ever will.

---

Early in my career, I was tasked with launching an administrative restructuring for a volunteer group. I did my homework: I consulted peers, researched best practices, and developed a detailed plan. Then, the time came to present this work to the volunteers themselves. It did not go well. The response was emphatically negative, even hostile.

Initially I was taken aback, even frustrated with their response. My plan was solid; it aligned with best practices and legal requirements. The volunteers had asked me to make it. So why was the response so icy?

On reflection, the mistake was obvious. I hadn’t built trust with the volunteers. [Trust is the currency that makes everything else in community work possible.](https://thenonprofithive.com/trust-based-nonprofit-funding-models/)

Without it, the most well-resourced initiative will stall, the most elegant strategy will collapse under the weight of skepticism, and the sincerest intentions will be read as manipulation. We talk endlessly in the nonprofit and civic sectors about capacity, innovation, and equity — and yet we rarely talk honestly about the condition that makes all three possible. Trust is not a soft skill or a feel-good add-on. It is the infrastructure.

## What Trust Actually Requires

There is a tendency in organizational life to treat trust as a byproduct, something that will naturally accumulate if people just do their jobs well. This is a mistake. Trust is not a byproduct. It is an intentional cycle- needs expressed, needs met.

Think about what happened in Flint, Michigan. The water crisis that began in 2014 was, at its core, a catastrophic collapse of institutional trust. Residents had been raising alarms about the water for months. They were dismissed, doubted, and in some cases actively deceived by the very agencies charged with protecting them. When the truth finally emerged, the damage was not only physical. It was relational. There was a deep wound in the bond between the community and its institutions. The lesson isn’t simply that bureaucracies can fail. It’s that trust, once broken by people in positions of power, cannot be rebuilt through press releases and apologies.

So how to build trust? In my mind there are three pillars: authenticity, reliability, and mutual respect. If they are not consistently present, trust will not flourish.

### Authenticity is the first pillar.

Communities of stakeholders, whether they are donors, clients, neighborhood residents, or partner organizations can detect performance from a considerable distance. When an executive director shows up at a community meeting with laminated talking points and a practiced smile, people in the room are not fooled. They have seen this before. Authenticity means being willing to say, “we don’t know yet,” to acknowledge when a program isn’t working, and to invite critique rather than manage it.

The Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation spent years learning a version of this lesson: the sheer scale of its resources created power asymmetries that made genuine partnership nearly impossible, even when the intentions were good. Authenticity requires honesty about power, not just warmth.

### Reliability is the second pillar, and it is more demanding than it sounds.

Reliability is not about keeping every promise. It’s true that circumstances change, funding dries up, plans shift. It is about being honest when things change and following through small commitments so that stakeholders have evidence, not just hope, that you will follow through with the big ones. Organizations that are reliable show up to the hard meetings, not just the celebratory ones. They return phone calls. They do what they said they would do, or they explain clearly why they couldn’t.

The RAND Corporation, in its decades of work with public institutions, has built an unusual level of credibility not only because its research is rigorous, but because it has repeatedly demonstrated a willingness to publish findings that contradict the preferences of its funders.

### Mutual respect is the third pillar, and it may be the most underappreciated.

[Mutual means both directions.](https://thenonprofithive.com/nonprofit-power-imbalances-and-burnout-breaking-cycle/) Communities of stakeholders often experience relationships with institutions as one-directional: the organization decides, the community receives. This arrangement may be efficient, but it is not respectful, and it does not generate trust.

When the city of Medellín, Colombia undertook its dramatic urban transformation in the 1990s and 2000s, it did something unusual. Instead of simply building infrastructure in its most marginalized communities, it engaged residents in the planning process, and not just through perfunctory consultations, but through earnest co-design that genuinely shaped outcomes.

The results were not just physical. Social trust increased. Community ownership of public spaces has increased. Crime rates dropped dramatically. Respect, expressed through participation, changed what was possible.

## The Trap of Well-Intentioned Power

One of the more uncomfortable truths in this conversation is that trust can be undermined not just by bad actors but by well-meaning ones. In the nonprofit sector, we see it constantly: a major donor enters a partnership with an underfunded community organization, arrives with a compelling theory of change and genuine enthusiasm, and proceeds to reshape the organization’s priorities around the donor’s vision rather than the community’s needs. The organization, dependent on the funding, acquiesces. The original stakeholders, like clients and neighbors, that the work was meant to serve gradually become an audience rather than a constituency.

This dynamic doesn’t require anyone to be malicious. It requires only that power goes unexamined. Trust in stakeholder communities demands that those with power actively interrogate how that power shapes the room. It demands structural humility: an honest accounting of who has voice, who doesn’t, and why.

## One Thing You Can Do Right Now

If you lead an organization, run a program, or convene any kind of stakeholder group, here is a concrete practice: hold a listening session with no agenda items and no decisions on the table. No strategy to unveil, no ask to make, no data to share. Simply invite your stakeholders, especially the ones you hear at least, to tell you what they think is working, what isn’t, and what they wish you understood about their experience. Then listen. Don’t defend, don’t explain, don’t immediately solve. Take notes. Follow up writing within two weeks, sharing what you heard and what you plan to do differently as a result.

This is not a revolutionary act. But it is a consistent one, and consistency is what trust is made of. The organizations that build durable trust in their communities are not necessarily the ones with the best programs or the most funding. They are the ones that show up, listen, and change day in and day out.

## The Long Game

Trust is slow to build and fast to lose. [Every nonprofit leader](https://thenonprofithive.com/leadership-through-trust-and-restraint-parenting-nonprofit-leadership/) who has watched a hard-won partnership dissolve over a single missed communication, a single tone-deaf announcement, knows this in their bones. What makes the work worth is the conviction that communities of stakeholders are not simply a means to organizational ends, they are the point.

If we are to thrive, we must treat the people we serve not as beneficiaries but as partners, not as audiences but as authors. When authenticity, reliability, and mutual respect become not aspirational values but daily practices, trust will follow, and where trust is strong, possibilities are endless.
