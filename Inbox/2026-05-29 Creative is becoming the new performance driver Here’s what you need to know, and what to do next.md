For years, nonprofit marketers were told that better targeting was the key to strong performance. Build smarter segments. Refine your lookalikes. Narrow in on the exact right audience. And for a long time, that was true.

But platforms are changing — quickly. And the biggest shift happening right now is this: **Creative is becoming a stronger performance driver than targeting itself.**

[Meta’s newest AI systems](https://www.usewonderful.com/blog/meta-andromeda-creative-strategies) are one of the clearest examples of this shift. Increasingly, they optimize less around advertiser-defined audience segments and more around creative signals: the emotional hook, storytelling angle, visual style, pacing, voice, and proof points within the ad itself

But this is not just a Meta story.

**We’re seeing similar dynamics across nearly every major platform as AI takes a larger role in ad delivery and content recommendations.** TikTok’s algorithm has long prioritized content resonance over explicit targeting. Google’s Performance Max campaigns increasingly automate audience decisions. Prime Video just related a new format that changes creative based on what creative viewers have already seen. Streaming, social, shopping, and search platforms are all moving toward the same model: give the system more creative inputs, and let AI determine who responds.

At the same time, people’s expectations online are changing too.

People don’t experience the internet in neat audience buckets anymore. They experience it through hyper-personalized feeds that constantly adapt to their behavior, interests, mood, and attention. Your TikTok feed does it. Your Spotify and Netflix accounts do it. [You’ve heard us talk about the importance of relevance in creative for a long, long time](https://www.mrss.com/lab/building-blocks-of-effective-creative-relevance/) — now it’s becoming a mandate, not a nice-to-have.

There’s also a broader cultural shift happening here. As people spend more time in AI-curated environments, they are becoming less tolerant of generic messaging. Audiences increasingly expect content that feels personally relevant, emotionally intelligent, and tailored to their interests in the moment.

That expectation doesn’t stop at entertainment platforms. It carries over into the fundraising, advocacy, and nonprofit communications work we do, too.

This doesn’t mean targeting disappears entirely. Strong audience strategy still matters, especially for prospecting, donor qualification, suppression, and lookalike modeling. It’s still important to use all the tools at your disposal to find the right people for your campaign’s message.

But the old model — where media strategy carried most of the performance burden and creative mainly filled placements — is becoming less effective.

#### So what’s new?

Increasingly, platforms want a wide range of differentiated creative inputs so their systems can determine which messages resonate with which people in real time. **And what’s key to know here is that platforms are not just asking advertisers to upload more** ***assets*****. They want more distinct** ***ideas*****.**

That’s an important difference: Swapping a headline or CTA is not the same thing as testing fundamentally different emotional approaches, storytelling structures, visual languages, or audience entry points. In fact, Meta has started to *deprioritize* ads that are too similar to one another. The systems underpinning ad platforms work better when they have genuinely different creative directions to learn from.

Which means that for many nonprofit marketers, the future is probably not “3–5 concepts with a few variations.” Instead we need to…

- Develop more (a lot more) distinct concepts
- Conduct more testing, in-market or pre-market
- Take more out-of-the-box swings with our creative

If you’re starting to feel a little sweaty at the thought of ballooning your ad creative mix overnight, I got you. Here’s what to do next:

**1\. Expand the number of unique concepts you’re running with intention.**

Just because Meta or Google says you need to run hundreds of ad concepts doesn’t mean you magically have the budget to make all those concepts overnight. (Though if you did, let’s get in touch — I’d love to help.)

Instead, play a little game I like to call “concept bingo.”

Instead of starting with formats (“we need another video ASAP!1!!!”), start with intersections:

- Who are we trying to reach?
- What emotional or storytelling angle might resonate with them?

Include the obvious audiences like lapsed donors or potential sustainers, but also broader mindset-based groups like:

- Someone who has never heard of your organization but cares deeply about your issue
- Parents with young children
- Someone who is feeling overwhelmed by the news cycle
- Someone looking for tangible ways to help right now

Then map those audiences against different creative approaches:

- Social proof
- First-person storytelling
- Humor (yes, really! Nonprofits are understandably cautious about this one, but in the rare times we do launch a concept that incorporates humor, we see it resonate extremely well)
- Urgency
- Creator-style content
- Unexpected visuals
- Static concepting
- Movement moments
- Hope- or optimism-based messaging

Suddenly, you’re not trying to invent 40 random ads. You’re building strategically distinct concepts with different points of entry for different people.

**2\. Get more comfortable with calculated risks.**

We, the nonprofit community, are not in the risk-taking business. I get it. The stakes are too high, the resources too tight, to go out on many limbs.

**But AI-driven ad systems increasingly reward novelty, variation, and distinctiveness. Playing it safe everywhere may actually become the riskier long-term strategy.**

The organizations navigating this best are not abandoning brand discipline. They’re just creating room for experimentation within the parameters of their brand identity.

Two organizations I admire here are Planned Parenthood and USA for UNHCR. Both intentionally protect space in their programs for calculated creative testing alongside more proven approaches. For them, they’ve found it helpful to frame riskier concepts as a response to a specific goal, like “reaching younger audiences” or “connecting with sports enthusiasts.”

![](https://www.mrss.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image4-1.png)

And importantly, “taking risks” does not necessarily mean abandoning your brand voice or making something shocking purely for the sake of it. Sometimes it simply means trying a different visual language, tapping into a cultural moment, testing a creator-style execution, or exploring a completely different emotional tone than your organization typically uses.

![](https://www.mrss.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Screenshot-2026-05-29-at-10.09.18-AM-scaled.png)

(And it’s worth saying that, despite these examples, taking risks does not necessarily mean making a basketball-themed ad.)

This shift also requires organizations to rethink how they manage risk internally. If platforms increasingly reward differentiated creative, teams may need approval processes that allow for faster testing, more experimentation, and a little more tolerance for concepts that feel unfamiliar at first glance.

**3\. Consider tools to make it easier on yourself.**

The good news is that new tools are also making this level of creative variation more manageable.

Dynamic creative optimization can help organizations build and serve many more combinations of messaging, visuals, and audience signals than would be practical manually.

For example, organizations like NRDC can dynamically tailor creative based on geography, local environmental issues, or even species relevance — creating experiences that feel much more personally meaningful to supporters.

That can be especially valuable for organizations trying to push into newer or riskier creative territory while still protecting brand trust and performance.

#### The bigger shift underneath all this

For years, the industry invested heavily in targeting infrastructure, attribution systems, and media optimization while creative processes often stayed relatively static. You launched a campaign with a handful of assets, optimized delivery, studied reporting, and iterated gradually over time.

As nonprofit marketers with limited resources, this often meant building concepts designed to appeal to the broadest possible audience so the same creative could work across many segments.

But as AI takes on more responsibility for distribution and targeting, creative is increasingly becoming the primary signal these systems use to match messages with people. It likely means investing more in creative development than many organizations have historically been used to. It means building systems for faster testing and iteration. It means creating a wider range of stories, formats, emotional tones, and audience entry points.

It’s more work…but it’s also an opportunity for us.

At a moment when attention is fragmented and trust is hard to earn, the organizations that succeed will be the ones that feel most relevant, authentic, and emotionally resonant to the people they’re trying to reach.
