Key Takeaways

- **Financial autopilot often starts with good intentions.** Most organizations don’t end up relying on “Do It Like Last Year” because they lack commitment. It happens when limited capacity, inherited systems, and competing priorities make change feel difficult.
- **Clear financial reporting creates better leadership.** When boards and staff can actually understand the numbers, financial conversations become opportunities for better decisions instead of routine compliance.
- **Financial knowledge shouldn’t belong to one person.**Whether it’s a domineering gatekeeper or simply confusing reports, organizations are at risk when only a handful of people feel equipped to engage with the financial picture.
- **Your mission deserves better than last year’s budget.** Instead of asking whether this year’s budget looks like last year’s, ask whether your financial decisions move the organization closer to the impact you want to create.
- **Changing financial culture changes organizational culture.** Transparency, shared understanding, and meaningful conversations about finances strengthen governance, leadership, and ultimately the mission itself.

---

There’s a habit hiding in plain sight in nonprofit boardrooms across the sector. It doesn’t show up on a risk register. Nobody calls it out in the annual audit. But it costs organizations dearly — in leadership, in momentum, and sometimes in survival.

We might call it the Autopilot Approach — AKA DILLY, “Do It Like Last Year”: when last year’s budget becomes this year’s budget with a minor cost-of-living nudge, when the same impenetrable spreadsheets get circulated at every board meeting, and when financial reports are treated as sacred artifacts that only a select few are qualified to decode.

If any of that sounds familiar, you’re not alone. You don’t have to stay stuck.

## How Good Organizations End Up Here

Before we diagnose the problem, it’s worth acknowledging something: DILLY doesn’t happen because people are lazy or irresponsible. It happens to good, smart, caring organizations — often for completely understandable reasons.

The **overhead myth** is a big one. The nonprofit sector has long been pressured to minimize administrative costs, which means finance and accounting functions are chronically under-resourced. When the finance team is stretched thin, things get bootstrapped — and “same as last year” is the path of least resistance.

**Fear of implicit criticism** is another. In some organizational cultures, changing how things are done carries an unspoken message: that what came before wasn’t good enough. When the people who built those old systems are still in the room — or still revered — proposing something different can feel like an attack on their legacy.

And sometimes it’s simply that **life got busy**. Strategic financial conversations are easy to defer when program deadlines loom, staff turnover creates chaos, and every meeting is already overloaded. These things fall off the radar for months, then years, and by the time someone notices, the gap between where you are and where you should be has quietly grown into a real risk.

Recognizing how you got here isn’t an excuse — it’s a starting point.

## A Story From the Field

Early in my work as a nonprofit finance professional, I was brought in to help an organization in serious trouble. On the surface, things looked functional. They had a board. They had a finance committee. They had a CPA as their Treasurer.

But underneath? Near-total financial blindness.

The board members openly admitted they had almost no sense of whether the organization was financially healthy. The top staff leader treated the numbers as background noise — secondary to the “real work.” And the reports themselves were so dense and disorganized that when I showed them to a colleague with decades of audit experience, we both came away scratching our heads.

The Treasurer’s contribution to board meetings was to read each line item aloud. That was it. Compliance theater, not governance. The board went in circles every time finances came up — not because they lacked intelligence or commitment, but because no one had given them a clear picture of reality.

---

## When the Gatekeeper Becomes the Problem

Nature abhors a vacuum, and so do organizations. In the absence of shared financial clarity, one person stepped in to fill the gap: the Finance Committee Chair.

He had an accomplished career — though not in finance. But because he was the only one willing to interpret the numbers, everyone deferred to him. And his interpretation came with a clear agenda: spend as little as possible, always, regardless of context or consequence.

He wasn’t a bad person. He genuinely believed he was protecting the organization. But in practice, he had become a wall between leadership and the truth. Every proposal to invest in infrastructure, retention, or program quality ran into his resistance. Every question about the data was met with dismissal — or worse.

It’s worth noting: this kind of domineering gatekeeper is one version of the problem, but honestly not the most common one. In most organizations, the culprit is more mundane. Nobody’s being abusive; the reports are just bad, the culture around financial conversations is passive, and nobody has pushed hard enough — or felt empowered enough — to change it. Both versions have the same outcome: leaders who can’t see clearly enough to steer.

The organization in this story, meanwhile, was quietly deteriorating. Infrastructure was failing. Key staff positions turned over constantly. Membership was fractured. And the mission — the reason everyone showed up — was stagnant.

Think of a family member who refuses basic medical treatment because of the cost of the appointment, all while the underlying condition grows more serious. Left untreated long enough, that condition doesn’t stay manageable — it either requires a far more expensive intervention down the road, or it becomes fatal. That’s what this organization was doing. It was “saving” its way toward a crisis that would either cost far more to fix than the original problem ever would have, or force it to close its doors entirely.

---

## The Shift That Changed Everything

The turnaround didn’t come from a windfall grant or a new leader swooping in with a magic plan. It came from a critical mass of leaders — and I was part of that process — deciding that the way things had always been done was no longer acceptable.

We drew a clear line: the mission is sacred, but the methods are not. And we started asking a different question — not “does this match what we did last year?” but “does this move us toward impact?”

Part of that shift was demanding financial reporting that people could actually use. Once the rest of the board could see the organization’s real condition — laid out clearly, visually, without jargon — the gatekeeper’s grip loosened. Transparency created accountability. Accountability created engagement. Engagement drove real decisions.

And critically, it wasn’t just the board. The top staff leader’s engagement with the financial picture was just as important. When leadership at every level — board members, the Executive Director, senior program staff — can read the same map and ask questions from their own vantage point, the quality of decisions improves dramatically.

Financial autopilot rarely starts with bad intentions. It starts with busy people, inherited systems, and conversations that slowly become routine. But as this organization discovered, changing the way leaders see the financial picture can change the trajectory of the mission itself.

Your mission deserves better. Stop running on autopilot—and make sure your leaders are behind the wheel.
