### TEAMS! Newsletter | Talent Edition | August 24, 2026

Working in the social services sector often puts you on the front lines of emotional intensity. When staff, community partners, or board members express deep frustration or feel excluded, the natural instinct for a mission-dedicated person is to leap into action and try to “fix it.” You may want to clarify misunderstandings, defend decisions, or resolve the problem instantly. Yet this immediate drive to problem-solve can inadvertently heighten defensiveness, erode trust, and even leave others feeling dismissed.

Leading and building lasting relationships in high-stakes environments requires moving beyond immediate defensive reactions. Shifting from immediate reaction to intentional presence enables each of us, as individuals and as leaders, to hold space for tension, turning friction into stronger alignment and trust. Drawing on the **A.I.R. Formula** developed by Diane Flynn in the *Empower Leadership Program*, we can practice more empathetic listening as a personal habit that will benefit our relationships and our leadership skills.

### Executive Summary

Empathetic listening reduces defensiveness and builds institutional trust by enabling leaders to lead with presence rather than jumping to solutions. By mastering the A.I.R. practices (**Acknowledge, Identify, Respond),** we can navigate difficult conversations more effectively, uphold psychological safety, and advance critical social impact work.

### Key Takeaways

- **Lead with Presence, Not Solutions:** Taking a brief breath before speaking signals respect for the conversation and halts automatic self-defense.
- **Acknowledge Without Debating:** Honor the person's experience directly, without justifying your intent or offering excuses.
- **Identify and Label the Emotion:** Naming the underlying emotion calms the nervous system and helps foster mutual understanding.
- **Choose a Clear Forward Pathway:** Advance the conversation by asking clarifying questions, agreeing on concrete steps, or creating time to reflect.
- **Separate Empathy from Agreement:** Validating someone's emotional reality does not imply conceding fault or changing strategic priorities.

### The Anatomy of the A.I.R. Formula

**Lead with Presence, Not Solutions.** Before offering answers, take a breath of A.I.R. to center yourself. Empathetic listening requires leading with your presence long before you offer your solutions. When we rush to fix things, it often signals that our discomfort with tension matters more than the person's experience. Setting aside the immediate urge to solve makes real conversation possible.

- ***Real-World Scenario:*** A senior manager approaches you in tears, overwhelmed by workload pressures. Your first instinct is to pull up a task tracker and reorganize their workflow on the spot. Instead, you intentionally take a breath, resist opening your laptop, and ground yourself in the moment. By offering full, unhurried presence first, you build the trust they need to regulate before co-creating a sustainable fix.

**Acknowledge Their Experience.** When a stakeholder shares a frustration, your first priority is to ensure they feel heard and not immediately judged. You are not fixing or debating; you are simply honoring their perspective. If a brief apology is warranted, keep it sincere and free of self-justification. Phrases like *"Thank you for telling me”, or, "I appreciate you raising this"* set an immediate tone of openness.

- ***Real-World Scenario:*** Imagine a Program Director frustrated that their team was left out of an important critical project discussion. Instead of immediately explaining the rationale, open with: *"Thank you for raising this. I didn't realize it came across that way, and I appreciate you telling me."*

**Identify the Emotion.** Labeling the emotional impact helps ease tension and signals genuine concern and interest in the other person. Even if you name the feeling imperfectly, the deliberate effort shows empathy. Recognizing that someone feels overlooked, discouraged, or stressed validates their human experience.

- ***Real-World Scenario:***Following up with the Program Director, you name the sentiment: *"It sounds like you felt sidelined, and it makes sense that would be frustrating."* By mirroring the emotion, you help them process the situation so you can move forward together constructively.

**Respond and Move Forward.** Once the individual feels fully heard, guide the conversation toward constructive action. Flynn’s A.I.R. framework outlines three pathways for navigating this transition:

- *Ask Questions:* *"What outcome would feel better to you?"*
- *Suggest a Next Step:* *"Here's what I can do next..."*
- *Buy Time:* *"I want to give this the thought it deserves. Let me reflect and get back to you."*
- ***Real-World Scenario:*** Conclude the discussion with the director by looking ahead: *"What would be better next time? I am open to changing how we involve team members in allocation decisions."*

**The Power of Nonverbal Presence and Silence.** Empathy relies heavily on nonverbal cues. Pausing for even a few seconds before responding signals that you are taking the input seriously rather than rushing to defend yourself. Nodding, maintaining soft eye contact, and offering brief reflections like *"I hear you"* reinforce active listening without interrupting.

- ***Real-World Scenario:*** During a heated town hall, an employee emotionally accuses leadership of ignoring frontline burnout. Instead of immediately jumping in with a list of wellness initiatives, you sit quietly, lean slightly forward, nod, and maintain soft eye contact. You hold a full four seconds of silence before speaking. That brief pause lowers the room’s collective heart rate, signaling that you are absorbing their reality rather than formulating a quick “PR” response.

### Putting A.I.R. into Action This Week:

- **Implement the 3-Second Pause:** Before responding in emotionally charged meetings, pause for three seconds to process the feedback nondefensively.
- **Reflect on Your Responses:** Review your recent written and verbal communications to eliminate self-justifying language (e.g., *"What I actually meant was..."*).
- **Practice Emotion Labeling:** In your 1-on-1s, actively practice identifying emotions, for example, by using phrases such as *"It sounds like this situation felt discouraging.”*
- **Deploy the "Buy Time" Option:** When caught off-guard by complex feedback, state: *"I want to give this the thought it deserves. Let me reflect and circle back by \[Time/Day\]"*; be specific about when you will respond.
- **Separate Empathy from Concession:** Remind yourself and your leadership team that validating a team member's feelings does not mean agreeing with their narrative or conceding on policy. It means taking in the feedback to find the best path forward with an open mind.
