### How artificial intelligence is reshaping donor engagement, operational efficiency, and the future of mission-driven work

Not long ago, technology played a supporting role in nonprofit fundraising. Today, it's becoming a strategic advantage. And increasingly, that advantage is being shaped by artificial intelligence.

According to the [2025 AI Benchmark Report](https://page.techsoup.org/ai-benchmark-report-2025) from TechSoup and Tapp Network, 85% of nonprofits are already exploring AI. Yet only 24% have a formal strategy for implementing it. That isn't surprising. The technology has advanced much faster than most organizations have had time to adapt.

Developing that strategy starts with asking a different question. Rather than focusing on the technology itself, nonprofit leaders should focus on where they're losing capacity. That's where AI has the potential to make a meaningful difference. The organizations that see the greatest benefit won't necessarily be the ones experimenting with the most tools. They'll be the ones that use AI to remove friction from everyday work, giving their teams more time to build relationships and advance the mission.

## AI creates more capacity for fundraising

The best fundraisers already know what inspires generosity. They build trust. They tell compelling stories. They connect people to a mission that matters. What often prevents them from doing more of that work isn't fundraising itself, it's everything surrounding it.

Hours disappear into data cleanup, reporting, donor research, segmentation, scheduling, and drafting communications. These aren't unimportant tasks, but they're rarely the highest-value use of a fundraiser's time. Every hour spent wrestling with administrative work is an hour that can't be spent building relationships with donors.

This is where AI is already proving its value.

**Predictive intelligence**

[Predictive donor](https://bloomerang.com/blog/predictive-intelligence-for-fundraisers) intelligence has become one of the most practical applications in nonprofit fundraising. By analyzing giving history, event participation, email engagement, and other behavioral signals, AI can help identify which supporters are most likely to give, when they may be ready for another conversation, and where a fundraiser should focus their attention first. Instead of replacing judgment, it helps direct it.

**Personalization at scale**

The same principle applies to donor communications. Bloomerang's [2026 Giving Signals Report](https://bloomerang.com/resources/giving-signals-report-2026) found that 87% of Millennial donors are motivated by personalized communication, compared with 63% of Baby Boomers+. AI makes that level of personalization achievable at scale by helping organizations segment supporters based on interests and engagement—not simply previous giving history.

**Content Generation**

Content creation follows the same pattern. According to the TechSoup report, 24% of nonprofits are already using AI for grant writing and 33% are using it for content marketing. AI isn't replacing the fundraiser or grant writer. It's producing the first draft, summarizing information, or organizing ideas so staff can spend more time strengthening the message and less time staring at a blank page.

That's the real opportunity. AI doesn't eliminate the human role. It removes friction so people can spend more of their day doing the work only humans can do.

## The opportunity extends well beyond fundraising

Every nonprofit has work that simply has to get done but rarely advances the mission directly. Volunteer coordination, scheduling, compliance reporting, database maintenance, and program documentation consume countless hours across organizations of every size. Individually these tasks seem manageable. Collectively they become one of the biggest drains on organizational capacity.

AI is beginning to change that as well. By reducing the administrative burden across these routine functions, nonprofits can redirect time toward strategic planning, community engagement, and mission delivery.

Some of the earliest examples are coming from large global organizations. The [World Food Programme](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01698-9) has piloted machine learning models that forecast hunger trends up to 60 days in advance across Mali, Nigeria, Syria, and Yemen. The [Nature Conservancy](https://www.nature.org/en-us/newsroom/palmyra-atoll-hawaii-artificial-intelligence-seabird-habitat-restoration/) is using AI-enhanced cameras to improve wildlife monitoring and species detection. These examples don't suggest every nonprofit needs sophisticated AI research projects. They simply illustrate where the technology is headed—and how capabilities that once required enormous budgets are becoming increasingly accessible to organizations of every size.

**Building an AI strategy with purpose**

One of the more encouraging findings from recent research is that donors appear more comfortable with AI than many nonprofit leaders assume. A 2025 donor survey found that 67% of donors are comfortable with nonprofits using AI for fundraising, marketing, and administrative work.

The larger challenge is organizational readiness. Seventy-six percent of nonprofits still have no formal AI policy, and many leaders simply aren't sure where to begin.

Organizations that start by identifying one or two specific pain points and deploy targeted AI tools to address them will build the confidence and institutional knowledge to expand over time.

If donor follow-up consistently falls through the cracks, start there. If grant applications consume too much staff time, focus there. If impact reporting requires days of manual work, make that your first use case. Organizations that begin with outcomes rather than software tend to see faster adoption because they're solving real operational challenges instead of experimenting with technology for its own sake.

## Closing the gap

The path forward doesn't require a large budget or a team of AI specialists. It requires intentionality. The organizations making the most progress aren't trying to transform everything at once. They're starting with one meaningful challenge and building confidence from there.

Research consistently shows that nonprofits with a defined AI strategy are better positioned to realize the benefits of these tools while navigating legitimate concerns around privacy, governance, and ethical use. That starts by asking a simple question: *What outcome are we trying to achieve?* Organizations that begin there are far more likely to make thoughtful technology decisions than those chasing the latest AI innovation.

AI is becoming another part of the nonprofit toolkit. The organizations that build their understanding now, experiment with purpose, and invest in their team's capacity will be better prepared for what's ahead—not because they adopted AI first, but because they adopted it thoughtfully.
