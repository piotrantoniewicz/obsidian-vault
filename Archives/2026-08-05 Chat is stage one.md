The first is efficiency. Work you already do, done faster. Drafting the email. Summarising the meeting notes. Turning a transcript into action items and an agenda. This is real value and I would never talk anyone out of it. Every hour you claw back is an hour you spend on work you actually care about instead of work you have to do.

The second is imagination. Work that was not possible for you six months ago. Not faster. Possible.

Hardly anyone is in the second bucket.

I ran a session for an organisation a few nights ago, walking through Claude’s features. Halfway through I realised the features were not the interesting part. The interesting part was that the group had been using the tool for a month and had spent that entire month in a chat box, which is roughly the equivalent of buying a workshop full of tools and only ever picking up the hammer.

Here is what the second bucket looks like in practice. I spoke to an organisation a while back who were fighting their CRM. Unusual structure, unusual needs, and the platform could not be bent to fit how they worked. Support tickets, workarounds, expensive, frustrating. So they hired a developer who builds with AI and they had a custom CRM made. Designed around them rather than around the average customer. It will cost them less over time, it does things the old system could not do, and there is a version of the future where they sell it to organisations that work the same way they do.

Two years ago that project would have been six figures and eighteen months. That is the whole shift.

The reason most organisations stay in bucket one is not budget and it is not skill. It is that they treat AI as a place to type questions. Typing questions is stage one of five.

## One. Chat

The brain, on its own.

Two things worth doing here. Pick a more capable model for anything strategic, anything where you would naturally be putting real thought in. The lighter, faster models have their place in high-volume work, but they are not who you want in the room when you are thinking. Most tools now also give you some version of an effort dial, which controls how long the model spends reasoning. Turn it up for hard things. Turn it down for mock-ups and drafts.

The second thing is the microphone. Speaking is three or four times faster than typing, but speed is not the real benefit. Typing puts a filter between your brain and the page. You start thinking about spelling and sentence structure and you flatten what you meant.

I was running an interview technique with a friend recently, where the AI asks one question at a time and you answer. He typed back four words. I told him to turn on voice and not stop talking until a minute had passed. The whole direction of the conversation changed, because somewhere in that minute he changed his mind about what he actually thought. You cannot do that in four typed words.

The ceiling of stage one is that you are the bottleneck. Every task starts from nothing and you paste the context in by hand, every time.

## Two. Context

This is the stage almost everyone skips, and it has the highest return of anything in this article.

Projects are a container. A shared space that holds a history of chats on one topic, a set of custom instructions, memory that builds over time, and a body of reference material the AI can reach into.

The comparison I keep coming back to is an intern. Give a new intern no context and they are useless for a month. Give them your strategic plan, your mission and values, your org chart, your policies, your programmatic work, a sense of who is on the team and how each person likes to receive information, and they are useful on day one. Same intelligence. Different context.

Three practical notes. Prefer text over PDFs where you can, because plain text is cheaper to process and easier for the model to work with. Copy your about page in rather than uploading the brochure. Second, project files are not swallowed whole every time you start a chat. The system pulls the relevant parts based on what you asked, which is both cheaper and better than dumping everything into a single conversation. Third, one enormous chat is not a substitute. Output quality degrades as a conversation gets very long, well before you hit any technical limit.

Build a project for the organisation and share it. Then build one for comms, one for fundraising, one for grant proposals, one per major funder with their website content and your successful past applications and the transcripts of your conversations with them.

Then build one for yourself. Open it and say: I want to work out how to use AI better in my role. Interview me one question at a time until you have enough to write it up. Who I work with, what my role covers, what tools I use, what I do every week that repeats. Then take what it produces and put it back into the project as context.

That personal project becomes a filter. Someone sends you a forty minute AI video, or a long article about a new framework. Paste the transcript in and ask whether this is worth your time given what you are working on. Half the time the answer is no, and you just got your evening back.

## Three. Output

We are still sharing documents with each other as though it is 2019.

Artifacts let the AI build a small web page or interactive thing instead of a wall of text. I have used them for a framework I put together from a few hundred meeting transcripts, and it reads as a proper document with structure and highlighting and warnings, rather than something you scroll past.

The version I like best is more useful than that. Say you have an AI policy and no idea whether anyone read it. Ask for a short quiz that finds out. Or take one report and build a single artifact with a button for each person you are sending it to, where the board member gets the summary they want and the operations lead gets the detail they want, from the same underlying data.

Alongside that sits Design, which is where slide decks, landing pages and app mock-ups get built. Load your fonts and colours in as a style guide, share it across the team, and everything anyone produces stops looking like it came from four different organisations.

## Four. Agency

Everything above is a brain. This is where it gets hands.

On the desktop app the AI can work with folders on your machine, which matters enormously if your files change constantly and you are tired of re-uploading spreadsheets. Connect the apps you already use. Then set up scheduled tasks, which run on their own. The common starting point is a morning brief that checks your calendar, flags the emails that actually need you, and summarises what happened in Slack overnight.

Two warnings. Human in the loop is a dial, not a switch. Start with high involvement, watch how it behaves, and hand over more only as you get comfortable. And these systems can add friction as easily as they remove it. A morning brief, an evening recap, a Friday review and a lunchtime digest is not four times the value. It is noise. Add one, live with it for a fortnight, keep it or kill it.

## Five. Process

The last stage is turning what worked into something reusable.

Say you have a meeting transcript. You ask for the concerns, the action items and anything with governance implications. The first answer is close but too long, and it missed the thing the CEO needs to see. You refine it three times and get something good. Most people stop there and do the same three refinements next month.

Instead, ask it to write a skill from what you just did. A skill is the process, written down in a form the AI can follow again, and it works across chat, the desktop app and the coding tools. Then share it. A comms skill that every external message runs through, checking your phrasing and how the organisation gets described, is worth more than any single output it produces.

If you take one prompting habit from this: describe the outcome you want and let it work out the middle steps, rather than specifying each step yourself. Your job moves from instructing to critiquing.

## The thing that actually decides this

Depth beats breadth, and it is not close.

The organisations getting the most out of AI are not the ones running Claude and ChatGPT and Gemini and three app builders. They are the ones who picked something and went deep. Skills, artifacts, scheduled tasks, connectors: each of those is a vertical you can spend months inside. Sampling five tools gets you the surface of all of them.

The multiplier is culture, not licences. Make a channel where people share what they tried. Give it ten minutes at the end of team meetings, and treat what did not work as equally worth hearing, because someone else in the room has probably solved it. Keep a running list of things you wish were possible and revisit it every couple of months. You will be surprised how often the answer changes.

Here is where to start. Make a project. Call it something like AI rollout. Paste this article into it. Then ask it to interview you about your role and your organisation, and to come back with a four week plan for which of these stages to work on first.

You will get a better answer than I could give you, because it will know things about your work that I do not.
