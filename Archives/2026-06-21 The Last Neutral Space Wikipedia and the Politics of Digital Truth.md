Power

21.06.2026

Stop treating Wikipedia as just a website. It sits underneath Google results, AI answers, parliamentary research, and the homework of half the planet. That makes it infrastructure. Public, global, shared memory. And like any piece of infrastructure that matters, people with power keep trying to bend it their way.

Here is what I find remarkable. It mostly holds.

\-

Wikipedia defends itself with three things working together. Transparency of every edit and every source. A culture of volunteers who argue in the open. And machine-learning tools that help human editors spot manipulation at scale without ever taking the final decision away from them. None of this is built for perfection. It is built for resilience. Make capture hard. Make contestation easy. Make failure visible. That is the whole game.

In a moment where platforms and AI systems are hoovering up information power into a few hands, this messy, decentralised model might be one of the last working templates we have for democratic control over knowledge itself.

Think about it. Every biography, every election article, every conflict page is a tiny constitutional fight. What counts as a fact. Whose sources are allowed. How you describe power in language that pretends to be neutral. The familiar layout hides all of that. The lead paragraph, the infobox, the blue links, the references. Underneath sits a political system made of revision histories, talk pages, and community norms that decide whether your edit lives or dies.

And unlike the platforms we have learned to distrust, Wikipedia shows you the machinery. Every page carries a public log of who changed what and when. That is the first real line of defence and almost nobody talks about it. You can scrutinise the process, not only the output.

Go and click View history on any article. The polished prose disappears and you get a dense ledger of edits, anonymous and named, piling up over years. Timestamps. Usernames. Little summaries where people explain themselves. Fixed a date. Added a source. Reverted vandalism. Then open the Talk page and you find editors arguing about wording and neutrality and which sources pass the bar. On the contested political stuff, that argument is where the real fight against manipulation happens. Slow, public, unglamorous.

#### When anyone can edit, anyone can attack

The weakness is obvious. Open the doors to everyone and you invite the vandals in. Coordinated campaigns, state-backed reputation laundering, the lot. The pages about elections, security services, and divisive politicians get the worst of it.

But the crude stuff barely lasts. Slurs, joke edits, obvious propaganda. Automated filters and watchful volunteers catch most of it within minutes. The real threat is quieter. I would call it slow capture. Well-sourced but slanted edits. Cherry-picked citations. Careful reframing that nudges an article towards one narrative while technically obeying every rule.

So the community builds layers. The high-traffic pages, heads of state, live conflicts, breaking crises, get locked down so only experienced or approved editors can touch them. Anything controversial has to be defended on the talk page where others can demand better sources or escalate the dispute. Over time articles settle around what the community calls a neutral point of view. But settle is the wrong word really. Neutrality never stays put. Politics shifts, new sources appear, and the argument cracks open again. That endless reopening is exactly what stops any government or party or company from freezing the story in its favour.

#### The algorithms flag, the humans decide

Here is the part people get wrong about the technology. The machine learning inside Wikipedia does not decide what is true. It triages risk. The foundation runs its own models like LiftWing, and the community builds bots like ClueBot. They score incoming edits for likely vandalism, flag the dodgy ones, and auto-revert the most blatant attacks like page blanking or profanity.

But every serious call still goes through a person. A moderator looks at the flagged edit, reads the context, and decides whether to back the algorithm or overrule it. That matters more than it sounds.

And the choice to run those models on Wikimedia's own hardware is political, not only technical. No commercial cloud provider, no national government can lean on the foundation to quietly retune a filter or bury a topic. At the same time, when a community-built model beats the in-house one, they adopt it. Distributed expertise over central command. That is the only way I can see to use AI as a sentry rather than a censor. The machine helps humans cope with the volume. It never becomes the judge.

#### Participation is a political right

If you build neutrality through conflict, then taking part stops being a hobby and becomes a kind of citizenship. The talk pages let any reader become a co-governor of the content. Question a source. Propose a rewrite. Challenge a consensus you think is bent. These fights get ugly. But they leave a documented trail of how a contested fact got negotiated, and that trail helps the next reader judge the text in front of them. Sometimes the disputes end in formal community rulings. Sometimes they end in carefully hedged language that holds two competing views and grounds both in citations.

The human side is the bit outsiders underestimate. Volunteers do not just edit. They form relationships, they argue across continents, and they meet at conferences where old online enemies discover they actually share a love of getting things right. That social glue is why mass manipulation is so much harder than it looks from the outside. To capture Wikipedia you do not simply game a rulebook. You have to outlast a motivated global community that genuinely believes it is guarding a commons. Protecting that community, keeping editors, welcoming newcomers, shielding people from harassment, is as important as any technical upgrade. Maybe more.

#### What this teaches us about AI

Now hold all of that up against the way AI answers political questions. Confident. Fluent. Untraceable. Large language models swallow Wikipedia whole and rephrase it, but they throw away the revision history, the talk pages, the visible sourcing. So you get the answer without any way to see where it came from or to push back. If AI becomes the front door to political knowledge, but not the place where that knowledge gets argued over and improved, then accountability quietly drains away.

I am not arguing we bin the technology. I am arguing AI answer systems should inherit the things Wikipedia gets right. Provenance you can inspect. Change logs you can audit. A way for people to challenge and correct what comes out.

And governments itching to police disinformation from the top should think again. The better move is to fund public, participatory knowledge instead. Open archives. Open parliamentary records. Civic wikis that can feed AI while staying under democratic control.

Here is the uncomfortable bit to finish on. If we keep treating information as a product to consume rather than a commons to govern, we will wake up one day and find our history quietly rewritten by whoever had the resources to game the opaque systems. Wikipedia, flaws and all, points at the other road. The one where the politics of knowledge stay out in the open, and resisting manipulation is everybody's job.
