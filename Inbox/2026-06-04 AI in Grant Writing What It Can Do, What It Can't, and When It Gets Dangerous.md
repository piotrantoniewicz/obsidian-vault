Last month the Chronicle of Philanthropy quoted me on AI and grant seeking. The line they pulled was a warning: it's really easy for AI to help you look bigger than you are.

A few of you asked what I meant. So here is the full version, from someone who uses AI on real grant applications.

Let me start with what AI does well, because it is genuinely useful.

**WHAT AI DOES WELL**

Reading the RFP. I can drop a 20-page request for proposal, the kind overflowing with legalese, into NotebookLM and get a clear synopsis in minutes. It tells me what the funder actually wants, what questions I have to answer, and what I will need to pull together: operating data, staff time from different departments, letters of support from partners. Work that used to eat an afternoon now takes a coffee break.

Organizing your thinking. AI is good at turning a messy pile of notes into a structured outline that matches the funder's priorities. It helps you see the gaps before you have written a single paragraph.

Drafting faster. Once you know what to say, AI gets you a rough first draft so you are editing instead of staring at a blank page. For a small shop where one person wears the fundraising hat along with four others, that is real time back.

Notice what all three have in common. AI is doing the support work. You are still making the decisions.

**WHERE IT GETS DANGEROUS**

Here is the trap. AI writes confident, polished prose. That is exactly what makes it risky in a grant application.

A grant is not a marketing email. It is a promise. Every outcome you put in that narrative is something you are committing your organization to deliver and report on later. And AI, left unchecked, will happily make your nonprofit sound bigger, faster, and more capable than it actually is. It may add language about scale you do not have and impact you have not yet proven, because it is optimizing for a strong-sounding application, not for what your team can realistically pull off.

You do not feel the cost at the writing stage. You feel it at the reporting stage, when you are accountable for numbers you never had the capacity to hit. Overcommitting on a grant can damage a funder relationship more than losing it ever would.

There is a second risk. Funders can tell. I have heard it directly from grant makers: when an application feels generic, or does not really answer the questions they asked, it raises red flags. A grant churned out by AI and shipped without a human shaping it often reads exactly that way. The shortcut that was supposed to save you time quietly costs you the award.

**THE NON-NEGOTIABLE: HUMAN IN THE LOOP**

So here is the rule I teach, and the one I follow. Use AI to understand the RFP. Use it to organize and to draft. Then put human eyes on every single claim before it goes out the door. Not a skim. A real read, by someone who knows what your team can actually deliver.

Three quick checks before you submit anything AI helped you write:

1\. Can we actually do every number and outcome in here? If not, cut it.

2\. Does this answer the questions the funder asked, in their language, not generic nonprofit-speak?

3\. Would I be comfortable reporting on these promises a year from now?

If a claim does not survive all three, it does not belong in the application.

**THE REAL LESSON**

My experience in nonprofit grant writing have taught me that the organizations that get the most out of AI are not the ones with the fanciest tools. They are the ones who know their own capacity cold, so they can catch the moment AI starts writing checks the organization cannot cash.

AI is a powerful assistant for grant writing. It is not a grant writer. The judgment, the honesty, and the promise still have to be yours.
