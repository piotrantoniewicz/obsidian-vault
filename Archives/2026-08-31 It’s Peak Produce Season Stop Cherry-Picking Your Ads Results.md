As a gardener, I’m pretty hands-on: I like to go in and mess with my plants. That kind of hands-in-the-dirt work is immensely meditative for me: after spending my whole working day staring at a screen, there’s something really nice about the physical labor of gardening.

It’s also, weirdly, sort of challenging for the “I want instant results for my efforts” part of my brain. Stuff just takes time out there — and gardens have a funny way of rewarding your attention, but resisting quick and easy optimization.

### Focusing on the right stuff

I have one specific tomato plant that is, frankly, popping tf off right now. And I don’t know why! Maybe it’s the fact that I’ve been meticulously pruning the suckers (trust me, this is a real thing and it’s actually quite important for increasing the yield of indeterminate tomatoes 😭), or that I used graduated container sizes for the seedling, or the fact that I splurged on [the fancy soil sold by California hippies who got their start in \*ahem\* medicinal agriculture](https://foxfarm.com/product/ocean-forest-potting-soil/). Or maybe it’s just in the exact right spot on a sunbathed rooftop during a lovely warm summer with generous rain, which is pretty cool and something I can’t take an ounce of credit for.

Obviously, given infinite time and resources, I would give each and every one of my plants the princess treatment, AKA do every possible thing to make them happy (and be rewarded for my efforts with infinite perfect produce).

But that is not my journey. I have other stuff to do, and other places to spend my money! (I can’t go broke spoiling my plants, I need to go broke spoiling my dogs.)

What I need is an understanding of the relative impact of each intervention. Something like…

- Most of the difference between a thriving tomato plant and a sad one comes down to a combo of soil, water, and sunshine.
- With those three handled, you have a set of optional strategies that will lead to growth.
- Timing is important: focus on root system development in the early summer – once the plant is established in its final container, pivot to pruning for maximal fruit production.
- Avoid overdoing it with specific interventions (namely, fertilizer), as they can backfire when done to excess. Also be mindful of diminishing returns: some plants might require a 25-gallon pot, others will be perfectly happy in a 15-gallon container.

With that knowledge, I can make smart gardening decisions — with time and money left over to enjoy the fruits of my labors.

### Marketing mix modeling: a key tool for advertisers

This is the part where I bring the analogy home 😉

Like a certain recreational gardener, nonprofits do not have infinite time and resources — especially in an environment that’s a lot less conducive to fundraising than my rooftop is to gardening.

Every organization I’ve ever worked with has faced a version of one question, and the details change but the big picture doesn’t: **across broad marketing channels (digital ads, direct mail, email) and within specific areas (social vs search vs display), where do you allocate your resources for maximum impact?**

Unfortunately for us all, fundraising is a lot harder than gardening, and coming up with a good answer to this question is actually pretty tricky.

One good solution is multi-touch attribution (MTA for short): by tracking a single donor across many channels, you can see the full path of their interactions with your organization, and model the most effective points of intervention.

MTA is pretty great — in fact, I spend a lot of my time on Scout Ads, our in-house MTA tool! (If you’d like to hear more about it, hit my line: [scoughlon@mrss.com](mailto:scoughlon@mrss.com) 🙂) However, it has some pretty serious limitations, especially in a reporting environment that’s increasingly opaque due to a shifting privacy landscape — not to mention a shift in donor behavior toward [the black box of conversational AI](https://www.mrss.com/lab/what-googling-will-mean-in-2027/) or [alternative forms of giving like DAFs](https://www.mrss.com/lab/new-research-understanding-why-donors-choose-dafs-stocks-and-more/).

To address this, we’re seeing a lot more attention toward a well-established (but oft-overlooked) methodology: it’s called marketing mix modeling, or MMM for short.

**It works by using advanced statistical analysis to compare your marketing efforts (like ads, email, and DM) against your fundraising performance over time — and critically, it controls for contextual factors like brand strength, seasonality, and the overall fundraising environment.**

Using this analysis, you can come up with a mathematically-rigorous answer to questions like “how much of my revenue was attributable to ads vs the broader environment?”, or “how are ads supporting offline efforts like direct mail?” You can also answer more ads-specific questions like “how long does it take for a TV buy to start boosting revenue?”, or “is it worth it to run display ads to our existing donor file?”

It, honestly, rules — and with those reporting shifts I mentioned, I see a lot of benefit for our community in familiarizing ourselves with how it works, why it works, and why nonprofit marketers have been historically less likely than our for-profit peers to use it.
