If you’re looking for a channel that lets you reach supporters directly, at low cost, with full control over the message, [email](https://www.engagingnetworks.net/solutions/nonprofit-email-marketing/) is it. No algorithm decides who sees your appeal. No ad budget required. Just your words, your mission, and a well-timed send.

Whether you’re sending a monthly newsletter or managing a multi-segment email automation program, the fundamentals are the same. This guide covers fundraising email best practices, from subject lines to list health, with examples to inspire you right now.

## Start With Strategy

The most common email mistake isn’t a bad subject line or a weak call to action (CTA); **it’s sending without a clear sense of why**. Before you write a word, here are some tips to keep in mind.

### What Is This Email Trying To Do?

Not all fundraising emails are direct appeals. Some build relationships and steward donors. Some re-engage lapsed supporters. Knowing the goal shapes everything: the tone, the ask, the timing, and the CTA.

### Who Are You Sending To?

Even for small organizations, your list is not a monolith. It may include first-time donors, long-time supporters, volunteers, event attendees, or people who signed a petition three years ago. They have different relationships with your organization, and a single email can’t speak equally well to all of them.

But audience segments don’t have to be complicated to be useful. Even a basic split (donors vs. non-donors, or active vs. lapsed) makes your appeals more relevant, which in turn makes them more likely to succeed.

**Engaging Networks Tip:** If managing multiple versions of the same email feels like too much, try **conditional content**. This lets you personalize sections of a single email based on who’s receiving it, so a donor and a prospect can get meaningfully different messages with a single send.

### Learn by Testing

Every email is an opportunity to learn something. Test your subject lines, send times, CTA copy, email length and more on a regular basis. Not everything needs to be a formal A/B test — sometimes just paying attention to what performs over time is enough.

### Maintain a Healthy Email List

Engagement is just as important as list size, if not more so. A list full of people who don’t remember signing up, or who signed up for something unrelated, is going to underperform regardless of how good your emails are. Regular list hygiene — removing hard bounces, unengaged contacts, and invalid addresses — keeps your sender reputation intact, your metrics meaningful, and your emails landing in front of people who actually want to hear from you.

## How Often Should You Send Fundraising Emails?

There’s no universal right answer. Email too rarely and you become a stranger; too often and you train people to ignore you. Most organizations find a rhythm somewhere between **once a month and weekly**, with frequency increasing around key campaigns. Political fundraising emails operate under different norms, with higher frequency and near-constant deadline-driven appeals.

The better question isn’t “how often?” but “do we have something worth saying?”

Not every email should be an ask. Mixing in impact updates, stories, and stewardship messages builds the kind of relationship that makes donors more likely to give when you do ask.

## Fundraising Email Subject Lines: Your First (and Sometimes Only) Impression

Your email’s subject line determines whether your message gets opened or ignored. That’s a lot of pressure on a handful of words. But with a few core principles and regular A/B testing, you can write fundraising email subject lines that capture your supporters’ attention.

### What Makes a Good Subject Line?

- **Specificity over vagueness:** “Help us make a difference” competes with every other email in the inbox. “17 families need housing before winter” gives the reader something tangible to grab onto.
- **Short subject lines:** Mobile inboxes typically display between 33 – 50 characters before cutting off, while desktop shows up to 70. Aiming for under 50 characters is a safe rule of thumb. Put the key message in the first 30 – 40 characters so it survives even the most restrictive email clients.
- **Curiosity:** A question or unexpected angle can stop a reader mid-scroll. Something like “The call that changed everything” or “How many families can $100 feed?” makes the email feel worth opening.
- **Personalization:** A first name in the subject line can help, but personalization that reflects actual donor behavior (“You gave last December. Here’s what happened next”) is even more powerful.
- **Urgency:** Deadlines work when they’re true — but “last chance” loses its meaning after the fifth time.

| **Less effective** | **More effective** |
| --- | --- |
| Help us reach our goal | We’re $4,200 away — will you close the gap? |
| Support our year-end giving campaign | Tonight’s the last night. Here’s why it matters. |
| Please donate today | 63 kids. One summer. $50 gives the opportunity of a lifetime. |
| It’s been a while | A lot has changed since your last gift, \[First Name\] |
| We need your help | 48 hours left to hit our match goal |
| Giving Tuesday is here | Your gift doubles today only |
| An update from our team | The phone call that changed everything |
| Thank you for your support | Three years of giving — here’s your impact, \[First Name\] |

### Don’t Forget Preview Text

Preview text is the sentence that appears after the subject line in most inboxes — and it’s one of the most underused tools in email. Think of it as a second subject line. It can add context, extend the hook, or give a different reason to open.

### Test Subject Lines, Then Test Again

Subject line testing is the best way to learn what works for your organization. Small changes, like a question versus a statement, can meaningfully shift open rates.

One underused tactic: Re-send the same email to non-openers a few days later with a different subject line. The content is already written, the audience hasn’t seen it, and a fresh subject line is often all it takes. It’s one of the simplest ways to extend the reach of an email you’ve already put work into.

## Writing the Email: Structure, Copy, and the Ask

A fundraising email isn’t a brochure and it isn’t a news update. The best ones are specific, anchored around a specific ask to a specific audience. They tend to follow a loose structure:

- **Hook:** Grab attention in the first sentence — a story, a surprising fact, a direct question. Don’t bury the lede.
- **Story or context:** Why does this matter right now? Connect the reader to a person, a moment, or a problem that feels specific and impactful.
- **The ask:** Be direct. Tell the reader what you want them to do and why it matters.
- **CTA:** A prominent button or link to the desired action.

### How Long Should A Fundraising Email Be?

Fundraising emails should be long enough to make the case, and short enough to keep attention. If you’re sharing a personal story about an individual your organization helped, you may need a longer email to create an emotional connection. If you’re sending a final reminder about a matching gift deadline, you may only need a sentence or two.

When in doubt, cut; if a sentence doesn’t earn its place, it costs you the reader’s attention.

### The Role of Visuals

Most people don’t read emails — they skim them. A strong visual near the top of your email can do a lot of heavy lifting, conveying emotion and communicating your main message before the recipient has read a single word. For longer emails especially, visuals help break up the text and give the eye somewhere to rest.

### One CTA, Always

Every fundraising email should have one primary action you want the reader to take. Two CTAs split attention. Three or more usually means nobody clicks anything. If you’re linking to a donation page, link to it consistently throughout — and make the button copy specific: “Give $50 today” beats “Donate now” every time.

Strategy is easier to see in action. These fundraising email examples show what these principles look like when they come together — from short donation message examples, to emotional impact stories, to thank-you messages.

**Subject:** The year’s almost over. Here’s what you made possible.  
**Preview:** (And one more thing we’re hoping to do before December 31.)

Maria,

A year ago, you gave $75 to **\[Nonprofit organization name\]**. You probably didn’t think much about it after that — but we did.

The support of donors like you helped us provide over 12,000 meals to families in our community this year, and three after-school programs that kept kids safe and engaged.

We have one goal left before the year ends: raise $15,000 to expand our food pantry hours into the weekend — the time when need is highest and resources are lowest.

We’re $6,200 away. Will you help us feed more families in need?

**Give Before December 31**

Thank you for being part of this,  
*Signature  
Title, Nonprofit organization*

#### Why It Works

- Opens by acknowledging the reader’s past action, immediately making them feel seen
- Leads with concrete impact before making a new ask
- Names a specific funding gap that makes the need feel real and closeable
- One CTA with a deadline

**Subject:** What you’ve missed at **\[Nonprofit organization name\]**  
**Preview:** We’ve grown a lot, and we’d love to have you back.

It’s been a couple of years since you last supported **\[Nonprofit organization name\]**, and we wanted to reach out with an update. A lot has changed!

- Last fall, we launched our after-school residency program, serving 120 students across four schools who otherwise have no access to arts education.
- This spring, three of our alumni were accepted to conservatory programs — the first time that’s happened in our organization’s history!
- This summer, we’re launching our first free youth playwriting intensive. Two weeks, no cost to participants, and a public showcase at the end.

If you’re in a position to give again, even a small gift makes a real difference right now. If it’s not the right time, we understand — but we hope you’ll stay connected to what we’re building.

Reconnect With a Gift

Either way, thank you for the support you’ve already given. It helped lay the foundation for everything above.

Warmly,

Title, Nonprofit organization*

#### Why It Works

- Acknowledges the gap without dwelling on it
- Shows the impact of previous gifts and provides a clear reason to give now
- Tone is warm, not transactional — appropriate for rebuilding a relationship

Brian,

- Opens with urgency and a specific fundraising gap
- Frames urgency honestly rather than artificially
- Short by design; this is a deadline email, not a story email, and the reader already has context from previous updates
