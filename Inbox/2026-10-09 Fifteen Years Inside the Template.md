This morning I added a Services page to my website. I opened the AI project that knows my site, showed it a couple of pages I liked, and it interviewed me: what do I offer, and what does it cost? A few minutes later the page was live.

On WordPress, that job started with a blank page and a page builder. Drag in a section, drag in another, nudge things until they lined up, then go hunting for an image. I worked that way for more than fifteen years, on my own sites and for organisations. I’m not a web developer and I’ve never claimed to be, but I knew my way around a template.

That was the problem. Everything I built lived inside it. If WordPress, the plugins or the page builder could do something, so could I. If they couldn’t, I needed a developer and a few thousand dollars, so mostly it didn’t happen.

When I launched [AI Impact Hub](https://aiimpacthub.com/), I found a form plugin that could call an AI model. Someone typed into the form, the request went off, and the answer appeared on the same page. I could even choose the model and set the temperature. I thought it was revolutionary. It was still a form.

Compare that with something I built recently. With Giving Tuesday coming, I wanted to show how AI can take on personas, so I made [the Giving Factor](https://hub.aiimpacthub.com/learn/giving-factor). It’s modelled on the X Factor. The curtains open on three judges: Emma, who has given before; Jess, who has never heard of you; and Tom, who volunteers but can’t give money. You paste in your fundraising copy and each of them tells you how it landed. The first-time donor needs a reason to trust you. The past donor wants to know what their last gift did. The volunteer wants a way to help that doesn’t involve their wallet. Same email, three different reads.

Inside WordPress, the best I could have offered was a form that handed you a prompt to paste somewhere else. This is a game.

Or take [Nonprofit Tools](https://nonprofittools.com/), which I built last week. The homepage is a pile of tool icons that tumble down the screen and part when you move your mouse through them. A year ago I wouldn’t have even considered it. This time I described what I wanted, and after a few rounds of fixing how the icons fell around the search bar and what happened when the results came up, it was exactly what I’d pictured.

No single build changed things. Each one taught me something for the next: the tools, the systems, the frustrations, what to watch out for. I built Nonprofit Tools in a fraction of the time it would have taken me at the start of the year. Somewhere along the way, I stopped working inside the template.

---

The part I didn’t expect is that nothing I build is finished anymore.

On WordPress, finishing a page meant you were done with it, because going back was so much work. A few weeks ago a new model came out that’s very good at front-end design. I asked it to look at every template in [Because of You](http://because-of-you.com/), the platform I’m building for digital donor thank-yous, and show me what version two could look like. They came back ten or twenty times better, and I could rework the whole thing in one go instead of page by page. When AI adds something, it looks at the whole site and adjusts the rest to fit, so new pages stop getting bolted on the way they do on most WordPress sites.

That’s what I keep trying to show organisations. The other day I was on a call with one that wanted to redesign its website. Too many pages, things all over the place, nobody sure where anything lived. While we talked, I had AI look at their site, pull in their images and reimagine it. Before we hung up, I showed them a very rough first draft of something cleaner, with clear sections.

They had no idea that was possible. They asked for the link so they could show the rest of their team.

Most organisations hear “website” and think WordPress, Wix, Squarespace or Webflow, because custom work has always sat outside the budget and outside the frame. It’s within reach now, and it costs less. That’s also why I won’t build on WordPress anymore. Every WordPress project would cost me ten I could build with AI.

The first question I get is always who looks after it. Say it’s Tuesday afternoon and you’ve just landed a spot on the news in an hour. If you’re waiting on a developer, or the volunteer who helps with the website, the story has come and gone before the change goes live. With a custom site set up properly, your AI connects to wherever the code lives (usually GitHub) and someone on your team makes the change in a couple of minutes. Setting that up safely matters: backups, the right tools, and access for the people who need it. It does carry more risk. I think the freedom is worth more.

---

So where do you start? Not with an iPhone app. My wife and I are building one, and between the App Store, payment gateways and privacy policies, it’s a hard first project.

Start with your own website. There’s no style guide or communications code to work inside, so you can make it look like you: clean and professional like a CV, or scattered with little icons AI drew for you. Mine has become a living portfolio of who I am, what I do and how to work with me.

Keep a note of what doesn’t work, because the next model might manage it. And don’t decide it’s too technical. Some of the people I least expected to pick this up have rebuilt their dream websites, because they had support and kept asking questions.

If you want a first step for Monday, copy this article into your AI and ask it to guide you through building your first site.

Then ask yourself the bigger question. If you could build anything, what would you build? What would your website be if it were more of an experience, for your donors, your volunteers or the people you support?

We’re in the age of possibilities. I’d encourage you to start seizing them.
