Power

06.07.2026

The central problem for democratic governance in 2026 is no longer just disinformation; it is the opacity of the systems that move information, target voters and mediate participation. Political tech sits at the fault line: it can either harden that opacity or expose it, making power legible and accountable again.

In a Europe where elections are shaped on platforms headquartered elsewhere, the question is whether the continent can build its own infrastructure, legal, technical and civic, fast enough to remain sovereign over its public sphere.

\-

Europe’s democratic problem is no longer only what people believe, but how little anyone can see of the machinery that shapes those beliefs. A dense web of opaque algorithms, targeted political ads and AI‑generated forgeries now mediates everything from election campaigns to local grievances, while the systems that run it mostly sit outside European jurisdiction. Political technology has become the nervous system of democracy; the question is whether that system serves public accountability or shields power from scrutiny.

In response, a new “democracy stack” is emerging: tools that track and archive political advertising, platforms that visualise disinformation waves in real time, and civic tech infrastructures that make debates and decisions legible to ordinary citizens. Around them, European policymakers are trying to build the legal and technical scaffolding, from sovereign digital infrastructure to stricter platform duties, that would give democratic institutions real leverage over their own public sphere. What is taking shape is less a single product than a trust protocol for the digital age.

#### A public sphere saturated with fakes

Scroll through any European election season and the line between reality and fabrication flickers. AI‑generated images of leaders in invented settings, synthetic protest scenes, doctored “leaks” and perfectly polished talking points circulate at speed, often designed less to convince than to exhaust. The cost of producing plausible political fabrications has collapsed; the cost of verifying or debunking them, for citizens and institutions alike, has not.

This asymmetry hits hardest where digital literacy is weakest. Older voters and those less familiar with platform dynamics are especially vulnerable to content that “feels” authentic because it confirms pre‑existing fears, whether about migration, inflation or geopolitical conflict. At the same time, core democratic institutions, parliaments, parties, regulators, rely heavily on foreign‑owned clouds, platforms and data tools, outsourcing not only infrastructure but visibility into how political narratives move. The result is a public sphere where manipulation scales faster than oversight.

#### Transparency by default, not by grace

The most effective antidote to this opacity is radical transparency built into the digital architecture of politics. If political advertising can be targeted with surgical precision, it can also be archived with equal precision: who paid, how much, to reach whom, with what message. A truly public repository of political ads, searchable by topic, geography, spend and sponsor, would turn the shadows of micro‑targeting into data that journalists, watchdogs and citizens can interrogate.

Beyond ads, network‑level tools can map how disinformation spreads. When a fabricated story about a candidate erupts, it rarely emerges from nowhere; it rides on pre‑built accounts, bots and communities. Visualising those networks in real time transforms vague fears of “foreign interference” or “troll farms” into concrete patterns: which narratives are amplified, which accounts act as hubs, how waves coincide with specific political events. Making those maps public, at least in anonymised or aggregated form, would change the incentives for both platforms and political actors tempted to outsource persuasion to shadowy networks.

Transparency, in this sense, is not a press release about “our commitment to democracy.” It is a design choice: whether citizens can see the full campaign being waged around them, or only the slice that their profile has earned them.

#### Accountability requires owning the rails

Transparency alone is not enough if the power to act remains elsewhere. European democracies discovered during the past decade that when the basic rails of digital life, cloud hosting, messaging backbones, ad networks, AI models, are controlled abroad, political accountability becomes a matter of pleading rather than governing.

That dependence has direct consequences for elections. A party whose fundraising, communications and member records run entirely on non‑European payment processors and infrastructure is one compliance review or geopolitical shock away from being cut off. A parliament whose livestreams, document management and public outreach rely on a handful of US‑based platforms must negotiate basic safety features as terms of service, not democratic guarantees.

A more resilient model starts from the other end: build and mandate sovereign options for critical democratic functions. That can mean EU‑based cloud and AI models for elections, digital identity systems that work across borders but remain under European law, and public infrastructures where political content and data can be processed without leaving the jurisdiction citizens vote in. Sovereignty here is not about protectionism; it is about having levers to pull when platforms fail, rather than depending on voluntary goodwill.

#### Civic tech as the connective tissue

Between the big platforms and big institutions lies an ecosystem that too often remains invisible: civic technologists, NGOs and mission‑driven startups that translate abstract values into usable products. These are the teams building participatory budgeting portals, local deliberation platforms, real‑time fact‑checking services and civic data dashboards that make governance legible.

Their work rarely fits neatly into either philanthropic or venture boxes. Many start as grant‑funded experiments, then discover real demand from cities, parliaments or activists; others are born as businesses but priced into markets where clients either cannot pay or are constrained by regulation. When impact is measured only in revenue growth, such projects are derided as “unscalable,” even as their absence leaves a vacuum filled by opaque platform tools.

A more realistic approach treats civic tech as essential infrastructure and finances it accordingly: blended models where grants fund risk and public goods, while service contracts, subscriptions or modest equity returns sustain operations. In practice, that can look like venture‑philanthropy funds that provide multi‑year support to organisations fighting disinformation and apathy, while pooling their demand for secure, interoperable tools. It can also mean procurement frameworks that make it easier for small, high‑integrity providers to work with public institutions without drowning in bureaucracy.

#### Extending the Democracy Shield beyond the EU

The struggle over digital transparency and accountability is not confined to Schengen borders. In candidate and neighbouring countries, from the Western Balkans to the Caucasus, disinformation campaigns and digital interference often hit first and hardest. These are the test beds where foreign actors refine techniques, probe platform weaknesses and normalise information warfare, long before similar tactics appear in Berlin or Paris.

Treating these spaces as merely “pre‑accession” problems is a strategic mistake. A genuine Democracy Shield extends expertise, tools and infrastructure across the continent: shared standards for political ad transparency, joint rapid‑response mechanisms for information crises, cross‑border collaborations between regulators, researchers and civic tech groups. It also flows both ways. Some of the sharpest innovations in tracking troll networks, securing communication or engaging sceptical citizens are coming from societies for whom democratic failure is not a theoretical risk but a lived memory.

Building such a shield does not mean exporting a prefabricated model; it means co‑creating defences that respect local realities while upholding a common baseline of openness and rights.

#### Towards a democratic trust protocol

Underneath these debates lies a larger question: what replaces the informal trust that once came from limited media channels, slower news cycles and thicker civic organisations? In a digital environment where every citizen carries a personalised propaganda machine, trust has to be engineered as deliberately as encryption or interoperability.

A democratic trust protocol would rest on three pillars. Technically, it would guarantee access to the data needed to monitor political influence, from ad libraries to algorithmic audit trails, and anchor critical electoral and civic processes in infrastructure subject to democratic control. Civically, it would invest in media literacy and digital competence as core democratic skills, perhaps even formalising them through mechanisms like “digital licences” that certify a basic understanding of online risks and responsibilities. Politically, it would treat transparency and accountability tools not as optional supplements but as core state functions, funded and maintained with the same seriousness as physical infrastructure.

The alternative is already visible in corners of Europe and beyond: democracies in name, governed through opaque systems no one really understands, where elections become spectacles staged on platforms optimised for outrage rather than truth. Political technology will not, by itself, prevent that future. But deployed with clarity and courage, it can give citizens and institutions the one thing modern propaganda fears most: a clear view of how power actually works.
