***Why AI Governance Is the Missing Foundation of Nonprofit Innovation***

### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | July 22, 2026

It likely happens more often than we care to admit: a staff member has been pasting case notes into a free AI chatbot on their personal phone. No one did it with bad intent. They were tired, the paperwork was long, and the tool was fast and efficient. That single scenario tells us more about where our sector actually stands with artificial intelligence than any conference panel or vendor pitch ever could. We are no longer deciding whether AI belongs in our work. It has already arrived, quietly, through the side door, one copied-and-pasted paragraph at a time.

The reality of “shadow AI use” isn’t necessarily a discipline problem; it’s more likely a runway problem. No pilot takes off without clearance, a fueled aircraft, and a flight plan filed in advance. Yet many of us are asking our teams to fly AI into daily practice without first building that runway: limited or no data boundaries, no vetted tools, and no shared understanding of what's safe to type into a chat window. This edition is about laying that runway before we ask anyone else to take off.

### Executive Summary: What You Need to Know

- **Unmanaged AI is already inside your organization:** Well-meaning staff are pasting sensitive records into free consumer tools, creating hidden compliance exposure long before any policy exists to address it.
- **Licensed enterprise tools are the shield, not the restriction:** Provider-backed data agreements and administrative oversight transform AI from a liability into a governed utility.
- **Disclosure builds the trust that automation quietly erodes:** The organizations that name where AI touches their work and where it doesn't are the ones families and staff keep believing in.
- **Human judgment is the non-negotiable last step:** AI can sort, draft, and summarize, but the decisions that shape a person's services, housing, or opportunity stay with an accountable human being.

### The Shadow AI We Need to Talk About

There is a version of AI adoption happening across all companies, including nonprofits, right now that no board or management team has approved and no policy governs. Case managers rephrasing incident reports during their lunch break. A development associate drafting a grant narrative from home, pasting donor histories to save time. None of it shows up on an org chart, and that's exactly the danger. Free consumer AI tools are built to improve themselves on whatever you feed them, which means a name, a diagnosis code, or a family's housing crisis can quietly become training data for a system none of us control.

At AbilityPath, our Children’s Therapy team handles exactly the kind of sensitive documentation that makes this risk real: developmental assessments, behavioral notes, family circumstances. The moment we realized staff might be using personal AI accounts to lighten that load, we understood the fix wasn't a memo telling people to stop. People don't stop trying to work smarter. Leaders have to give them somewhere safer to do it, which is why last year we implemented a fully HIPAA-compliant EMR and case management system last year.

### Enterprise-Grade as an Act of Care

I used to think of software licensing as a budget line item. I've come to see it differently: the tier of AI tool we choose is a direct statement of how much we value the people whose information passes through it. Enterprise-grade AI platforms come with contractual guarantees, data isolated from public model training, formal agreements covering protected health information, and administrative logs that let a compliance officer actually see what's happening. None of that exists in the free version of the same tool. We have recently signed two enterprise versions of AI tools, with a third being completed this week. Our teams will have the tools to help them do the paperwork more efficiently, in a safe, data-protected system.

### A Note on Choosing an AI Platform:

> *Before signing anything, ask your provider three questions directly: Will our data ever train your public models? Will you execute a signed data-protection agreement covering health or client information? Can our IT lead see and control who uses this tool, and how? If a vendor hesitates on any of the three, that's your answer.*

The cost of the upgrade is real, though there are discounts for nonprofits. So is the cost of a single unencrypted disclosure that results in a data protection violation. We chose the former.

### Transparency Is Your Trust Contract

Families don't choose our nonprofits because we’re “efficient.” They choose us because we've kept our word on how we treat the people in our care. That reputation doesn't survive an era of quiet automation unless we say the quiet part out loud. Before we let any tool touch a workflow that reaches a client or a donor, we need to write down, in plain language, exactly what the tool does and doesn't do, and put that language where a family member can actually find it.

A regional housing agency I've studied did this well: before rolling out a document-summarizing tool, they held open sessions with the advocates who represent their applicants and posted a one-line commitment stating that a human caseworker makes the final call. The technology didn't change much about their workflow. The willingness to say so out loud changed how the tool was received.

### Bias Doesn't Retire When the Algorithm Arrives

Every AI system bears the fingerprints of the data it learned from, and that data reflects a world with real inequities baked in. For organizations built to widen opportunity for people with developmental differences, adopting a tool without first evaluating it would mean importing bias under the banner of innovation. That's the opposite of our mission.

Picture a workforce program screening resumes to match graduates with local employers, not far from what our vocational job-coaching team does every week. If that screening tool quietly penalizes employment gaps, it would systematically disadvantage the very candidates our coaching is meant to support: people who took time away for caregiving, training, or a disability-related leave. Testing a tool against real cases before it ever touches a real applicant is due diligence for the people we serve.

### The Human Hand on the Final Decision

Of everything in this edition, this is the line I'd want remembered:

> ***AI can shorten a draft, sort a spreadsheet, or flag a pattern worth a second look. It should never be the final signature on a decision that changes someone's services, housing, or opportunity.***

That line belongs to a person who can be asked to explain their reasoning, who carries the weight of getting it right, and who our clients can actually sit across from.

Camp Via West runs on relationships: counselors who notice when a camper is having a harder week, who adjust a plan on instinct built from years of trust. No governance framework should ever ask a tool to make that call. Ours doesn't, and that boundary is written into policy, not left to assumption.

### The Action Playbook: Moves You Can Make This Quarter

- **Publish a data boundary this week.** Tell every team, plainly, that free consumer AI tools must never access a client name, diagnosis, or donor record.
- **Run a no-blame AI usage audit.** Ask staff, anonymously, which AI tools they're already using and why. You can't govern what you can’t see.
- **Move to licensed, agreement-backed platforms.** Have IT formally execute a data-protection agreement and disable public model training across all accounts.
- **Establish a cross-functional review group.** Program staff, IT, compliance, and a board representative should sign off on any new AI use case before it launches.
- **Test every tool against your own equity questions.** Before adoption, run real cases through it and ask who it would have overlooked.
- **Say it out loud to your board and your community.** Share the governance framework in plain language and specify where human judgment remains firmly in charge.

### Closing Thoughts

Our 2030 plan asks us to build an organization defined by innovation and lifelong growth, one that honors every journey it touches. I don't think those words and this quarter's AI questions are separate conversations. Innovation without a runway isn't courage; it's exposure.

But governance built early, in plain sight, with families and staff at the table, is exactly how an organization stays worthy of a century of trust as it learns something new. We don't have to choose between moving forward and moving carefully. We just have to build the runway first.

> ***“Govern First. Fly Faster and Further.”***
