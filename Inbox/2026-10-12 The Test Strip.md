### TEAMS! Newsletter | Talent Edition | October 12, 2026

***What We Learned Over Three Days at Stanford on AI and People Development***

I was a student for three days last week. Armed with a workbook, a whiteboard and markers, sticky notes, and my laptop, I took my place among leaders from government, philanthropy, and the nonprofit sector in Human-Centered AI for Social Impact, the joint offering of the Stanford [d.school](http://d.school/) and the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI (“HAI”). I went in to learn about technology and came away with something more important to learn about people.

Many amateur photographers know the darkroom ritual. You focus on the subject, then, before you use up an entire sheet of paper, you run a narrow strip through the developer in stages and check it under the safelight. The strip is cheap and tells you what you need to know; only then do you make the print you actually want. During our program at Stanford, the instructors took a similar approach to how we should apply that discipline to using AI. That is also how I would describe some of the lessons on developing and cultivating talent.

### Executive Summary

- **Focus first.** The program’s opening lesson was to slow down, determine who you are designing for and their requirements, and only then pick a tool.
- **Run a test strip.** A prototype thrown in front of actual people over a few days is more instructive than any plan stuck away in a binder.
- **Work under the safelight.** Use evaluation, safety, and privacy as design skills from the outset.

In short, this is a core component of the strategy. The habits that put the human back in AI are what allow teams and leaders to grow.

### Three Days, One Question

We moved from context to craft to construction. Day one set the scene with HAI co-founder Professor James Landay and Stanford Law’s Daniel Ho, who outlined what the current moment holds for the social sector, then we got our hands on the tools. Day two was deeper work: Professor Rob Reich on purposeful leadership, Dr. Jennifer King and Professor Sanmi Koyejo on the metrics of safety and privacy, and Professor Angela Aristidou on partnerships for social good. Nadia Roumani and Ariam Mogos of the [d.school](http://d.school/) wove the human-centered design element throughout. On day three, we put it to the test, turning a live project into a working prototype by the end of the morning.

For my part, I was working on a voice-first intake guide for the parents and caregivers of individuals with developmental disabilities, an AbilityPath project we’re piloting. But the question that ran through every session was the one a photographer must answer before making a print: who is the subject, and how can you be sure you have captured it?

### Five Principles I’m Taking Back to AbilityPath

**The Person Comes Before the Tool.** The [d.school](http://d.school/) faculty suggested that we focus on the basics before considering models or vendors. Why are we using AI? What does the person we are designing for need? As Mogos and Roumani have written about the program, “AI is not a magic fix.” We should apply the same rigor to our own staff before implementing a new system or career path.

**Put Your Assumptions on the Table.** The workbook included an exercise to write down what we think we know about those we serve. My list for caregivers was easy to prepare: they have phones, they’re willing to talk to a chatbot, and they know what they want. These assumptions seemed sound until I tested them. Any leader has a similar set of assumptions about employees and what they want. There is no better way to test those assumptions than to put them in writing and validate them.

**Get to Know People on a Deeper Level.** The [d.school](http://d.school/) would call this *ethnographic work*, but the methods are deceptively straightforward: card sorts, journey maps, lived-experience interviews, and the simple request to “show me how you do that.” An interview worth having will establish rapport, draw out a story or two, and then press for specifics or the feelings behind them. For a leader, it is the equivalent of a well-run stay interview or an onboarding path charted from the new hire’s vantage point. You might call it a person-centered approach with a bit of method to it.

**Build in Order to Learn.** The workbook’s instructions for experimentation were in all caps: test your ideas in days, not months. A prototype need be nothing more than “low resolution”; so long as it can be experienced and reacted to, it does not have to look like the final “product”. I made a note to that effect: our concept has to be a pilot we can put in front of staff, the board, and some families. That is our test strip. Applying this approach to talent, it means running a new onboarding step with one team next week rather than spending time designing the ideal program for next year. It is an “Edisonian” approach to prototyping – 10,000 ways of not working in order to get to the one that does.

**Think of Trust as Your Safelight.** In a darkroom, the safelight lets you work without ruining what you are developing. One careless flash of white light and the paper is done for. Our responsible AI sessions asked how a leader gauges risk and determines whether a tool is in the public interest, while safeguarding the data of those they serve. The d. school puts the stakes in perspective: for a community group, a wrong move with new tech can undo decades of hard-won trust. We are not here to make nonprofit staff engineers. Instead, we want to be decision-makers with the vocabulary for good questions and the confidence to ask them. It is a learning agenda at every level of the organization.

### The Six-Step Playbook

- Start with an actual project and write a sentence stating who it is for and how it improves things for them.
- Make a list of your assumptions. What do you take for granted about your staff or the people you serve? Circle any you have not tested.
- Have five conversations where you listen. Use a “tell me about a time” prompt to get a story, and don’t be tempted to put forward your own idea.
- This week, put something rough together. A sketch, a script, or a chatbot will do. The fact that it is unfinished will elicit the best kind of feedback.
- Examine your test strip. Present it to a few families, some board members, and staff, and see how they respond. Make your adjustments.
- Then decide on your metrics and safeguards. Before you put anything in motion, be clear about who is accountable, how data will be handled, and what constitutes success.

### A Closing Thought

The HAI | [d.school](http://d.school/) design was practical, hands-on, and full of learning and insightful takeaways. The cohort was sharp, and the faculty were very generous with the resources and knowledge. What I took away from the three days was the feeling of being a beginner once more, rekindling the “Beginner's Mindset. I was reminded of the courage we ask of our staff when we put something new in their hands.

At AbilityPath, we have given our word to the people we serve and those who work with them that there will be lifelong learning. Technology is a moving target. But the habits of the darkroom still apply: be patient until the picture is fully developed, protect it, make your test strip, and focus on the subject. Do that, and whatever we put together next will truly reflect the people we are here to serve.
