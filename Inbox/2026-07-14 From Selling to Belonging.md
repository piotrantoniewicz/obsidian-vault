***How Genuine Engagement Wins Attention in a Noisy World***

### TEAMS! Newsletter | Engagement Edition | July 14, 2026

I spent over two decades in corporate leadership before I came to nonprofit work, and a lesson that has resonated with me, especially when I’m advocating for our mission or fundraising for our programs, is that nobody owes us their attention. Not because they don't care, but because they're drowning. Every notification, headline, and thirty-second ad is competing for the same tiny sliver of a person's attention that your mission is also competing for. Marketing thinker Seth Godin has argued that marketing is no longer about the product itself but about the stories you tell around it, and I've come to believe nonprofits sometimes resist that idea more than almost any sector I've worked in.

We tell ourselves a purity myth: that if the work is good enough, it will speak for itself. That positioning, voice, and brand discipline are somehow beneath the mission, tools for people selling sneakers, not for those changing lives. I understand the instinct. But it's a luxury we can't afford. In an oversaturated, information-saturated world, silence isn't humility. It's invisibility. Engagement is a form of advocacy, and if we're not intentional about how we earn attention, we're not protecting our mission; we're quietly failing it.

### Key Takeaways:

- **What Business Taught Me About Belonging.** Brand, voice, and positioning are how authenticity reaches communities, supporters, and donors.
- **The First Five Minutes.** A family's or a potential supporter's first encounter with your organization is where positioning becomes lived experience and serves as their introduction to your mission.
- **The Story Before the Strategy.** Every engagement strategy should be built backward from one true transformation, not from a program description. It should put a human face on your mission.
- **Every Touchpoint Is a Trust Deposit.** Connection, once earned, compounds or erodes with every subsequent interaction, and those interactions need to be authentic.

### What Business Taught Me About Belonging

When I was at Electronic Arts, nobody assumed a great game would simply find its audience. Every studio was obsessed with how a player felt in the first ten minutes, what a trailer promised, and how a brand's voice remained consistent across a hundred touchpoints. That obsession wasn't cynical. It was respect. It was respect for the fact that people are busy, skeptical, and free to look away at any moment.

I've watched nonprofits treat that same discipline with suspicion, as if a consistent voice or a deliberate first impression were somehow manipulative. But there's a difference between manufacturing a story and earning the right to tell a true story well. Godin has said that brands fall apart when people discover they're not who they claimed to be. When an organization is consistent with who people already believe it is, it's read as authentic, and that authenticity makes the whole relationship more valuable. That's the whole point. Not spin. Consistency in the service of something real.

Our 2030 strategic plan talks about valued friends and honored journeys. That language only means something if the people encountering AbilityPath for the first time feel it. That brings me to the moment when all of this becomes real.

### The First Five Minutes

Picture a parent walking into a children’s therapy session for the first time. They don't yet know our outcomes data, tenure, or accreditations. They know how they’re greeted and how they feel upon arrival. They know whether the person on the phone sounded rushed or present. They know whether the first interaction felt like a canned sales pitch or an invitation to start a journey together.

This is where positioning stops being an abstraction and becomes a felt experience. Five-star service on a nonprofit budget doesn't mean marble floors and gift baskets; it means every person on your team understands that, in that moment, they embody the mission and are the “brand.” The paycheck excuse doesn't hold here either: a warm, unhurried greeting costs nothing but attention, and attention is the resource we can't take for granted.

If you want to know what your organization's story really is, don't read your mission statement. Walk through your front door as a stranger would and notice what you feel in the first five minutes. That feeling is your real brand, the one your marketing should authentically reflect.

### The Story Before the Strategy

Once that first impression lands, the question is: what story does it lead into? Too many of our sector's materials try to tell every story at once, covering every program, every outcome, and every grateful family, and end up telling none of them memorably.

The gardener's approach applies here. You don't grow ten seedlings by dividing your attention evenly; you tend the ones with the strongest roots and let them anchor everything else. Identify the one transformation, family, Camp Via West summer camper, or vocational placement that changed a trajectory and most honestly represents what you deliver. Build your voice around that story until staff can recall it without a script, because a story that only lives in a brochure isn't a story yet. It's a stat, and it is flat. It has no authentic human dimension.

> ***Godin was right: people don't buy the product; they buy the story that lets them believe it's true. Ours has to be true first, and specific second.***

### Every Touchpoint Is a Trust Deposit

A strong first impression and a compelling story will earn you an opening. What happens next determines whether that opening becomes a relationship.

Every interaction after that first moment, whether a returned call, a rescheduled visit, or a promise kept or quietly dropped, either deposits into or withdraws from a “trust account”. Nonprofits often assume trust is a given because our motives are considered good. But good intentions don't survive a missed callback any better than bad ones. In a low-trust, high-noise world, consistency is an important currency that compounds rather than inflates.

This is where “genuine” stops being a nice word and becomes an operational discipline. Advocacy built on real transformation, delivered consistently, earns loyalty. Advocacy that oversells or postures gets noticed once and distrusted forever. Consistent, genuine follow-through builds trust. Being true to our word, our promises, and our stories told is remembered and either helps build a relationship or, when missed, erodes it.

### The Engagement Playbook

**Map Your First Five Minutes.** Walk through your own front door as a stranger would. Trace the actual sequence, first call, first greeting, first tour, and write it down as a sequence, not an impression. Understand the journey from their eyes.

**Name Your Anchor Story.** Resist telling every story at once. Choose the single transformation that most honestly represents your organization, specific enough that any staff member can recall it unscripted, and one that tells, in human terms, the impact your team is making.

**Have a Consistent Voice.** Decide how that story sounds across all of your messaging: every channel, newsletters, tour scripts, social posts, grant narratives, so it reaches people intact and consistently wherever they encounter it.

**Treat Every Touchpoint as a Trust-Building Opportunity.** Train staff to understand that each interaction adds to or withdraws from the “trust account”, and build a simple way to course-correct when one underdelivers.

**Positioning Should Serve the Mission, Not Compete With It.** Retire the belief that good work should speak for itself. Give your team explicit permission to see “brand and positioning” as a form of respect in a distracted world, to further the mission.

### Closing

I used to think the discipline I learned at EA and the soul of nonprofit work belonged in different rooms. I don't believe that anymore. The care is the same care; only the fluency is different. If we want people to trust us with their attention, and eventually, their loyalty, we have to earn it the same way anyone earns it: by showing up consistently, telling the truth well, and never mistaking a single good interaction for a relationship. The individuals and families we serve deserve our respect and understanding, and we need to earn their trust by demonstrating it in every interaction. This lets them know they matter.

Maya Angelou is widely credited with the observation that people will never forget how you made them feel, and every family who walks through our doors, every partner who meets with us, every donor who reads this newsletter is quietly keeping that account. Let's make sure it's always authentic and that we respect how they feel.

> ***Attention is earned. Trust is compounded. Tell it true, tell it well.***
