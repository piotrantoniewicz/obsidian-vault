Since there seems to be A LOT of reflecting going on (to varying degrees), I figured I’d share a few specific thoughts through an advertising lens. I had the privilege of serving as paid media director on the Biden and Harris campaigns in 2024, and with some distance now, there are a few things I think are worth highlighting– both for the people who will run the next campaign and for anyone who cares about how we move forward as a party.

I will say in advance that this is not an exhaustive list, and this is certainly not meant to highlight all that we did right and/or all the unique and interesting ways we reached voters.

And this is in no way about assigning blame. Campaigns of this scale are enormously complex, and the transition from Biden to Harris created unprecedented operational and strategic challenges in real time. But if Democrats are serious about rebuilding trust, strengthening our coalition, and modernizing how we communicate, we have to be candid about what worked, what didn’t, and what we should do differently next time.

**One Team, One Dream**

Paid media needs to function as a single, unified team. TV, digital, out-of-home, mail, radio, etc., all of it needs to be rolled up to one team, which was not the case on this campaign. When those functions are siloed, you end up with teams working in parallel rather than in concert, resulting in a lack of cohesion across creative and buying. You also lose leverage on buying.

At a moment when voters experience media seamlessly across platforms, campaigns cannot afford internal fragmentation. Voters do not distinguish between “digital” and “TV” messaging, they experience media as a single narrative and creating a surround sound should be core to how we structure our campaigns.

A unified team makes holistic decisions about where dollars go and trade off across channels in ways that simply cannot happen in siloes, especially at a campaign of this scale. This is particularly important for Democratic campaigns, where coalition messaging often requires balancing persuasion, mobilization, and constituency-specific communication at the same time.

**Know Your Ground Game, and Start It Early**

I’ll be the first to admit this might be a surprising one coming from me, a ‘digital person’. But I came away from 2024 with a deep appreciation for what a strong ground game can do. People need to know and feel an election is happening and understand the stakes for their families and communities. They want to be part of something. Physical presence creates a feeling of excitement and community that can be critical in order to win.

In an era of fragmented media consumption and declining institutional trust, real-world organizing is so important. Campaigns cannot rely exclusively on screens and algorithms to build emotional connection or civic participation.

The ground game cannot be an afterthought. It needs to be planned early and budgeted as its own line item, with the same rigor we bring to any other paid channel. One example I keep coming back to: we created beautiful out-of-home murals in battleground states, amplified it through digital channels, merging the offline and online.

The strongest programs created moments that people could physically experience and then organically share online– turning supporters into validators and messengers within their own communities.

Bringing in ground-game experts during the planning process ensures they are very much a part of the overall strategy which allows for more thoughtful collaboration and stronger alignment from the onset. The future of campaigning requires those functions to work hand-in-hand from the very beginning.

**Hone Your Message**

When the campaign flipped from Biden to Harris, the number of messaging tracks we were running increased. In retrospect, it felt like we were trying to figure out our message in real time, and because we were so tight on time, we were throwing a lot at the wall. Outside PACs weren’t running any negative contrast against Trump, so the campaign felt it had to fill the gap (and testing did tell us that we needed to).

But message discipline suffered. And the other side does message discipline extraordinarily well. We sometimes shy away from repetition because it feels like we’re being redundant, but we know that is how a message burns in. In this noisy and fragmented media environment, voters need to hear things multiple times, across multiple channels, before it registers.

Republicans have become highly effective at identifying a small number of emotionally resonant themes and repeating them relentlessly across every platform and validator. Democrats often default toward complexity, nuance, and issue proliferation which doesn’t easily break through or stick.

Message discipline does not mean abandoning complexity or coalition politics. It means communicating core values in ways that are emotionally accessible, memorable, and repeatable.

**Don’t Test Yourself to Death**

We had the financial resources to test virtually everything, and so we did. But pre-flight testing, as rigorous and accurate as ours was, could take several days to return results. In a modern digital campaign, that is simply too long. There were moments where our commitment to testing actually prevented us from being nimble, particularly when we needed to respond quickly to inoculate attacks from our opponent.

Democrats tend to value process, data, and validation (and often for good reason). But campaigns are ultimately about people: their fears, hopes, identities, culture, and lived experiences. Winning requires not just analytics and rigor, but instincts, emotional resonance, speed, and a willingness to act under imperfect conditions.

What I’ve been advocating for going forward is to build a learning agenda, a structured plan for what you’re trying to learn and when, early and before you decide to produce a full flight of creative. Test content types, messages, and messengers against your key audiences and let those results inform your overall strategic framework.

Not every piece of creative needs to go through pre-flight testing. That investment should be saved for genuinely new formats or higher-stakes executions. And throughout the campaign, use lift studies and in-flight surveys to ensure your ads are actually performing in the real world, not just in a testing environment. That combination of up-front rigor and ongoing measurement is how you stay both disciplined and nimble.

The future of political communication likely belongs to campaigns that can combine strong analytics with a faster creative culture, one that empowers teams to iterate rapidly while still grounding decisions in real evidence.

**Get the Most Out of Your Consultants**

The campaign hired a lot of outside consultants. A lot. It makes sense, it was a huge campaign. But not all of them had clearly defined lanes, which meant you sometimes had overlap and at times inefficiencies. Switching candidates seemed to exacerbate the issue, as we just added more consultants and did not make any changes to the existing structure.

Large Democratic campaigns often assemble broad ecosystems of firms, advisors, movement partners, and stakeholders. That can bring tremendous expertise, but it can also create diffusion of responsibility and slower decision-making if roles are not clearly defined.

Great consultants, the ones you really trust, are invaluable at this scale. They should function as an extension of the campaign, able to weigh in on strategy and provide counsel (nearly) around the clock. Moving forward, consider quality over quantity as much as possible.

Campaigns work best when leadership creates clear accountability structures, empowers decision-makers, and builds teams around trust rather than sheer volume.

**Final Thought**

Democrats are operating in a fundamentally different communications environment than a decade ago. Trust in institutions is lower. Media consumption is more fragmented. Attention spans are shorter. Culture moves faster.

But despite all of that, voters are still looking for authenticity, clarity, competence, and a sense that someone understands the pressures in their daily lives. The campaigns that succeed moving forward will be the ones that can combine modern media sophistication with emotional connection, organizing infrastructure, and a coherent governing vision.

There is no silver bullet. But there are lessons worth learning (and improving on) before the next fight begins.

*Cat Stern served as Paid Media Director for the Biden & Harris 2024 presidential campaigns.*
