In recent election cycles, the traditional political media apparatus have lost its monopoly over political communication. Especially when it comes to how people aged 40-50 and below engage with politicians and opinion leaders. The 24-hour cable news cycle, once the premier battleground for shaping public discourse, has increasingly yielded ground to other, more direct channels of reaching voters.

These include personal channels of politicians simultaneously operating on multiple social media platforms, as well as the consistent rise of influencers of all kinds. And podcasts, both in ‘pure audio’ as well as video podcasts, have gained a significant share in the shaping of our social and political discourse, and therefore in how candidates, grassroots organizers, and advocacy campaigns operate and convey messages.

\-

Where traditional media forces and rewards brevity, podcasts offer depth and an unmatched level of audience trust.

#### Tearing down walls and bypassing legacy media gatekeepers

The defining characteristic of modern political podcasting is creating direct connections with your audience. The ability to speak and engage to your voters without the filter, editing, or adversarial framing of mainstream media journalists. This is not only beneficial to the candidate itself, but in fact very much in demand by the public, too.

On traditional TV or radio, a candidate might get 90 seconds to summarize a complex economic policy before being interrupted. On a podcast, especially if it’s ‘self-hosted one, that same candidate can spend two hours walking through the systemic causes of an issue, sharing personal stories, and outlining policy proposals in detail.

Political figures and advocacy groups now routinely launch their own flagship shows to serve as steady, uncensored newsrooms for their movements. This direct line of communication keeps key supporters informed and aligned without reliance on press coverage or media filters. Not to mention, puts ‘legacy media’ in a position of following as well, rather than setting a direction that is not controlled by your candidate’s campaign plan and agenda. A great example of this is Peter Magyar’s media strategy with constant live feeds of every major events on his own socials- even after the successful elections in April. But the same could be said about Trump’s Truth Social, which he created in response to being banned from Twitter (now X).

#### The power of intimacy and trust

“Podcasting” - whether it’s viewing or listening only - can be quite personal. Consumers typically listen through headphones while commuting, exercising, or doing household chores. This dynamic creates what media researchers term parasocial intimacy: a feeling of close connection between the listener and the host.

While traditional broadcast media achieves high reach, it often operates (especially lately) with low interpersonal trust, resulting in lower persuasion and conversion rates. Niche and cultural podcasts work in reverse: they offer highly targeted reach which numerically create a less wider but more targeted reach but in return achieves high intimacy and trust, which drives significantly more impactful mobilization. Long-form conversations naturally build trust because scripted talking points fall away over an hour or two, forcing a level of raw, unpolished authenticity that a five-minute TV segment can never replicate.

In social activism and political persuasion, trust is one of the highest-value currencies. When a trusted host or guest speaks candidly for an hour, the listener’s defensive guard might drop more compared to when they are watching a polished 30-second campaign advertisement.

#### Don’t try to persuade them, i.e. how to engage with unaligned voters

One major strategic mistake political campaigns make is treating podcasting purely as a political tool rather than a cultural platform. The most effective activism and persuasion happen on shows that are not explicitly about politics.

Appearing on comedy, sports, gaming, parenting, or industry-specific podcasts allows organizers to reach low-information or politically disengaged voters who actively tune out mainstream news. By embedding political perspectives within everyday conversations about culture, work, or lifestyle, activists can frame their ideas as common-sense solutions rather than partisan arguments.

However, appearing on one or two niche shows won't shift the needle. Persuasion through podcasts requires relentless consistency: the candidate needs to show up across dozens of different shows, covering a wide range of topics and communities. It is an exhausting strategy, but showing up in people's everyday audio feeds over and over again is what actually builds familiarity and shifts mindsets.

#### Two ways to set yourself up for your podcast journey

When it gets to how ‘hit the ground running’ you have two tracks that you can choose from; largely depending on available resources, and more importantly: time, that you have at hand:

**Track A: The "guest tour" model (pros: broad reach & persuasion cons: no ownership of the content and the audience)**

Your first option is that instead of spending the capital and more importantly, the time required to build a massive audience from scratch, candidates go onto established shows with an already established, “built-in” and loyal audiences. As mentioned above: strategic booking allows campaigns to speak directly to specific demographics, such as reaching young male voters through comedy and sports podcasts, or healthcare professionals through industry-focused shows. Furthermore, a single 90-minute podcast guest appearance also creates weeks of reusable content across Shorts, Reels, TikTok, newsletters, and LinkedIn. The negative side of this coin, however, is that you will not have long-term ownership of the content, and in case something goes awry, you will not be able to change or edit the way you wish to do that. Plus, and this point might be even more important, you will not ‘own the audience’, meaning you will not be able to extract clean, first hand data from your viewer or listenership. And as discussed in my previous article: first hand clean data is worth more than gold to any campaign.

**Track B: The "owned show" model (pros: base building & loyal mobilization cons: it requires massive amount of time, and usually larger financial investment as well )**

Your second option is to start everything from scratch. This way though the podcast will become a tool of your own, which can greatly boost your base, and later on mobilisation capabilities. This option is probably better suited though between election cycles when you have the time and resources to invest in a slowly yielding, resource needed engagement tool. Later on though, your owned podcasts can keep your base energized, educated, and ready for action. You also have much bigger input, naturally, on the agenda as well. Your candidates can create deep-dive episodes to break down complex legal rulings, legislative proposals, or smaller, or more niche topics in far greater detail than social media posts ever could. But once again: this can only work as a long-term strategy, which will yield its first significant results months or even years later.

#### Moving from "attention economy" to "intimacy economy"

As the ‘new age’ digital voters grow weary of high-contrast rage-bait and hyper-polished, essentially ingenuine campaign ads, podcasts are a great way to stand out by offering room for human warmth, complexity, and authenticity. For years, political campaigning has been dominated by the attention economy: a system that rewards a high amplitude of emotions, conflict, and division, powered by 15-second ads designed to hijack an algorithm. But these days voters are experiencing fatigue from constantly feeling these strong negative emotions.

That is why podcasts are the flagship medium of this new era. They succeed not by dazzling voters for a couple of seconds, but by inviting them into a conversation. Whether used by a candidate seeking to win over undecided voters or as part of a long term strategy to build a broad and loyal audience base from the ground up, podcasting has the chance to shift political communication from shouting at an audience to having an actual, genuine discussion with them. And that is what voters really look for in their future leaders.
