*Written by Josef Lentsch.*

I have spent much of my career building political organisations, and a good part of the rest studying other people’s attempts to save theirs. There is one mistake I keep seeing in the centre: they assume that being right is a communications strategy. It is not, and Saxony-Anhalt has just shown what that costs.

The calendars ahead differ. France votes for a new president next April, Germany has just held the first of three state elections this September, Spain’s fragile arithmetic means a national vote could arrive before the term expires in 2027. Across the board, mainstream parties keep making the same error, good at explaining why their policies are sound, bad at giving people a reason to care. Their challengers rarely struggle with the second part.

**France: The Cost of Fragmentation**

Emmanuel Macron cannot stand again, and the presidential election is set for 18 April and 2 May 2027. On 7 July an appeals court upheld Marine Le Pen’s conviction over misuse of European Parliament funds, but time already served means she remains eligible, and she intends to run. She is appealing further, but for planning purposes National Rally’s candidate is Le Pen, with Jordan Bardella waiting exactly where a party wants its next leader. Against that, France’s centre and left remain split across several blocs, a left wing primary running through October, a centre still defining itself. A divided message aimed at a unified opponent is not a fair fight, it is a head start handed over for free.

**Germany: The Austrian Turn**

Germany’s turn came on 6 September in Saxony-Anhalt, and the numbers are stark. The AfD won [43.9% of the vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Saxony-Anhalt_state_election), the CDU slumped to 17.2%, and every other party is fighting over what is left. I have argued for some time that Germany is Austrianising, the far right pushing into the mainstream the way the FPÖ once did at home. The comparison only goes so far, though. In Austria the right has sat in government with both the Social Democrats and the Conservatives since the 1970s. In Germany it has not, because of the Brandmauer, the cordon sanitaire meant to keep the hard right away from power. Anyone who has opened a history book understands why.

I am not arguing the Brandmauer is wrong, as some now do, with fantasies of taming the far right by putting it in government. But a second factor matters just as much. While the Brandmauer held, the parties from centre left to centre right squandered their chance to reform themselves, or the country, and never built a convincing story about the future. Exclusion plus incapacity, some would say complacency, builds pressure underground, and Saxony-Anhalt is the first tremor to release it, probably not the last this year.

For scale, [Jörg Haider’s best result](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Carinthian_state_election) in a long career was 42.1% for the FPÖ in Carinthia. The AfD has now passed that mark in a German state. A shift this size tests the foundations of liberal democracy itself, and it is also, uncomfortably, an opening. There are capable political entrepreneurs in established and new parties across the centre. The question is who is ready to use the window before it closes.

**Spain: When Survival Becomes the Story**

Spain deserves close attention too, partly because it is easy to overlook. There is no election legally required before August 2027, but Pedro Sánchez’s coalition has been fragile for so long that an early vote is now treated in Madrid as a matter of when, not if. Governing from a permanent knife edge leaves little room to build a story bigger than survival, and survival does not travel as a message the way a clear promise does. An opponent whose whole pitch is that the government should not exist will collect on that.

**Sweden**

Sweden voted this weekend, and the count is not finished as I write this. With most of the country in, Magdalena Andersson’s opposition, the Social Democrats, the Greens, the Left Party and the Centre Party, led Ulf Kristersson’s governing coalition by a handful of seats. One thing is already clear though, the Sweden Democrats lost ground, in both vote share and seats, for the first time in Jimmie Åkesson’s two decades running the party. That is worth noticing.

But a lead is not a government, and this is where the caution belongs. Andersson’s bloc is four parties that agree on removing Kristersson and disagree on almost everything else, migration, tax, nuclear power. The Left Party wants seats at the cabinet table, the Centre Party has ruled out sharing that table with the Left Party, and Kristersson, despite trailing, says he will explore whether his own coalition can survive anyway. None of that resolves quickly. And weeks of it will not read to voters as a win, they will read as paralysis, which is exactly the story Åkesson knows how to tell without saying a word himself. Sweden may turn out to be the sharpest version of the argument in this piece, a centre that can win an election and still lose the story, because a victory this slow looks, to most people watching from the outside, indistinguishable from no victory at all.

**The Centre’s Communication Problem**

The pattern is one I recognise from a dozen campaigns I have watched up close, some of them my own, and Saxony-Anhalt has just confirmed it at scale. The centre believes its job is to be correct, careful and slightly boring, on the theory that voters will reward seriousness once the alternative proves itself unserious. That theory has had a poor run. Seriousness without a story is not neutral, it reads as an absence, and absences get filled by whoever is willing to fill them with something simpler and angrier.

**Narrative is Part of the Work**

None of this means centrist parties should become populists themselves, and I would be wary of anyone telling you otherwise. It means narrative has to stop being bolted on after the policy is decided, and become part of the policy work itself, present in the room rather than summoned afterward to sell it.

Here is a test any team can run today. What is the one sentence a voter in Saxony-Anhalt, or Madrid, or France, would actually repeat to a neighbour over coffee? Not the correct sentence – the one they would actually say, the next morning. If nobody on the team can answer that in under ten seconds, the policy might be sound and the campaign built on top of it is already losing.

Saxony-Anhalt is a warning, but it is also a chance. There is an open window for established parties as much as new ones.

That is the argument I want to make in Vienna this October, at the [Political Communication Summit](https://www.politicalcomms.eu/), the first gathering Partisan has built around this problem. It brings together more than 500 people from campaigns, institutions, media and civil society, under the patronage of Vienna’s Deputy Mayor, for two days built on one premise, that political communication is a discipline in its own right, not decoration on top of good politics. Europe’s mainstream parties have neglected it for a decade, and are still wondering why.
