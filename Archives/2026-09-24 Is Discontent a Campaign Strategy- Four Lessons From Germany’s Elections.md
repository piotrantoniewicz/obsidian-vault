Germany held two very different elections on Sunday. In Berlin, Die Linke finished first with 25.8%, overtaking the CDU and making substantial gains on its 2023 result. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, the AfD finished first with roughly 38%, while Manuela Schwesig’s SPD recovered enough ground during the campaign to finish much closer behind it than many had expected. Two weeks earlier, the AfD had already won 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt.

\-

Four lessons from Berlin, Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Saxony-Anhalt on turning political discontent into electoral movement.

By Miles Maftean

It would be tempting to read those results primarily as evidence of better campaigning by the parties that gained ground. That would give campaign strategy too much credit. Die Linke and the AfD were both operating in an environment of growing dissatisfaction: economic pressure, frustration with incumbent governments, issue-specific grievances and increasing pressure on Germany’s established parties all helped create an electorate more willing to look elsewhere. Campaigns did not manufacture those conditions, and no amount of clever TikTok content, candidate branding or field organising could have done so.

But neither does dissatisfaction automatically produce a vote for a particular party. The same voter who is unhappy with the economy might switch parties, stay home, vote against an incumbent or respond to a completely different issue. That is where campaigning enters the picture. The political environment created the opportunity; campaigns still had to decide which part of that discontent they could credibly capture and how to convert it into votes.

Berlin is a particularly good example. A housing crisis existed independently of Die Linke, just as broader dissatisfaction with established parties existed independently of the AfD in eastern Germany. What Die Linke did was make housing an unusually clear route through which dissatisfaction could become support for the party. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD assembled a different coalition from voters dissatisfied with established parties, including substantial numbers who had previously voted for other parties or had not voted at all.

That becomes clearer when we look beyond the final percentages and ask where the votes actually came from. In Berlin, Die Linke took tens of thousands of voters from the Greens and SPD while also bringing previous non-voters into its coalition. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, the AfD gained voters from the SPD and CDU as well as the non-voter pool. The same pattern had appeared in Saxony-Anhalt, where previous non-voters represented one of the largest sources of the AfD’s growth.

So the useful distinction for practitioners is between political conditions and campaign conversion. Campaigns cannot choose the economy, manufacture a housing shortage or decide whether voters trust established parties. They can identify which pressures are creating potential movement, determine which voters are susceptible to it, and build a campaign capable of turning that potential into electoral behaviour. Seen through that lens, Germany’s September elections offer four useful lessons about how campaigns acquire new voters

#### 1\. Die Linke didn’t win Berlin because it was good at TikTok

The temptation after an election like Berlin is to look at Die Linke’s social media performance and conclude that other parties need to become better at short-form video. There is some truth in the first half of that argument. Elif Eralp and the Berlin Linke ran a noticeably more native digital campaign than many of their competitors, particularly when communicating with younger voters. The content looked less like political advertising repackaged for Instagram and more like something designed for the platforms on which it appeared.

But copying the format without understanding what sat underneath it would miss the important part of the campaign. Die Linke had something much more valuable than a good content operation: it had a problem that it could credibly own. In a city where the overwhelming majority of residents rent and housing costs have become an increasingly important political pressure point, the party made housing the organising principle of the campaign.

That gave the digital operation something concrete to distribute. The campaign could talk about rent, corporate landlords, the implementation of Berlin’s 2021 expropriation referendum and the everyday cost of living without forcing voters to make the connection between individual pieces of content themselves. Even campaign stunts such as Eralp appearing at a low-cost “Kiezkantine” worked within the same frame. The visual content, candidate appearances and policy proposition kept returning to a recognisable problem in voters’ lives.

This distinction matters because political teams frequently mistake distribution for strategy. They see another campaign accumulating millions of views and respond by hiring another video producer, increasing the content calendar or telling candidates that they need to be more active on TikTok. But distribution works considerably better when repeated exposure reinforces something voters already understand about you. Forty unrelated pieces of highly optimised content may produce impressive engagement numbers while leaving almost no political association behind.

The preliminary voter flows make Berlin particularly interesting. Die Linke appears to have gained substantial numbers of former Green and SPD voters while also attracting previous non-voters. That means the campaign was doing more than generating enthusiasm among an existing ideological constituency. It was expanding the coalition.

The practical lesson from Berlin is therefore not that campaigns should make more Reels. Before deciding how much content to produce, a campaign should be able to identify the problem it wants a particular group of voters to associate with it and understand why that problem could cause those voters to change their behaviour. Once that exists, TikTok, Instagram, posters, candidate visits and field activity become distribution mechanisms for the same proposition rather than separate campaign activities competing for attention.

#### 2\. When the party brand becomes a liability, change the brand architecture

Mecklenburg-Vorpommern presents almost the opposite case. The SPD entered the election carrying the weight of an unpopular national party, but Manuela Schwesig remained a much stronger political brand in her own state. Rather than pretending those two things were equally valuable, the campaign increasingly organised itself around Schwesig.

You could see that decision in the campaign material. Schwesig became the dominant visual asset while the SPD itself became considerably less prominent. The closing “Frau gegen Blau” framing reduced a complicated multiparty election to a much simpler contest between the incumbent minister-president and the AfD. Whatever one thinks about that choice politically, the campaign logic was easy to understand: if voters had considerably more confidence in Schwesig than in the SPD nationally, the campaign wanted the voting decision to be about her.

Post-election research helps show why that mattered. A large proportion of SPD voters told pollsters that they would not have supported the party without Schwesig, while roughly half identified her as an important reason for their vote. The campaign also allowed her to distance herself from the federal government rather than asking voters to treat the state and national SPD as a single political product.

This is useful beyond Germany because European campaigns often have an overly rigid idea of brand consistency. National headquarters produces a visual identity, a slogan and a set of campaign messages, and regional campaigns are expected to reproduce them regardless of whether the national brand is helping locally. From a communications perspective that creates consistency. From a voter-acquisition perspective it can create unnecessary friction.

Campaigns should think more deliberately about their brand architecture. In some elections, the party itself is the strongest cue and the candidate benefits from being closely associated with it. In others, a mayor, regional leader or constituency candidate may have credibility among voters who would hesitate to support the national party. The relevant operational question is not whether every piece of material is perfectly “on brand,” but which political identity makes it easiest for the target voter to cross the line.

#### 3\. Stop treating non-voters as if they were one group of people

Across the recent German regional elections, one of the most interesting patterns is the movement of previous non-voters. Die Linke brought a significant number of them into its Berlin coalition. The AfD did the same in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, and the effect was even larger in Saxony-Anhalt. This should matter to campaign professionals because it challenges the way many campaigns operationally think about turnout.

Modern campaigns are very good at assigning people probabilities. Someone who has voted in four consecutive elections looks valuable, while someone who has repeatedly stayed home looks expensive and uncertain. When budgets are tight, the rational response is to spend more resources on voters who are already likely to participate. Over time, however, that logic can become self-reinforcing: campaigns increasingly communicate with people who already behave like political participants while largely ignoring everyone else.

The problem is that “non-voter” describes a previous behaviour, not a motivation. A person might stay home because they feel alienated from politics, because no party has addressed the issue they care about, because their partisan attachment is weak, because they do not think an election matters, or simply because nobody in their social environment has given them a reason to participate. Those people may look identical in a turnout model while requiring completely different forms of persuasion and mobilisation.

Saxony-Anhalt is useful here because the AfD campaign built several routes into participation at once. Ulrich Siegmund already had considerable reach through TikTok, but the campaign also invested in large physical events, a strongly personalised candidate identity and recognisable regional cultural cues. The Simson moped became one of those devices: mundane enough to feel culturally familiar, but distinctive enough to reinforce the candidate’s local identity. Campaign events then converted some of that mediated familiarity into physical participation.

We should be careful about claiming that any one of those tactics caused previous non-voters to turn out. Election data cannot tell us that TikTok views became ballots. What the campaign does show is a useful way of thinking about acquisition: digital content can create discovery and familiarity, offline events can deepen identification, and field infrastructure can then try to convert that interest into participation.

For campaign teams, the important change is to stop building a single “non-voter strategy.” Low-propensity voters need to be segmented by the reason they are low propensity in the first place. The useful question is not simply how to reach people who did not vote last time, but what motivation could make a particular group of them behave differently this time.

#### 4\. Vote share tells you who won. Voter flows tell you what worked.

Election-night analysis is understandably obsessed with percentages. Parties go up six points or fall eight; broadcasters colour maps; campaign teams celebrate crossing psychologically important thresholds. Those numbers determine seats and governments, but they are much less useful when the objective is to understand what a campaign actually accomplished.

A party can gain five percentage points in several fundamentally different ways. It might improve turnout among existing supporters, persuade voters from its nearest competitor, attract people who previously supported a completely different part of the political spectrum, or mobilise people who did not vote at all. The final vote share can look identical while the campaign mechanics underneath it are completely different.

The Mecklenburg-Vorpommern flows illustrate this particularly well. The AfD gained voters from the SPD and CDU as well as previous non-voters. At the same time, Schwesig’s SPD appears to have attracted some former CDU voters. Looking only at the final result therefore hides a much more complicated market underneath it, in which different parties were successfully acquiring different groups of voters for different reasons.

Berlin tells another version of the same story. Die Linke’s gains from Greens and SPD voters suggest ideological switching, but its gains among previous non-voters show that the coalition was also being expanded beyond voters simply moving between neighbouring parties. For a campaign team planning the next election, those are very different acquisition pools and should not be treated as one bloc.

This is where political campaigns could borrow something useful from growth teams. Instead of beginning with “we need 27%,” campaign planning can work backwards from the additional votes required and identify where they could realistically come from. A campaign might need 15,000 previous supporters who stayed home last time, 20,000 low-propensity renters motivated by housing and 10,000 switchers currently supporting a neighbouring party. Each pool then needs its own reason to move, distribution strategy and conversion path.

That approach also makes campaign measurement more meaningful. Social engagement, canvassing contacts, email sign-ups, event attendance and volunteer recruitment stop being isolated KPIs and become signals within an acquisition model. The question is no longer whether a Reel received 400,000 views, but whether the campaign is seeing evidence that the voter pool it was designed to reach is actually moving.

#### What Germany’s campaigns actually tell us

It would be easy to force Berlin, Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Saxony-Anhalt into one grand story about German politics. For campaign practitioners, however, their differences are more useful than their similarities. Die Linke built a campaign around ownership of an intensely salient urban problem. Schwesig relied on a candidate brand that was stronger than the national party attached to it. The AfD demonstrated again that voters who previously stayed home can become a major source of electoral growth rather than a permanently unreachable audience.

What connects the three is not ideology or even a particular campaign tactic. It is that successful voter acquisition began with a reason for someone to change an existing behaviour. Only after that came the content, candidate, event, platform or field operation that delivered and reinforced it.

That is also why copying the most visible tactic from these elections would be a mistake. The takeaway from Berlin is not “do TikTok.” The takeaway from Saxony-Anhalt is not “hold bigger rallies.” And the takeaway from Mecklenburg-Vorpommern is certainly not that every struggling party should hide its logo. Each of those choices worked within a specific electoral problem that the campaign was trying to solve.

For practitioners, the more useful exercise after an election is therefore to reconstruct the acquisition system underneath the result. Which voters moved? What motivation appears to have moved them? How did the campaign reach them? What gave them permission to switch or participate? And what infrastructure converted that interest into a vote?

Those questions get us much closer to understanding how campaigns actually grow. And unlike another analysis of who gained three percentage points on election night, they give campaign teams something they can use in the next one.
