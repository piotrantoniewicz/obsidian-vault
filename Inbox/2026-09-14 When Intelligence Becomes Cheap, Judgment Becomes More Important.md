When a nonprofit gains access to the same powerful AI models used by the world’s largest companies, the first instinct is predictable: attack the paperwork. Draft case notes faster. Process intake forms sooner. Summarize meetings. Write emails. Fill out reports. All worthwhile and should be done. Yet this may be missing the bigger point.

A century ago, factories made almost the same mistake with electricity. Owners removed the giant steam engine that powered the belts and pulleys and replaced it with a giant electric motor. Same factory floor. Same workflow. Same assumptions. Just a better engine. It took years for manufacturers to understand what electricity made possible: small motors placed wherever they were needed, redesigned factory floors, new production systems, and eventually the modern assembly line.

The breakthrough wasn’t replacing the engine. **It was rethinking the factory.** AI is putting nonprofits in a similar moment. The most important question isn’t which model we choose. It’s whether we have the imagination to redesign the work.

### What’s in This Edition of TEAMS!

- **Intelligence is becoming infrastructure.** When a national provider, a neighborhood nonprofit, and a Fortune 500 company can all access extraordinarily capable AI, the technology itself stops being much of an advantage.
- **Judgment becomes more valuable.** The differentiator isn’t who can generate the answer. It’s who knows whether the answer is sensible.
- **Some of your least tech-savvy employees may become your most important AI experts.** They possess something the models don’t: years of accumulated, often undocumented knowledge of people.
- **Adoption is not the same as capability.** Prompt counts and usage dashboards tell us who opened the tool, but they tell us very little about whether anyone got better at the work.

### Redesign the work. Recognize human expertise. Develop judgment. Know when AI gets it wrong.

**Stop Thinking “Upgrade.”** Thirty years of technology implementation have trained organizations to think in familiar terms: migration, rollout, upgrade, adoption. AI doesn’t fit comfortably into that sequence.

Technologies such as electricity and the internet mattered because they eventually changed how organizations created value. These are general-purpose technologies. Their real impact came when people stopped using them merely to improve old processes and began building entirely new ones.

That same distinction matters now. If we use AI to make every existing workflow 20 percent faster, we may produce a more efficient version of the organization we already have. While this is certainly useful, it is not transformational. The real opportunity starts by asking:

> ***What becomes possible for our people when some of the work that has consumed their time doesn’t have to exist in its current form anymore?***

This is a talent question, not a technology question.

**Everyone gets a Genius on their desktop.** Imagine having a brilliant expert available 24/7, someone who can research, analyze, draft, summarize, and reason across almost any subject. That sounds like an extraordinary competitive advantage, until everyone else gets the same expert.

That is roughly where AI is taking us. Nonprofits, hospitals, universities, startups, and enormous corporations are increasingly relying on the same small group of highly capable AI platforms. The intelligence itself is becoming common. And when something becomes common, it stops being the differentiator. The advantage moves elsewhere.

> ***It moves to the person asking the question, and even more importantly, to the person looking at the answer and saying: “No. That’s not quite right.”***

**The Rocket Scientist in the room.** Ask an AI system to design a rocket propulsion system, and it can produce something breathtakingly convincing. Equations. Schematics. Technical language. Beautiful formatting. There’s only one problem. Unless you’re a rocket scientist, you may have no idea whether the rocket will fly or explode on the launchpad. You still need someone who can review the work and say, “That valve fails under these conditions.”

Now apply that same idea to human services. Some employees we have historically described as less “digitally fluent” may possess the most important expertise an AI-powered organization needs. They may not move quickly through a database, **but they know people.** They notice the caregiver whose exhaustion isn’t recorded in the case record. They recognize when a family’s “yes” really means “I’m not comfortable saying no.” They know when a perfectly logical recommendation will damage trust the moment it leaves someone’s mouth. Much of that knowledge has never been digitized. It lives in experience. In relationships. In thousands of interactions accumulated over years. AI can produce an answer in seconds.

> ***Knowing when the answer is wrong, that AI can’t discern, changes what talent looks like.***

**From Executor to Orchestrator.** If the greatest value frontline staff bring isn’t their ability to complete administrative tasks, our goal shouldn’t be to turn them into amateur software engineers. It should be to help them become better orchestrators. Give AI direction. Challenge its output. Add context. Catch what it missed. Decide what matters.

> ***Apply the human judgment the technology lacks.***

But leaders should resist pretending this transition will be effortless. Even paperwork people hate can provide structure. Documentation can mark the end of an emotionally difficult interaction. Completing a note creates closure. Checking something off a list provides visible evidence that the work is done.

Take that away overnight, and we haven’t simply “saved time.” We may have changed someone’s professional rhythm, and perhaps even how they understand their job. That deserves more than an adoption campaign. It deserves an honest conversation.

**Stop counting prompts.** Here is one of the easiest traps people fall into when measuring AI as a goal: A dashboard shows thousands of prompts, millions of tokens, and rising weekly usage. Everyone congratulates themselves. We’re transforming! Maybe.

Or maybe people are simply using AI to do yesterday’s work faster. Usage is easy to measure because software provides convenient metrics. But those metrics can create the illusion of progress. We should measure whether people are becoming more capable.

- Are staff spotting patterns they previously missed?
- Are they challenging AI recommendations using knowledge the model doesn’t have?
- Are supervisors spending more time coaching?
- Are teams designing better services?
- Are we returning meaningful hours to participants, families, and communities?

> ***This is skilling, and if we’re serious about talent strategy, we should measure what people are becoming more capable.***

### Your Playbook

- **Rewrite the job description.** Look for requirements centered on judgment, curiosity, relationship-building, and domain expertise that deserve greater weight.
- **Name the identity shift.** Don’t talk only about hours saved. Discuss how jobs themselves may change, and give people permission to have mixed feelings about it.
- **Make judgment visible.** In team meetings, ask one simple question: Where did the AI get it wrong this week? Then discuss why. Turn frontline expertise into organizational learning.
- **Measuring capability.** Better decisions, stronger outcomes, new service models, and time returned to mission matter most.
- **Rebuild one workflow from scratch.** Don’t ask how AI can accelerate every step. Pick one process and ask a harder question, applying **First Principles** thinking: If we were designing this today, knowing what is now possible, would we build it differently?

### Closing Thoughts

AI is usually discussed as a technology strategy. I increasingly think that misses the point.

> ***AI is a talent strategy wearing a technology costume.***

Everyone will eventually have access to extraordinary intelligence. The organizations that thrive over the next several years will be the ones that recognize something less obvious: The people closest to the work have been carrying the most valuable knowledge all along. They know the participant. They know the family. They know the community. They know what works. They know when something “technically correct” is humanly wrong.

At AbilityPath, our vision has never been about technology for its own sake. It is about valued friends, honored journeys, and a lifetime of growth. AI matters only if it helps our people make that vision more real. The more powerful artificial intelligence becomes, the more important deeply human intelligence becomes.

> ***Artificial intelligence gets cheaper; human judgment is becoming even more valuable.***
