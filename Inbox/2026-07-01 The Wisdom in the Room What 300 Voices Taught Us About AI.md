### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI & Technology Edition | July 1, 2026

Over a year ago, I started asking every team across AbilityPath the same question: *how could technology help you better serve our clients?* I asked it in staff meetings, hallway conversations, and one-on-ones with direct support professionals who had never been asked to weigh in on a technology decision before. I'll admit I wasn't sure what I'd hear. The conventional wisdom about new technology in nonprofits, especially artificial intelligence, is that you'll encounter resistance, fear, and a wall of “this isn’t for us.” That conventional wisdom was wrong.

What I found, again and again, was eagerness. Our people *want* better tools. But, and this is the part I find genuinely instructive, they want them on the right terms, and their instincts about those terms are sharper than much of the expert commentary I read. I have sat in rooms with consultants who charge more per hour than you can imagine, and I have heard less clarity from them than from a job coach on her lunch break.

### Here is what our teams said.

**First, AI is not a colleague. It is a tool.** They see AI as a tool. Not a colleague, not a replacement, not a magic answer, but a tool in the oldest and most honest sense of the word. A hammer doesn’t build the house; the carpenter does. Our staff understands that the value they bring to a client is human: the relationship, the judgment, the noticing, the patience. No LLM does that. What technology can do is clear away friction, including the mountains of administrative documentation we're required to prepare, the scheduling, and the administrative weight, so that more of our people’s hours go to the human work only humans can do.

> ***“Give me back my time, and I will give our clients the rest of me.”***

**Second, they want to know what's behind the "AI curtain".** They believe in good governance. I didn’t have to sell our teams on guardrails. They asked for them. They want to know how decisions are made, which data is used, and where the limits lie. In an organization that serves a vulnerable population, that instinct isn’t bureaucratic caution; it’s care, expressed as policy.

> ***“Trust is not a feature you bolt on afterward. It is the foundation, or it is nothing.”***

**Third, they want to be taught how to use AI more thoughtfully and safely.** They’re asking for more training, not less oversight and more autonomy, as you might expect. They want both greater skill *and* greater structure. When the people closest to the work ask for both sharper tools and stronger guardrails, you’re looking at a mature, healthy culture. You should give them exactly what they’re asking for so they can do more of the work they’re so passionate about.

> ***“The best safeguard against a technology is a workforce that understands it and how to use it.”***

Taken together, these three instincts (tool, not replacement; governance, not gatekeeping; training alongside oversight) describe something I don’t think appears often enough in the national conversation about AI and the workforce: a picture of adoption led from the front line rather than imposed from the top down. That is not a small thing. It is, I’d argue, the difference between technology that survives contact with real work and technology that is quietly abandoned a year after rollout.

This is where technology connects directly to our plans for the future. Our vision was never to become a more automated organization. It was to extend our care to more people, more fully, without losing the soul of how we do it, to scale with soul, as I keep putting it. Technology is one of the three legs that make that possible, alongside our team and our training. Like any three-legged structure, it fails the moment one leg is asked to carry what the other two were built to carry. Used well, AI doesn’t make us less human. It makes our people more available, more present for the person in front of them, and less buried in the paperwork behind them.

**Human in the loop is a moral imperative, not a technical one.** There’s a phrase many of you have already heard: *“human in the loop.”* It’s a technical term, but I read it as a moral one. The human stays in the loop because the human is the point. The technology serves the mission; the mission serves the person. A person, one of ours, exercising judgment and care, is always the one making the call that matters.

So we’ll keep moving forward, deliberately and with our eyes open. We’ll invest in the training our teams request. We’ll build the governance they want. And we’ll measure success the only way that counts, not by how much we automate, but by how much more of our people’s time and attention we free up for the work no AI will do for us: caring for others.

> ***“We are not building our organization to automate care. We're building it to give our team more room to be present.”***
