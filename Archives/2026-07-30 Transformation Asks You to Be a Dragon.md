Are you watching House of Dragons season 3? (*If you are, give me a signal and we will make an edition just to explore what’s wrong with House Hightower!)*

I am. And the more I watch it, the more I realize I would like a dragon.

Not in an abstract, motivational sense. I mean an *actual dragon*.

A large one. Preferably fire-breathing, emotionally intelligent, and available for the occasional AI strategy planning meetings.

A dragon would solve several problems for me, you see.

It could sit beside me in meetings where someone says, “We have always done it this way.” It could breathe a small, controlled flame whenever a committee proposes creating another committee to explore whether the organization is ready to begin discussing AI adoption.

It could guard the data. I could instruct it to eat unnecessary reports. It could hover over organizations when someone confidently says there is no budget for staff development, yet there is somehow always a budget for an AI upgrade nobody asked for.

A dragon would be useful, yes. *Do you not agree with me?*

Unfortunately, most nonprofit budgets do not include a line for mythical creatures (that I could add to my proposals).

And even if they did, I have done the math enough times to realize buying one would be complicated.

Just for a minute, say, I propose this to one of my nonprofits to get one instead of another AI subscription in the next 6 months. Here is how I see that going – first, someone would suggest we need three quotes. HR would ask whether the dragon was a contractor or an employee. Then we would want a liability waiver. Finance would need to know whether the fire counted as an operating expense or a capital improvement. The board would request a dragon-risk framework. Someone would ask six times whether the dragon had experience in the sector.

At that point, I am sure someone would ask the dragon to submit 5-8 fire samples (I mean, why shouldn’t we take tests and samples? Not like we have any big picture things waiting for us).

A consultant would then be hired to conduct a dragon-readiness assessment.

Six months later, we would have a beautiful report recommending the formation of a cross-functional Dragon Exploration Task Force.

*Still no dragon.*

So perhaps the answer is not to buy one.

And this is the part I want to write and explore today with you - perhaps the answer is to become one.

That sounds dramatic, but stick with me, please. I promise to explain why I am reaching the point where I need you and me to be the dragons.

First, the premise – why dragons, why now? You see, a dragon does not wait for a perfectly formatted invitation to take up space. A dragon does not ask whether its boundaries are sufficiently collaborative.

A dragon protects what matters. A dragon recognizes a threat. A dragon knows when to breathe fire and when to simply sit in the doorway, making it clear that nothing harmful is going through.

A dragon will say no at this point.And for that one “no”, I want to optimistically think differently about AI transformations.

I think real AI transformations ask of us not just tools, frameworks, plans, or inspirational learning spaces, but a much more honest comfort with breaking things and saying no.

---

## Let’s establish the depths of the why behind this ask I am making of you.

### Our first problem at the moment is - we keep trying to purchase AI transformation.

We put it in strategic plans, funding proposals, conference themes, and job descriptions. We speak of transforming systems, communities, technology, fundraising, leadership, and impact.

Transformation is one of our favorite words (at least in the conversations I am part of).

We hire someone to transform the culture. We buy a platform to transform data. We bring in AI to transform the work. We create a working group to transform the strategy. We hope transformation can arrive as a package: scoped, priced, facilitated, and completed by the end of the fiscal year.

But transformation is not something an organization can purchase and install.

At some point, transformation requires the people inside the system to behave differently.

Someone has to interrupt the meeting. Someone has to stop the project. Someone has to question the metric. Someone has to decline the funding. Someone has to tell the senior leader that the new idea is creating old harm. Someone has to say that a process is not inefficient because staff is failing – it is inefficient because the process itself is absurd.

Someone has to break something. Not recklessly or cruelly. Just deliberately.

The problem is that many of us were trained to understand breaking things as failure. We are rewarded for maintaining relationships, preserving stability, and keeping the work moving. We learn to soften disagreement until it is almost indistinguishable from agreement. We frame objections as cruel questions.

We wrap boundaries in six layers of gratitude. “Thank you so much for this exciting opportunity. I wonder whether there may perhaps be a possibility that this timeline could potentially be reconsidered?”

*Again, a dragon would simply say, “No.”*

This does not mean we should become rude, inflexible, or needlessly destructive. It means we must stop confusing politeness with integrity. Sometimes the most responsible thing we can do is refuse. *Hence, be a dragon.*

### Our second problem is -change requires comfort with breaking things, and we are not comfortable with it.

We often talk about change as though it is a smooth transition from an old state to a better one. There will be a vision—then a roadmap, and then implementation. Everyone will receive training, complete a survey, and gradually move together toward the future.

That is lovely on many PowerPoints we have seen and designed (and I admit I am a culprit there too). But it is rarely how change works.

Real change disrupts routines. It challenges authority. It exposes contradictions. It makes some people uncomfortable, because a system that worked well for them may no longer be protected.

Transformation is not only the creation of something new. It is also the ending of something old.

A harmful practice must stop. An unnecessary expectation must be removed. A decision-making structure must lose power. A familiar story must be retired. A leader may need to give up control. A funder may need to accept less certainty. A team may need to stop producing work that has been treated as essential for years.

These endings can feel like breaking things, because that is exactly what they are.

We cannot transform a culture of overwork while preserving every deadline, meeting, and deliverable. We cannot transform data practices while continuing to collect everything simply because it may be useful later. We cannot transform power while ensuring that nobody powerful feels any loss. We cannot transform AI use while letting speed, convenience, and vendor enthusiasm decide what is acceptable.

Something must give.

Our discomfort with breaking things is one reason organizations keep layering new systems over old ones. We add a wellness initiative without reducing workload. We add an equity statement without changing who makes decisions. We add an AI policy without changing the incentives that reward risky use. We add community consultation without giving communities authority.

Nothing is removed. Nothing is surrendered. Nothing is broken. The organization becomes more complicated and calls that complexity progress.

A dragon would take one look at this and start clearing the room. *Hence, be a dragon.*

### Our third problem is – saying “no” is a fault or lacking in our dictionary.

We tend to describe transformational leaders as visionary. They imagine what others cannot yet see. They inspire people toward a new future. They embrace possibility.

Yes, all that matters. I have seen, worked with, followed, and read about those people.

But transformational leadership also requires refusal. A leader must be able to say:

“No, we will not automate this decision. No, we will not collect that data. No, we will not accept this funding under those conditions. No, we will not launch this project without the people it affects. No, we will not use staff exhaustion as evidence of commitment. No, we will not move faster simply because a vendor says everyone else is moving faster. No, we will not call something innovative when it merely produces more work.”

The thing I am realizing is – our ability to say no is not separate from imagination. It, in fact, protects imagination.

Every time we say yes to an unnecessary obligation, extractive practice, or a harmful use of technology, we reduce the space available to create something different.

No creates room. No protects attention. No clarifies values. No is how boundaries become real.

Organizations often say they value dignity, equity, consent, trust, and care. But those words become meaningful only when they cost something. Will we delay launching something to protect consent? Will we give up useful data because collecting it would be intrusive? Will we refuse an AI use case that saves time but weakens accountability? Will we challenge a funder whose reporting requirements distort the work? Will we stop a successful campaign because the storytelling is dehumanizing?

---

## So, say, you and I became this dragon. Now, what should we break?

- **We could begin with the belief that all work must continue simply because it already exists.** Every organization has processes that became permanent without anyone deciding to keep them. Reports are produced because they were requested once, six years ago. Meetings exist because someone created a recurring calendar invitation and nobody knows who has the authority to remove it. Data is collected because the database contains a field. Approvals multiply because one mistake happened in 2019. Staff maintain systems nobody trusts because replacing them feels overwhelming. These things begin to appear inevitable. They are not.
- **We should break the belief that more information always creates better decisions.** Many nonprofits are drowning in data and starving for understanding. We collect more than we can analyze, retain more than we can protect, and request more than communities should reasonably be expected to provide. AI now makes it possible to process more information, which could tempt us to collect even more. But the ability to analyze everything is not a moral obligation to gather everything.
- **We should break the reflex to solve every organizational problem with individual resilience.** When people are exhausted, we offer time-management training. When they are overwhelmed, we introduce a productivity tool. When they are anxious about AI, we teach prompting. But sometimes people are overwhelmed because the workload is impossible, and we must learn to read that energy. Sometimes the problem is not that staff need to adapt. It’s simply that the organization needs to stop.
- **We should break the idea that speed is evidence of leadership.** Fast decisions can feel decisive. Rapid adoption can look innovative. Urgency can create energy. It can also prevent people from noticing what they are building.

*Just so you did not know about dragons already, let me enlighten you - a dragon is not constantly moving. It spends a surprising amount of time sitting on a pile of gold, rocks, or something really big and meaningful. Perhaps doing some strategic reflection while considering the inevitable hard question – “what is for dinner today”. Perhaps the dragon understands that possessing power does not mean using it every five minutes.*

## If you are convinced enough, let’s ask what threatens our dragon-a-bility.

If you are half as motivated reading this as I am writing it, chances are you are already thinking about being one.

*Good! Get your pens, sketch pens, and chart papers out. We are mapping transformations.*

Know that we will be met with challenges and threats.

- The first threat is our desire to be liked. Liked by our years-long built relationships. Yes, that is a strength. But the desire to preserve every relationship can turn into an avoidance of necessary conflict. We say yes to timelines we cannot meet. We accept work outside our scope. We keep partnerships that no longer align. We avoid naming harm because the person causing it has good intentions. *Another dragon lesson for you – they are not universally liked. They are respected. Occasionally feared. Often misunderstood. But nobody asks a dragon to join a working group and then ignores everything it says.*
- The second threat is our collective worship for scarcity. Scarcity convinces organizations that they cannot risk saying no. We cannot decline the grant. We cannot challenge the board member. We cannot stop collecting this data. We cannot question the donor. We cannot slow down. Scarcity makes every boundary feel dangerous. But saying yes to everything does not eliminate scarcity. It often deepens it, by spreading thin people, attention, and integrity across too many obligations.
- The third threat is some twisted mix of professionalism with perfectionism. That is a code for maintaining comfort. To be measured, diplomatic, constructive…no matter the cost. We are reminded - do not become emotional, bring solutions, choose the right time and the correct tone. These expectations sound reasonable until they become a way to delay every difficult truth.
- The final threat is the belief that being a dragon means doing everything alone. It does not. Even dragons need allies.

A single person cannot permanently hold back a system designed to restore itself. Individual courage matters, but collective permission is stronger. What if teams agreed together on what they would no longer tolerate? What if leaders publicly protected staff who raised concerns? What if boards rewarded thoughtful refusal? What if funders celebrated organizations for stopping ineffective work? What if communities had real power to say no to how their stories and data were used?

*One dragon is impressive. A sky full of them changes the weather.*

---

## So, the final question becomes – what will it take to be that mighty and beautiful dragon?

Most of us will not receive a formal invitation to transform our organizations. There will be no memo saying:

*“You now have permission to question every inherited assumption. Please identify unnecessary systems, challenge concentrated power, and decline work that contradicts our values. Fire-breathing is authorized between 9:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m.”*

Transformation (AI or not) rarely arrives with that level of clarity. Sometimes we must grant ourselves the permission we are waiting to receive.

Permission to ask the question that changes the room. Permission to say the work is not working. Permission to stop confusing endurance with commitment. Permission to protect what matters, even when protection is inconvenient. Permission to break something that should never have become permanent.

It will also take imagination. Breaking things is not the destination. The dragon is not here to burn the entire village and then congratulate itself on being innovative.

We break what prevents something better from being built. We say no so that a more meaningful yes becomes possible. We end work that drains people so they can do work that matters. We challenge certainty so our staff and communities can shape the future. We protect the slow, relational, deeply human work that cannot be automated without becoming something else.

The sad truth is - we may never get to buy a dragon. *The HR process alone would be unbearable.*

But perhaps we do not need to.

Perhaps the dragon can be you who refuses to let urgency make every decision. Or your team that stops producing what nobody needs. Or the leader in and through you who protects dissent. Or your board member who asks who carries the risk. Or maybe your organization that can explain not only what it will do, but what it will not do.

If we want AI transformations, we must understand something very basic, very clearly: change requires imagination, but it also requires boundaries. It requires fire. It requires people willing to stand at the entrance to the future and say: “Not everything gets to come with us, and we are okay with it.”

So, break the unnecessary process. Say no to protect the good yeses, and the people who are courageously raising their hands for the mission every day.

Create the space that something more humane will need.

And when someone asks where the dragon is, try not to look behind you.

***I believe in you enough to say – it is you.***
