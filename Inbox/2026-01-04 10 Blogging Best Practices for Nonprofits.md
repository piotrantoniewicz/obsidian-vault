With more than 600 million blogs worldwide, many nonprofits may think that the internet does not need another blog, but that’s not the case. Brands that blog report [97% more links to their website and 434% more indexed pages in search engines](https://www.nptechforgood.com/101-best-practices/blogging-statistics-for-nonprofits/). New, timely content is more vital to the success of your nonprofit’s digital marketing and fundraising strategy than it has ever been.

**First,** blogging allows your nonprofit to have a consistent source of new content to feature in your email newsletter and to share on social media. News articles hosted on your website can serve the same function, provided they are written as a news story, not a press release.

**Second,** search engines are constantly searching the web to index fresh content. Nonprofits that regularly post new blog content with [keywords](https://backlinko.com/hub/seo/seo-keywords) in the title are much more likely to get indexed by search engines, especially Google.

**Third,** large language models, such as [Claude](https://claude.com/solutions/nonprofits), Gemini, and ChatGPT, also index the internet in search of content to train their models and to display sources in their search results. Consistently publishing new content to your blog and website is essential to a successful [generative engine optimization (GEO)](https://searchengineland.com/what-is-generative-engine-optimization-geo-444418) strategy.

**Fourth,** as discussed in [Email Marketing Best Practices for Nonprofits](http://www.nptechforgood.com/2020/02/12/10-email-marketing-best-practices-for-nonprofits/), a pop-up on your blog posts can quickly grow your email list. If your nonprofit is not making a concerted effort to grow your email list in 2026, then online fundraising success will remain elusive.

## 1) Understand and embrace modern blogging.

Launched in 1999, [Blogger](https://www.blogger.com/about/) was the first blogging platform, ushering in the beginning of the Social Web. For the first time, readers could comment publicly on a piece of online content. At the time, it was a transformative concept, whereas today our daily online experience is one based on public commentary and feedback.

The blogging of the past was primarily editorial content, such as an opinion piece by an executive director. Blogs were most often written in first person, more than 1,000 words in length, and the accepted best practice was that blog posts needed to be published at least every other day. Blog comments were also taken very seriously.

Today’s blogging is much different. Most blog posts are now written by multiple staff or guest bloggers with the occasional editorial piece written by an executive staff member. A nonprofit can choose to blog once a week or every other week, and posts can be as little as 250 words—and most nonprofits have turned off comments since the online conversation has shifted to social media.

The primary purpose of blogging today is to share news updates and tell the stories of your nonprofit, rather than the opinions of your executive leadership. To demonstrate this evolution in blogging, [Oceana was an early adopter of blogging](https://oceana.org/blog/) in the early 2000s. Today, their blog primarily focuses on news updates and stories written by multiple program staff, with occasional editorial pieces written by executive staff. Their posts retain the defining characteristics of blogs, namely a published date and an attributed author, but blog posts are rarely written in first person.

## 4) Feature “Popular Posts.”

Once a blog post makes its way into the “Popular” module featured in the right bar of Nonprofit Tech for Good, it consistently receives more traffic than other blog posts, even if the post is months old.

Ensure that your nonprofit is also increasing page views and user engagement on your blog by using a [“Popular” posts plugin](https://www.wpbeginner.com/plugins/5-best-popular-posts-plugins-for-wordpress/) on your blog and/or news section. At the end of your posts, you can also include “ [Related Posts](https://www.wpbeginner.com/plugins/5-best-related-posts-plugins-for-wordpress/) ” or a large, prominent call-to-action.

## 5) Write and format blog content for easy reading.

Visitors to your blog are unlikely to read your blog posts if they find the content difficult to mentally and visually process. Here are some basic best practices for formatting blog content:

- Write short paragraphs
- Break up text with bold, large headings
- Use bold to highlight key text and hyperlinks
- Use bullet points or numbers to break up long sections of text
- Feature important quotes in a large, bold font
- Embed images and videos that are consistent in size
- [Utilize internal linking](https://www.semrush.com/blog/internal-links/) to improve SEO

## 6) Share the impact of your nonprofit.

Supporters and donors want to hear how your organization is making an impact. Today’s email Inboxes and social media feeds are flooded with negativity, so blog posts about your nonprofit’s positive impact stand out and can inspire readers to give, get more involved, and result in increased engagement on social media.

The [Second Harvest Food Bank of Northwest North Carolina](https://www.secondharvestnwnc.org/post/five-years-forward-a-community-investment-continuing-to-grow) recently featured its impact since opening in 2021. The post clarifies *in numbers* their accomplishments and programs in action. Strangely, it’s rare for nonprofits to share their impact in this way in blog posts — doing so on a quarterly basis is excellent content for your blog.[![Second Harvest Food Bank of Northwest NC 'Five Years Forward' flyer: volunteers packing food boxes and canned goods at the Greensboro Distribution Center.](https://i0.wp.com/www.nptechforgood.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Second-Harvest-Food-Bank.png?w=648&ssl=1)](https://www.secondharvestnwnc.org/post/five-years-forward-a-community-investment-continuing-to-grow)

## 7) Feature the stories of individuals served, staff, and volunteers.

Whether in the form of a guest post, an interview, or a feature story, highlighting individuals served by your nonprofit and the good work of staff members and volunteers humanizes your brand. A good example to follow is a [series of stories written by women served by Women for Women International](https://www.womenforwomen.org/our-stories).

## 8) Publish numbered lists.

Most readers scan online content rather than read it thoroughly which is why numbered lists are consistently top-performing blog posts and news articles. Numbered lists also pique the curiosity of potential readers and they often click to see what’s on the list.

Regularly publishing numbered lists is a strategy that has worked well for Nonprofit Tech for Good as our numbered lists are consistently our most trafficked posts. A good example is [30 Tips to Help Your Family Eat Better](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/nutrition-basics/daily-tips-to-help-your-family-eat-better) by the American Heart Association.

## 9) Provide useful, educational content.

Educating your readers about a topic is a great way to be useful and spark continued interest in reading your blog. A good example is [8 Ways to Lower Stress in High School](https://jedfoundation.org/resource/8-ways-to-lower-stress-in-high-school/) by the Jed Foundation. The post educates readers about ways to manage anxiety during high school and is educational to both teens and parents.

## 10) Share commentary and calls-to-action about important issues.

If an issue relevant to your mission is in the news (local, national, or international), write up a summary of the issue, add your commentary with a call-to-action (CTA), and then share your post to your social media and in your newsletter.

For example, this [blog post from Return to Freedom](https://returntofreedom.org/update-1240-wild-horses-captured-5-killed-in-callaghan-nev-roundup/) provides a news update that the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) recently captured and killed 1,240 wild horses in Nevada and killed five during the first week of its largest-ever helicopter roundup. The author explains that fertility control is a better solution than roundups and that the BLM is destroying wild herds to allow livestock grazing. Prominently featured is a “How to Help: Send a Message to Congress” CTA which links to a petition where supporters can email their government representatives urging them to stop the BLM roundups.
