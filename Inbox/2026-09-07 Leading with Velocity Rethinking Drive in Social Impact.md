### TEAMS! Newsletter | Talent Edition | September 7, 2026

Thirty years in technology teaches you a very specific rhythm. It’s a drumbeat built on fast decision cycles, clear hierarchies, and metrics everyone in the room agrees on: revenue goals, quarterly product releases, and the bottom line. You push your teams hard and pay them well for their intensity because a definitive finish line is always in sight. A product ships, bonuses are paid, and the cycle resets.

Ten years ago, I stepped out of tech and into the nonprofit sector. I quickly learned that social services run on a different engine and a different cadence. The finish line doesn't really exist as it does in tech; resources are perennially tight, and the currency isn't financial; it's the mission. Yet early on, I wrestled with a persistent leadership challenge: How hard can, and should, I push? In an underfunded sector where staff carries the heavy emotional weight of direct-service work, how do you balance the drive for operational excellence with the real threat of compassion fatigue?

### The Shift to Mission as “Currency”

In the corporate world, directness and high speed are celebrated as efficiency. In a nonprofit, that same directness can easily be misread as impatience with the complex, human realities of the work. Nonprofits run on distributed authority; you constantly balance board alignment, funder expectations, and staff who intentionally chose this work because it was mission-focused and not corporate.

When you push hard on pace without context, direct-service staff, who often view mission commitment as the true currency, may hear an unsettling message: that you value output over the profound human care they provide every day. My positional authority as an executive was real, but I soon realized I had to earn my influence relationally, especially with veteran social service team members who had been doing the work for many years.

> ***The breakthrough wasn't dialing down my drive; it was learning to wire that drive differently.***

### Translating Pace into Purpose

The personal challenge for any leader transitioning into social impact work is translating corporate urgency into mission language. In tech, telling a team, “We need to hit this milestone two weeks sooner,” works because the financial and operational incentives are clear. In social services, that same phrase can sound cold and impersonal.

Instead, reframe the “push” through the lens of community impact. Reframe the directive: "The families waiting for our services need us to streamline this process so we can reach them sooner." The urgency remains the same, but the “currency” shifts from corporate-speak to a focus on human care.

Similarly, top-down announcements rarely build lasting momentum in a mission-driven culture. While a tech executive might deploy teams and resources to a new project with little warning, a nonprofit leader must socialize the "why" with key influencers, board members, program directors, community members, and long-tenured staff long before issuing a directive. Building consensus beforehand ensures that when the expectation is set, it is seen as collaborative leadership rather than a decree. What I have experienced is that when we engage and align sincerely, involving people in the process, it is a very powerful force for good. I wish I had learned the power of true listening earlier in my tech career.

Clarity as an Act of Care

One of the most delicate balances in managing social impact teams is distinguishing pace from tone. Compassion fatigue is a real risk when dedicated people pour their emotional energy, heart, and soul into solving real human challenges day after day. However, backing off on clear expectations doesn’t necessarily protect a team from burnout; ambiguity often creates more anxiety than high expectations.

You can be direct about deadlines and operational expectations while remaining deeply attuned to the emotional side of the work. Clear standards are a gift to a team. Data can also become a shared language for progress. When outcome metrics are framed by the question, "Are we serving our community as effectively as we possibly can?" rather than used as a personal scorecard, staff lean into accountability with pride because it is person-centered and mission-focused.

We also need to be honest about our discomfort with slowness. Being candid about this internal struggle, for example, by saying, "I know my instinct is to move faster than our current structure or funding allows, and I'm actively working to balance my impatience with our team’s capacity and our culture," builds trust. It is a personal statement that says I hear you and understand the personal nature of the important work we do together.

### Finding Balance

In tech, rewards are immediate, quantifiable, and often tied to a single moment. In social impact work, rewards are unquantifiable, quiet, and last a lifetime. Navigating this difference isn't about choosing between empathy and achievement. It is about translating individual drive into a shared, mission-driven urgency and striving for impact while honoring the extraordinary hearts doing the work.
