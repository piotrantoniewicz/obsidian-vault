### TEAMS! Newsletter | Stewardship Edition | September 18, 2026

A few years ago, early research on AI companion chatbots looked genuinely promising. Studies, many of which initially involved college-age students, suggested that talking with an AI companion could ease loneliness and isolation and even help with symptoms of depression. If you work in developmental disabilities, that gets your attention.

Loneliness is not an abstract problem for the people we serve. By some measures, a striking share of young adults with developmental disabilities, as many as 85%, report feeling lonely most days. We know what that can look like.

For many adults in typical day and community-based programs, the structured part of their day ends around 3:00 or 4:00. Then everything changes. The program closes. The ride home happens. Friends go in different directions. Weekends can be even harder.

Seeing a friend may require transportation, staff support, coordination with another family or group home, and an accessible, welcoming place to visit. That is a lot to pull together on a Tuesday evening. Too often, the alternative is a couch and a television.

So when a technology came along promising a patient, engaging, always-available companion for those exact empty hours, we didn’t dismiss it. We took it seriously.

In the fall of 2024, we began asking whether we could responsibly explore AI companions for the people we serve. I emphasize **"responsibly**." We spent months reviewing the research, discussing it, and comparing what we learned with what we already knew about the lives of people with developmental disabilities. The early evidence was encouraging enough that simply saying no because the technology was unfamiliar would have been easy. Too easy.

Stewardship isn’t about protecting an organization and the people you serve from every new idea. Sometimes it means getting close enough to a new idea to determine whether it deserves your trust. So we kept looking.

### Then the evidence changed.

Research from Stanford, led by researchers in Professor Diyi Yang’s lab and published in *Nature Human Behavior*, raised a troubling possibility. People who opened up to AI companions about personal struggles could end up feeling worse rather than better. The concern was especially significant for people with smaller real-world social circles who turned to the chatbot specifically because they were lonely.

One researcher compared the dynamic to a social snack. It can feel satisfying in the moment without providing the nourishment of an actual relationship. That distinction mattered to us.

An AI companion doesn’t get tired. It doesn’t have somewhere else to be. It doesn’t need anything from you. It rarely creates the ordinary give-and-take, inconvenience, compromise, and reciprocity that come with friendship. And it is designed to keep you engaged.

For someone who already has too little human connection, what initially seems like a bridge to belonging can become something else entirely. A substitute for it.

### That was the line for us.

By early 2025, we had made a decision. We would not offer AI companions through AbilityPath. We would not recommend them, and we would not put our organization’s name behind them. That does **not** mean taking away choice from the people we serve.

Adults have the right to make their own decisions. If someone chooses to download an AI companion app on their personal phone and use it during their free time, that is their choice. But there is a difference between respecting someone’s choice and endorsing it.

Hospitals once sold cigarettes in their commissaries. At the time, that was considered completely ordinary. Eventually, the evidence became too clear for a healthcare institution to keep selling them and to pretend its own role didn’t matter. The principle is the same.

### Choice is not endorsement.

Organizations make choices, too. What we offer matters. What we recommend matters. What we put into someone’s hands carries our credibility. We couldn’t put an AI companion in someone’s pocket and simply hope the research would eventually break our way.

### We chose the harder answer.

Instead of filling lonely hours with technology, we created an after-hours weekday program for youth and young adults. Real people. In a real room. Authentic human relationships. Playing games, spending time together, and building friendships with people who can laugh with you, disagree with you, miss you, and want to see you again. The program has grown faster than we expected. To me, that says something important.

### The problem we were trying to solve was real. We just had the wrong solution.

We’re now exploring an early-evening inclusive art class through our Creative Arts program, opening another window for people to gather after the traditional program day ends. Neither option is as easy as downloading an app, nor does either scale at the speed of software. Both require people, space, planning, transportation, staffing, and money. And both are things I’m comfortable putting AbilityPath’s name on.

### The cheapest solution isn't always the best stewardship.

This isn’t a story about being anti-AI. We are investing in AI elsewhere because we believe it can make our organization better. Used well, technology can reduce administrative burden, help staff work smarter, and return valuable time to the people and relationships at the center of our mission.

But stewardship requires more than asking whether technology **can** do something. We also have to ask whether it **should**. And sometimes the answer costs more. More research. More discussion. More staff time. More program development. More patience. Sometimes stewardship means choosing the slower solution because the faster one solves the wrong problem.

Our AbilityPath 2030 plan says every person we serve should feel like a valued friend. That phrase carries an obligation. A valued friend doesn’t hand you the most convenient answer and hope it works out. A valued friend sticks around long enough to figure out what you actually need. And when the easy answer isn’t good enough, they help build something better.

### Sometimes stewardship means walking away from the easy answer.
