Industry Voices

![](https://campaignsandelections.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/3dc3ee941a488baefa344af7df21b37c20be713631010c1dd2176ff9505e.octet-stream)

Let’s say Feb. 14 is coming up. If you are a digital director or campaign manager, you already know what’s on the schedule.

It’s a cutesy, branded Valentine’s Day graphic. It has been sitting in your content calendar since January, meticulously approved by three senior consultants who have since moved on to other priorities. It’s safe. It’s “on-brand.”

But by the time it goes live, the internet has moved on to something else entirely: a floor speech that went viral, a gaffe from the other side, a meme format that’s already been through four iterations. Folks in the comment section of your “on-brand” card are mad that you didn’t post about the anniversary of the Parkland school shooting. Your post isn’t wrong. It’s just irrelevant, which is almost worse.

So what do you do about it? Listen to the current conversation instead. Post none of it.

In today’s [fractured, algorithm-gated media ecosystem](https://campaignsandelections.com/industry-news/how-the-influencer-economy-is-shaping-political-creative/), that white-knuckling and dictatorial calendar control is what’s killing brand messaging, not protecting them. That predictability is crippling campaigns’ abilities to actually enter, much less lead, a cultural conversation.

The content calendar has become the institutional crutch that disguises risk aversion as a communications strategy. It exists to soothe the anxiety general consultants and campaign managers feel around digital, not to move the voters you actually need to turn out.

Campaigns have built communications systems optimized for internal approval instead of external attention. Every layer of review, every pre-scheduled post, every sign-off chain is designed to reduce institutional discomfort not increase public relevance.

Under this current media environment, starved by a lack of legitimate funding, a content calendar is a document that neglects taste, cultural discourse and the ability to show up organically and authentically. You cannot make culture and define values if you do not actively participate within the structure you are attempting to mold.

A tightly worded statement dropped into the middle of a fast-moving conversation is a rock in a river; it doesn’t redirect the current, it just gets swept around, ignored, then forgotten.

Why? You are not participating in the online conversation happening *right now*. You’re shouting in a crowded room when no one is listening.

And, honestly, at this point, the belief that a message-tested press release or generic piece of digital content can enter a fast-moving conversation and meaningfully shape it is a proud declaration of cultural incompetence.

Under this new era of digital marketing, the function of an effective communications team must shift from broadcasting messages to actively participating in culture because today’s online audiences do not trust polish, they trust presence. Participating in cultural dialogue is no longer an option because silence reads as conciliatory. Audiences increasingly interpret absence as a decision rather than neutrality.

Conflict is not going away and neither are the demands for honest leadership for those looking to make an impact at scale. Voters are not just watching what you say about trending topics. They are watching whether you say anything at all.

Leaders face a clear choice: cede the narrative to bad actors with no strategy and no credibility, or use this moment to speak to the values and issues that will define this era.

When your campaign content calendar acts as a dictator rather than a collaborative guide, you are a modern day French dilletante: “There go my people. I must find out where they are going so I can lead them.”

That’s the trap. The crowd has already moved on to the next conversation while your calendar is still executing last week’s plan, and by the time your scheduled post goes live, you’re not leading anyone. You’re chasing a protest that already turned the corner.

In past campaign-cycle audits, we’ve seen how a reliance on rigidity is why progressive campaigns are getting clobbered in the organic earned media space by right-wing populist accounts that move at the speed of the internet.

Los Angeles based digital director and co-founder of the LA Pizza Alliance David Turkell put it well: “When your campaign starts to pander to an imaginary audience, your real audience tends to lose faith. Always tie your social media to your core message so you can empower your team and give them the tools to spread it.”

Innovators show up in the moment, respond to what’s actually happening, and let the conversation shape the message. Not the other way around.

Zohran Mamdani’s social accounts exploded because he was filmed outside. He walked the length of Manhattan and had [block by block conversations with local creators](https://www.prweek.com/article/1924326/zohran-mamdani-proved-power-earned-media-brands-listening). While it was a detailed, well-executed online to offline event, the production quality signaled that he isn’t performing authenticity. He’s living it.

Mamdani didn’t win because he had a sleek digital grid. He won because his digital team treated the internet like a real-life organizing space, capturing authentic friction on the ground.

No calendar produces that. A calendar can’t tell you which block to walk down or which conversation is worth having. Only presence can.

The message has to be worth spreading before you pay someone to spread it. That’s true whether you’re running a Senate race or a streaming show.

While data-driven decision making in marketing is inarguably important, it is not the hill worth dying on according to Kareem Rahma from Subway Takes. In a recent conversation with AdWeek, Kareem nonchalantly said that he has never looked at his data. Not once. And that the data is good because the show is good. Not the other way around.

Translate that into campaign terms: Digital communication is most impactful when the candidate is actually saying something worth repeating. You cannot amplify a messenger that doesn’t have a clear voice yet. You’ll just spend more money saying the wrong thing to more people, faster. A candidate with a stump speech nobody would repeat unprompted has a message problem, not a distribution problem, and no amount of paid ad spend fixes that.

Subway Takes is proof of what happens when you pour your focus and resources into making the work genuinely good first. Tasty, well-seasoned, led by rich storytelling. The data followed because the show earned it. That is not luck. That is sequencing and cultural resonance — two things any campaign worth its salt should be chasing.

So what changes on Monday morning? Two things, concretely. First, kill the stale content plan and replace it with a standing real-time response protocol: who has authority to post within the hour when news breaks, what the approval chain looks like when it’s compressed to minutes instead of days and which two or three people on your team are trusted to speak without a sign-off chain.

None of this means abandoning judgment for velocity. A real-time response protocol without any guardrails is how a campaign ends up explaining, three news cycles later, why the candidate’s account replied to something it shouldn’t have touched. The fix isn’t a sign-off chain: that’s the calendar again, just faster. It’s a shared, pre-agreed list: two or three topics (active tragedies, ongoing legal matters, anything involving a minor) where even your most trusted person pauses and gets one more set of eyes, and everything else is theirs to run with. Speed without any friction isn’t judgment. It’s just recklessness with better branding.

Second, hire and train digital staff who understand your values well enough to improvise on-message, then get out of their way. A calendar can be built by anyone. Judgment, taste and cultural discernment can’t be templated.

To find success under this new digital environment, practitioners must be in the room where it’s happening, let the moment shape the message and trust your people to speak.
