For the past two years, I’ve been working with a foundation in the US to provide free AI access to their grantee organisations. Over sixty nonprofits, all given paid tools through various platforms, all supported with resources and encouragement.

We’re winding it down now. Of those sixty-plus organisations, four were using it.

Four.

I sat with that number for a while. It would be easy to explain it away. People are busy. The sector is stretched. Change takes time. All true. But I’ve been telling organisations for three years that one of the barriers to AI adoption is tool access and cost. That if we could just get paid tools into people’s hands, the rest would follow. This experience broke that story for me.

Tool access is not the problem. Free training is not the problem. Information is not the problem. You can find everything you need to learn about AI on YouTube right now, for nothing, and most people won’t.

So what is the problem?

I keep arriving at the same answer, and it’s the one nobody wants to hear: it’s culture. Specifically, it’s whether an organisation has created the conditions for people to actually use these tools, talk about them openly, experiment without fear, and keep showing up to the conversation week after week.

Without that, everything else is theatre.

## What I keep seeing at conferences

I spoke at one of the largest fundraising conferences in Australia a few months ago. I taught people about projects in ChatGPT, voice mode, using AI as a strategic thought partner. The room was electric. It was voted one of the best sessions at the conference.

And I walked out feeling confused.

Because none of what I showed them was new. Projects have been around for over a year. Voice mode even longer. These weren’t cutting-edge features I was demoing. They were table stakes. The kind of thing people should have been comfortable with twelve months ago.

I sat in on another AI workshop at a different conference a few weeks back. Same material. Prompting, projects, voice. Same reaction from the room. Genuine amazement. And the same quiet frustration on my end, because these organisations should not be learning this in the middle of 2026.

The gap between what’s possible and what most nonprofits know is possible isn’t just wide. It’s accelerating. Every month these organisations wait, they fall further behind. Not only because the technology is moving too fast, but because nobody inside their organisation is creating the conditions for people to keep up.

## The leadership problem nobody wants to name

At that conference, someone asked me a question I’ve heard versions of a dozen times now: “What do we do when our leadership isn’t prioritising this?”

I didn’t have a good answer. I still don’t.

You can suggest they talk to the board. You can point out that funders are starting to ask about AI capability. You can make the case that a newer, leaner organisation could emerge tomorrow and do everything they do with a fraction of the funding and a fraction of the staff, and that funders will notice.

But if leadership doesn’t prioritise AI, the organisation stays stagnant. One or two passionate individuals might experiment in their own time. They become shadow AI users, getting enormous value while their colleagues don’t even know what voice mode is. And the gap inside the organisation mirrors the gap outside it.

I had a conversation recently with someone who’d never heard of voice mode. Not because they’re not smart or curious. Because their organisation had never created the space for them to discover it. I’d take that as a failure of the organisation, not the individual. Voice mode is a feature. How you use it has to be figured out by each person. But if you don’t even know it exists, you can’t walk down that path.

This is what I mean when I say culture is the problem. It’s not about whether individuals are interested. Most are. It’s about whether the organisation has made AI visible enough, safe enough, and normal enough that people can actually engage with it.

## What worked

I worked with one organisation for about eight months. Weekly meetings, an hour or so each time. We went through every component of what I think of as an AI strategy: policy, tool selection, surveys, training, a maturity model, and the culture practices to hold it all together.

What made them different wasn’t budget or technical skill. It was commitment. Leadership decided this mattered, blocked the time, and showed up every week. The team did the work between sessions. They took the maturity model I’d developed, which was deliberately broad, and customised it to their own tools, their own goals, their own strategy. They created something that told each person exactly where they were and what progression looked like for them specifically.

By the end, they didn’t need me anymore. That’s the goal. They had the frameworks, the shared language, the habits, and the confidence to keep going on their own.

I’ve since worked with another organisation on a shorter engagement, focused mainly on policy and tool usage. Different scope, different needs, same principle. Each organisation needs to find their own version of this, but the underlying structure is remarkably consistent.

## The components that matter

I don’t think culture is something you build with a single initiative. It’s a set of small, recurring practices that keep AI visible and conversational across the organisation. Here’s what I’ve seen work.

**A standing agenda item.** Five minutes at the end of every team meeting. Someone shares something they tried with AI that week. It doesn’t have to be positive. “I tried using it for grant research and it hallucinated three funding bodies that don’t exist” is just as valuable as a success story. Maybe more, because someone else in the room might have a suggestion, or you note it and revisit it in a few months when the models have improved. The value is the conversation, not the outcome.

**A shared channel.** Slack, Teams, whatever you use. Dedicated to AI use cases, questions, wins, and failures. It creates ambient visibility. People see what their colleagues are experimenting with even when they’re not actively seeking it out.

**Regular policy review.** I’ve written about this before, but the AI policy isn’t just a governance document. It’s a conversation starter that keeps regenerating. Every couple of months, you come back to it: is there anything we need to update? Any new tools people want to explore? Any use cases that push against the current guardrails? That review process forces the conversation to keep happening.

**The policy hackathon.** Take your AI policy, the document everyone was supposed to read, and give teams an hour to transform it into something else using AI. A song. A video. An infographic. A web app. I keep recommending this because it does three things at once: people actually engage with the policy content, they build practical AI skills, and they have to proof the output for hallucinations. One organisation I spoke with had tools monitoring how long staff spent reading their policies. The average was fifteen seconds. You can’t understand a policy in fifteen seconds. But you can understand it when you’ve spent an hour turning it into a podcast.

**AI champions.** Not everyone needs to be an expert. But you need people inside the organisation who are driving this forward, keeping the audits happening, surfacing new features in existing tools, and holding colleagues accountable in a supportive way. These might be people already doing it informally. Give them formal permission and time.

None of these practices require budget. None require technical expertise. They require a decision from leadership that this matters, and the discipline to keep showing up to it.

## Why information isn’t enough

I want to be direct about something I’ve been circling for a while in my own thinking. I used to believe that the main barrier to AI adoption was access to information and tools. That if we could just educate people and give them access, adoption would follow.

I don’t believe that anymore.

Information is abundant. It’s essentially free. There are courses, tutorials, webinars, communities, entire platforms dedicated to teaching AI skills. I run one myself. And yet the foundation experiment showed me sixty organisations with free access and free resources, and almost none of them engaged.

The missing piece was never information. It was never cost. It was culture. It was whether someone inside those organisations was making this a priority, keeping the conversation alive, creating space for experimentation, and building the kind of environment where people felt safe to try, fail, share, and try again.

In systems thinking terms, information and tools are the most visible parts of the system, which is exactly why organisations default to them. They’re tangible. You can point to a subscription, a training session, a resource library. But the leverage point is deeper. It’s in the norms, the habits, the recurring rhythms of how an organisation talks about and engages with change. Shift those, and adoption follows. Leave them untouched, and no amount of free tools will make a difference.

## The cost of waiting

I keep coming back to the conferences. Rooms full of smart, mission-driven people, genuinely amazed by features that have been available for a year or more. The enthusiasm is real. The gap is real too.

Every month an organisation doesn’t build these cultural practices, the distance between where they are and where they could be grows. Not because the technology is inherently hard, but because fluency compounds. The organisation that starts having these conversations now, even imperfectly, even for five minutes a week, will be in a fundamentally different position twelve months from now. They’ll have shared language, shared confidence, and a track record of small experiments that build on each other.

The one that waits will still be amazed by voice mode at next year’s conference.

I know that sounds harsh. I don’t mean it to be. The people in those rooms are doing important, difficult work with too few resources. But that’s precisely why this matters. The organisations doing the hardest work with the fewest people are the ones that stand to gain the most from building this into their culture. And they’re the ones that can least afford to wait.

If you don’t know where to start, start with the smallest thing. Add five minutes to your next team meeting. Ask people what they’ve tried. Listen to what comes back. That’s culture. Everything else builds from there.
