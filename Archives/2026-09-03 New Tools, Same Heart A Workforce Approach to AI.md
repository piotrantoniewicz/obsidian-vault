### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | September 2, 2026

Every few years, our sector gets handed a new tool or technology and told it will change everything. Sometimes it does; mostly it doesn't, and almost always the truth lands in the messy middle where the real work happens. AI is the current version of that moment. It is arriving faster than most of us can build policy around it and landing in workplaces where the real value was never the paperwork or the process. It was always in the people doing the work.

I've spent enough years managing large teams through technology shifts to know the question that matters isn't “will AI replace our people.” It's a narrower, harder question: what do we owe the people already doing this work as the tools around them change faster than their job descriptions? That's a workforce question before it's a technology question, and it deserves to be treated as one.

### In This Edition of TEAMS!

- **AI's real value shows up in specific tasks.** Organizations getting this right identify discrete tasks worth augmenting, not entire roles worth automating.
- **Shortcuts for new staff become gaps for the organization.** Letting AI handle foundational work before someone has learned to do it themselves trades short-term efficiency for long-term capability and knowledge loss.
- **Augmentation and hollowing-out look identical on paper.** The difference is a deliberate leadership choice about what stays human.
- **Reskilling is a retention strategy, not just a training program.** Staff taught to work well with new tools are more likely to stay than those who watch tools arrive with no explanation.

### Where AI Actually Helps Frontline Work

The honest truth is that AI is genuinely a useful tool for a narrower set of tasks than many suggest. Drafting a first pass at documentation, summarizing a long intake file, and generating a first draft of a family communication these are real time savings for people whose days are already full.

The social services sector has to start by adopting tools that free up time for the parts of the job that actually require a person: reading a family's dynamics, making a judgment call in a coaching session, and being there when someone needs to be heard. The organizations that will benefit most are those doing the unglamorous work of mapping specific tasks to specific tools, rather than making grand announcements about “AI strategy” and hoping it somehow all works out magically.

**Protecting the Fundamentals New Staff Need to Learn.** Some AI adoption looks efficient in year one and like a hollowed-out workforce in year three. It happens when new staff are handed AI-assisted shortcuts before they've built the underlying skills and knowledge those shortcuts are meant to replace, such as writing their own case notes, thinking through their own behavioral observations, and drafting their own family communications from scratch at least a few times before a tool starts doing the first draft for them.

They have to learn the craft before they can be assisted. Organizations that skip this step aren't saving time; they're deferring a skills gap to a moment when it will be much more expensive to fix, usually right after a seasoned staff member leaves, when no one behind them knows how to do the work without the tool.

**Augmenting Roles Without Hollowing Them Out.** Every role in a social service organization has a version that's mostly human judgment and another that's mostly repeatable process and paperwork. AI adoption decisions, whether or not anyone calls them that, are choices about which version of each role your organization retains for people to make decisions about.

Done well, this is deliberate: leadership sits down, reviews a role, and decides what stays human by design because the judgment involved is the position's real value. Done poorly, roles get thinned out, task by task, department by department, until someone realizes the role that's left doesn't need the person who used to hold it. The difference isn't the technology. It's whether anyone made the decision with a greater appreciation for the important role people should continue to play, despite new technology.

**Building an Internal Culture of AI Literacy.** Most staff won't learn to use these tools well by osmosis, and most won't ask for training they don't know exists. Building impactful AI literacy means normalizing questions like “where does this tool help me and where does it get in my way” as part of ongoing professional development. It means giving staff permission to say a tool isn't working for a particular task and building feedback loops that change how the tool is used.

> ***At its best, workforce innovation treats this the same way it treats any other skill-building: as an investment in the people already here.***

**What Doesn't Change: Values-Based Hiring in an AI-Augmented Workplace.** None of this changes who we should be hiring. If anything, it sharpens the case for hiring the person who shows up with judgment, empathy, and the instinct to do right by the people in front of them, because those are the qualities no tool can replace.

Organizations that can't compete on salary have always had to compete on mission and values. That hasn't changed. What's changed is that the case for hiring on values has become easier to make because the tasks AI can't touch are the ones values-driven hires were always best at.

### Six-Step Action Playbook

- Inventory the specific tasks in each role that could be AI-assisted, not the whole role.
- Set a training floor: new staff build the core skill manually before AI assists.
- Name, in writing, what stays human-led in each role and why.
- Build a recurring feedback channel for staff to flag where tools help or hinder.
- Fold AI literacy into existing professional development, not a standalone rollout.
- Keep hiring for values first; AI adoption is a reason to double down, not a reason to hedge.

### Closing Thought

New tools will keep arriving; that part isn't optional. What's optional is whether we let them arrive without a plan for what they mean for the humans already doing the work. Every person on your team is a valued friend first, someone whose growth we're responsible for, whatever the tools around them look like next.

> ***New tools change the tempo. They don't change what the work is for.***
