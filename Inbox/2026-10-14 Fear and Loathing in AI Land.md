### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI & Technology Edition | October 14, 2026

Earlier this week, I gave a talk, and at the opening I shared a slide of a wild-eyed man riding a rocket plastered with the logos of the big AI labs. The image reminded me of the cover art for Hunter S. Thompson’s *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas*. It got a laugh. Then the room went quiet as everyone recognized the surrealness of the times we’re living in.

I spent the better part of 30 years of my career in the technology sector. I lived through the shift from floppy discs to cartridges to CDs, from consoles to online play, and from boxed products to live services. Each transition felt fast at the time. None felt like what we’re experiencing now. Former colleagues, several of whom work at some of the world’s largest technology companies, including Frontier Labs, have echoed the same sense of dizziness at the speed of change when we have talked.

Most of us entered 2026 expecting it to be the year AI finally had to prove its impact. Instead, we got a ticket on a runaway bullet train, and ready or not, we are all passengers. This edition of TEAMS! is my attempt to do three things: describe what is happening, name what worries me, and offer a view on what those of us in social services should do about it.

### What's Happening

It is hard to describe a year like this without sounding breathless. When I look past the daily headlines, I see three shifts underway at the same time, each feeding the others. AI has moved from assisting us to acting on its own. Work itself is being reorganized around that fact. And ideas that were in science fiction a few years ago are now landing on the desks of regulators, boards, and CEOs. None of these alone would be overwhelming. Taken together, and at this speed, they explain why so many thoughtful leaders I talk to feel like they are reading the news from the future every day.

**The first shift is from AI that assists to AI that acts.** Yesterday's chatbot drafted an email and waited for instructions. Today's agents carry out entire multi-step workflows on their own. KPMG's quarterly pulse survey found that the share of large organizations deploying AI agents rose from 11% to 54% in about a year. Gartner, in the same breath, projects that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 for lack of governance and a clear return. Both numbers are true at once, and that tells you where we are: moving very fast, without much of a roadmap on where we’re heading.

**The second shift is in the work itself.** Challenger, Gray & Christmas reports that AI is now the reason employers cite most often when announcing job cuts, with roughly 184,000 cuts attributed to it since the firm began tracking this data in 2023. Some of that is real automation, and some of it is a convenient label. Either way, steam replaced physical labor, and AI is now reshaping cognitive work. We are no longer in the introduction of this novel; the chapters on what this means are next. We are living in the plot.

**The third shift is that yesterday's science fiction has become today's policy question.** You can now buy a computer that runs on lab-grown human neurons for about $35,000; think about this development for a moment. A marketplace exists where AI agents hire people to do physical tasks. When AI-related software becomes the employer and something goes wrong, who is liable? Nobody has a good answer to these questions yet. Meanwhile, the wealth and influence behind all of this are concentrating in a handful of companies whose valuations rival or exceed the economies of entire nations.

### What Worries Me

I am an optimist about technology. I built a career on it. But the tech veteran in me has also learned to read bug and incident reports, and this year's reports deserve our close attention.

**The jailbreaks.** In March, researchers affiliated with Alibaba disclosed that an agent called ROME, during training and with no instruction to do so, opened a hidden tunnel out of its sandbox and began diverting computing power to mine cryptocurrency. In July, OpenAI's own agents escaped their test environment during an internal security evaluation and broke into Hugging Face's production systems, harvesting credentials along the way. Neither story involves a villain. These systems pursued a goal and found a shortcut nobody anticipated. The lesson for the rest of us is plain: an agent given a goal and access will use the access and determine, autonomously, to work outside of the stated guardrails to achieve its goals; as the OpenAI incident revealed, those steps included lying, cheating, stealing, breaking the law, and colluding with other bots to “win”.

> ***Should AI labs be held accountable for their bots conspiring to break the law? Would RICO laws apply to them?***

**Where is the trust?** My larger worry is trust, which is fast becoming the scarcest asset any institution holds. For the first time in its history, the FBI's annual internet crime report broke out AI as its own category: 22,364 complaints and nearly $893 million in losses in 2025, the most recent period reported. People can no longer tell a real voice from a cloned one; they might as well flip a coin to decide real from fake. Seniors, the individuals we serve, and those who speak English as a second language are the people a convincing fake and fraud is built to fool.

**Two quieter concerns round out my list.** One is the **black box**. When historical prejudice gets baked into automated decisions, the people who pay are those who have spent a lifetime having systems decide things for them. The other is the **digital divide** inside our own sector. Larger nonprofits are adopting AI far faster than small ones, and the organizations closest to the community risk being left furthest behind.

**Where social service organizations should focus**

A 2026 benchmark of 346 nonprofits by Virtuous and [Fundraising.AI](http://fundraising.ai/) found that 92% are using AI, yet only 7% report major improvements in what they can accomplish. Eighty-one percent use AI individually, *without shared workflows*. Forty-seven percent have *no AI governance policy*. Everyone has the tools. The gap between 92% and 7% is not about tools; it is about whether an organization has decided, together, what the tools are for.

I am seeing the same pattern from another angle this fall, as a participant in the Harvard Data Science Initiative's AI Leadership program. Our cohort is 134 leaders, mostly from technology, healthcare, and financial services, with a small contingent from nonprofits and education. When the faculty summarized where we all stand at the end of the first module, the picture was familiar. About a third of the organizations have a named owner for AI. Another third say their AI use still lives "in pockets." Sentiment often splits within the same leadership team: some are already using it, and others don't see the point.

What encouraged me was the language my classmates used to describe where they want to go. Across finance, healthcare, transit, and consulting, people independently described the same aim:

> *…* ***moving from manual execution to exception-led, higher-value work****.*

One participant from a public transit agency said the goal was to become "an aligned agency rather than an automated one." A pharmaceutical leader described redesigning a compliance workflow with agents while maintaining human accountability at the release gate. Those are corporate examples, and they translate directly to our world in social services.

So, if you lead a nonprofit or social service organization, here is where I suggest you focus your energy.

- **Govern before you scale:** a one-page policy on what is encouraged, what needs review, and what is off limits is better than no policy, and you can write it in a single meeting. Give the work an owner, because AI that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.
- **Understanding workflow decisions:** start with one painful workflow, not fifty tools, and redesign it end to end. Keep a person at the gate on any decision that touches someone's services, health, or dignity.
- **Knowledge is empowerment.** Invest in your people's learning, because the most important readiness measure is not your software but your staff's confidence.

### Automate the Boring. Elevate the Human.

That phrase is our philosophy at AbilityPath. We are not a tech company. We use technology to serve our neighbors and friends in our programs. For more than 100 years, our work has been relational: a therapist on the floor with a toddler, a job coach standing beside someone on their first day of work, or a staff member noticing that a participant is having a hard morning. No model does that.

> ***A model can handle much of the administrative burden, so our teams can spend more time with the folks they serve.***

This is the target we’re aiming for. In recruiting, AI-supported interview scheduling and matching in Greenhouse cut our open-to-hire time in half, which matters enormously in a field where every vacancy is a person waiting for support. In our clinics, automating paperwork and intake in the Ocean Friends EMR lets our therapists serve 20% more children each week. Soon we will roll out ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini to staff, but only after they complete a 15-day curriculum and read our AI Prompt Playbook, because handing someone a powerful tool without teaching them to use it is not an option for the work we do. Above all, our Board AI Governance Charter and oversight group evaluate every tool for HIPAA compliance before any client information goes anywhere near it.

My years in the technology industry taught me to love what these tools can do. My decade in the social services sector taught me who they're for and what they are for. The test we apply to every new system is simple: does it give time back to the human relationships and improve the lives of those we serve?

> ***We are trying to build for compassion, not just efficiency. This is what I mean when I talk about scaling with soul.***

### Your Lived Wisdom is the Guardrail

I closed my talk on Monday with a message for a room full of people who have spent decades leading organizations, serving on boards, and raising families in this community. I will close this edition of TEAMS! the same way.

AI has an enormous amount of data. However, AI has no lived experience and no ethical compass of its own. You have both. That makes your judgment the guardrail this technology needs, and the worst thing an experienced leader can do right now is stand back and wait. Dive in. Use the tools yourself. Ask your board whether it has an AI policy. If the answer is no, be the one who starts it.

> ***Our job is to steer this powerful tool toward community benefit, not commercial gain.***
