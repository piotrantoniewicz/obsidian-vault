Two weeks out from an election, a late chunk of money lands. I've sat near this moment more times than I can count, and it always comes down to the same fork.

Put it into people- the thousands of supporters who've been asking what else they can do. That means recruiting, briefing, finding someone to lead, and accepting that a good portion will be chaotic. You find out whether it worked sometime after the election.

Or put it into ads. One meeting, three creatives, spent by Thursday, reportable by Friday. And if it doesn't work, nobody can say you did the wrong thing.

They take the ads. They almost always take the ads.

The ads do nothing. But that isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is that taking them was the right decision, not for the campaign, but for the person making it. In reality they had less than a fortnight, no capacity, a board, and a real need not to be the one who gambled.

Nobody in that room was cynical. Everybody was solving the problem in front of them.

Ask any middle manager what the hardest part of the job is. Not one says the strategy. They all say the people. And almost everything the last thirty years has sold us (the polling, the fundraising machinery, the consultants) is a way of doing politics without having to be in a room with any.

**A list cannot want anything.** Nobody removed constituents from the center. We replaced the thing that held them there with something that looked bigger.
