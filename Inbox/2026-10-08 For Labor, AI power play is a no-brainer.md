*This is my op-ed that ran in print in The Mercury today. It might appear to be Tasmanian-focused, but I think it's applicable to progressives everywhere in Australia and Canada. Which side of this issue do we want to be on?*

**Data centre debate allows party to stand up for communities**

Over the past month we have been treated to a preview of the future of our politics, one dominated by AI.

From a hack of the Medicare website by OpenAI agents to the release of documents that confirm a number of corporations are working with the Liberal government to build six additional AI data centres in Tasmania.

Community opposition to these noisy, thirsty and power-hungry facilities is solidifying.

More than 10,000 people have signed a parliamentary petition calling for a moratorium on data centre construction, and local councils are sounding the alarm that our planning laws are not fit for purpose.

Polling by EMRS shows a convincing majority of 54 per cent of Tasmanians oppose new data centre construction in the state. Notably, this opposition is felt intensely, with 32 per cent of people strongly opposing construction and fewer than one in 10 strongly in support. This makes data centres roughly as popular as nuclear power plants.

The Liberals have decided to let data centres rip in Tasmania. They propose no effective regulation of the industry.

Felix Ellis rejected community calls for a moratorium on new construction, and Jeremy Rockliff dismissed concerns as just another scare campaign.

The Liberals’ Draft Statement of Expectations released following pressure from the crossbench is weak and non-binding.

The Greens and crossbench have taken up the fight on behalf of local communities concerned about these factories. Meanwhile, the Labor opposition – in desperate need of a popular position to rally voters around – has remained largely silent, absent some attempts to raise in parliament the issue of power consumption.

But a few questions is not a strategy. A real strategy would see Labor engage fully. It would show up in communities and hear these concerns.

It would openly discuss the risks the industry brings, as well as any claimed benefits. It would organise alongside communities.

Politics is about representation. Voters ask: Who do I trust to represent my interests and take seriously the things I feel strongly about?

The people of George Town, Rowella, Beauty Point and Wesley Vale would be justified in feeling let down by Labor.

Let’s not forget that we are talking about one of the largest state resource allocations in a generation. Firmus alone has asked Hydro Tasmania for 450MW – up to 30 per cent of the state’s electricity consumption.

The six proposals revealed this month are in addition to this, and if approved would require that much power again. Tasmania already has significant constraints on power supply, a key sticking point in efforts to keep Bell Bay Aluminium and Liberty Bell Bay open.

This is fertile political ground for any party or candidate in Tasmania.

Polling shows Labor with the support of 22 per cent of Tasmanians, down from its devastating 26 per cent at the 2025 state election.

If the party isn’t at a break-glass moment today, it never will be.

Data centre construction tends to cluster in regional areas, giving Labor an opportunity to re-engage with voters who have drifted away from the party over the past decade and shake its image as a party exclusively for the urban centres.

Unfortunately we’ve seen this before. Last year Labor voted against a ban on greyhound racing, an issue on which EMRS found 74 per cent of Tasmanians in favour of a phase-out and only 14 per cent opposed.

On the stadium, Labor’s own 2022 polling found that 67 per cent of people opposed construction. It decided to back it anyway.

Whatever you think of the merits of these positions, there is no escaping the fact that Labor consistently positions itself on the unpopular side of key issues.

Why? The two-party monopoly is gone. Labor needs to justify its continued existence to voters, or they will simply seek out alternatives who speak for them.

In 2025 Labor lost its fifth election in a row. While the party admirably takes up the fight on public services, health and education, it cannot by sheer force of repetition turn these issues into vote changers.

We know this, because it’s been the strategy for 12 years, and Labor’s vote continues to decline.

The most important rule in politics is to meet people where they are. Labor has a chance to do that, to stand up for communities, and just maybe revive their political fortunes at the same time.

*Jack Milroy is the founder of Fieldwork Advisory and a former Labor strategist. This piece was first published in the Mercury on Thursday 8 October 2026.*
