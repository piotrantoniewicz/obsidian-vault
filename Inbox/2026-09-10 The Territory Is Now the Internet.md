In 2023, while Montevideo was drinking salt water because a drought had emptied the reservoirs, the Uruguayan government was negotiating to allow Google to build a data center in Canelones that was going to consume \[[up to 7.6 million liters of drinking water per day](https://ladiaria.com.uy/ambiente/articulo/2023/3/data-center-de-google-podria-utilizar-un-maximo-de-7600000-litros-de-agua-potable-por-dia/), equivalent of the household consumption of around 55,000 people. The government didn't release that figure: a sociologist got it out with a public-records lawsuit, after the Ministry of the Environment classified the data as a trade secret. "No es sequía, es saqueo" (“It’s not drought, it's plunder”) showed up painted on walls and on protest banners. At the end of the year Google came back with a project a third the size, air-cooled.

I've been using AI tools every day for more than a year: to write, to translate, to examine public documents, to build tools that would have otherwise required a whole team. I use them, but I can't stop thinking about what they are. Not about whether the machine thinks (it doesn’t), but about what happens to a political movement that decides to use it. The questions are three, and all three are about sovereignty: whose data it is, whose infrastructure it is, and whose judgment it is.

The data first. What goes into a commercial system every time you use ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude is not private by default, and the privacy line depends on account type, not price: [OpenAI doesn't train on its enterprise customers' data](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/5722486-how-your-data-is-used-to-improve-model-performance), but it does on ChatGPT's, paid plans included; [Google trains on Gemini whether you pay or not](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en); [Anthropic doesn't train on consumer Claude](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/10023580-is-my-data-used-for-model-training) unless you authorize it.

So the membership list you ran through a chat to clean up the formatting now exists on a server you don't control, under laws that aren't yours, held by a company whose cooperation with governments can change with the next election. For movements that work under surveillance or repression—and in the Global South that's many—that's an unacceptable operational risk. The fix is simple: what never leaves your machine, nobody can hand over, leak, or cite in a courtroom. Open-weights models on a local machine for the sensitive material; commercial services for what's already public or about to be.

The infrastructure second. Every chat response consumes water in a data center and electricity from a grid that probably still burns fossil fuels, and the costs are paid by other people, somewhere else, off the receipt. A machine in your office puts the cost on your own electric bill, which sounds worse and is better, because a cost you can see is a cost you can decide about. And sovereignty over your software without sovereignty over your hardware is half sovereignty. [Latam-GPT](https://cenia.cl/2026/02/10/latam-gpt-la-primera-ia-regional-abierta-creada-con-datos-latinoamericanos/), the open model CENIA in Chile is coordinating with sixty-plus institutions across fifteen countries, is headed in the right direction. But it's built on Llama, from Meta, and it was trained on Amazon's cloud. We already know what it costs not to control your own infrastructure. Compute is one more chapter in that story.

The judgment third, and it's the hardest to defend because it isn't lost all at once. When I did my bachelor's in graphic design, my first typography professor wouldn't let us touch a computer for the entire first semester. We set lead type, word by word; when we got to the software we understood what it was doing for us—and what it was hiding from us. In an organization, AI can almost always do a task. What matters is what we lose if we take it out of a person's hands. Nobody is going to miss transcribing a three-hour meeting. But the political summary of that meeting—what was really decided, what tension was left open—is the work where the judgment the organization is going to need in five years gets formed.

None of this can be sustained just by what each organization does on its own. The regulatory frameworks are being written (or not) right now, in Brussels, Washington, and Beijing, at tables where the South isn't. But the demands aren't abstract, and they can be won at the level of the country, the municipality, even the building permit: that every data center publish its water and electricity consumption before it gets a permit; that companies disclose what they trained their models on and pay for the work they used, to the author and not the intermediary; and that our societies invest in public compute infrastructure, run as a public service, like the library or the power grid should be. That last one already exists in a lab version: [Current AI](https://www.aipotluck.org/chat), a French nonprofit, built an open chatbot with ten organizations in under two months, running on sovereign Swiss public compute. It's tiny and it's a prototype. But it's no longer an idea.

In Canelones the data center is now almost finished. Smaller, air-cooled, and on the same site. In July, [four neighboring families asked to have its environmental permit annulled](https://www.xn--lamaana-7za.uy/actualidad/vecinos-de-canelones-recurren-contra-el-data-center-de-google/): no longer over the water, but over the noise and the lights. That's the shape these victories take: partial, boring, won at the level of the permit, and they have to be fought again next year. Between waiting for this technology to become just on its own and contesting the ground while it's being built, I'll take contesting it.
