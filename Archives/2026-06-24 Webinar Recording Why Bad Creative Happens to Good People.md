We dug into a ton yesterday — from people and process to generating fresh ideas with tight timelines or budgets or energy levels to thoughtfully using data. I always enjoy talking creative with nonprofit folks, partly because I tend to walk away with new questions to chew on. Thanks to a lively chat, yesterday’s webinar was no exception. A few things I’m going to need to spend more time thinking about:

#### How do you have “process” with a tiny team?

As a consultant, I am never alone. I am surrounded by smart, talented, experienced folks at M+R, of course, and every project I work on has at least one client. So cooperation is baked right into the process, and a lot of creative hurdles (and benefits!) stem from the challenges (and blessings!) of working collaboratively.  
  
But if you are your entire dev team, things look very different. It’s less about working together and more about managing how much work one person can do. My colleague Madeline talked about the importance of triage, and thinking about how much return you can expect from a given effort. Re-using content and finding efficiencies in your program can help as well.  
  
One thing I will add: there are *amazing* freelance writers and designers out there. Budgets are tight, especially for smaller nonprofits, I get that. But if you find that writing or design are creating an outsized burden for you or your team, it may be worth the investment to work with a creative specialist.

#### What do I do when leadership is the problem?

We covered the need to limit who is influencing creative during the approvals process, and a few folks raised the challenges of managing up.  
  
One answer is to test — objective data on what works is always a good foundation! But this is really where the thoughtful, long-term work of building trust and collaboration comes in. Goals, strategy, and creative intent should be part of an ongoing, open conversation. That doesn’t happen all at once, but I think it’s the best way to create space to push back where you disagree and earn the trust you need to take risks.  
  
Or, if your boss wants to do something you think is a bad idea, tell them you heard me say on a webinar that that’s a bad idea.

#### What about urgency fatigue?

One thing we’ve seen over and over again is that urgency drives response. At the same time, many of us feel (or are hearing from supporters) overwhelmed and exhausted by the constant state of emergency. And don’t we run the risk of sounding like a phishing scam if we are always screaming?

I would say, first, unfortunately in many ways we *are* in an ongoing state of emergency with no particular end in sight. If it feels like you keep on having to say that something is an unprecedented threat that undermines our democracy or poisons our planet or restricts our rights or causes widespread suffering for no better reason than greed, stupidity, or plain cruelty… well, that’s kind of how it is? It’s not your messaging that makes 2026 feel like A Lot.

But also, urgency isn’t only about BREAKING NEWS TIME IS RUNNING OUT THE HOUSE IS ON FIRE. It is about defining the moment — showing your audience why this today matters, and how it differs from yesterday or tomorrow. You can get there lots of ways, and remain authentic and honest with your audience.
