### You don't have an open rate problem

If you've picked this up, something isn't working. Results have flattened (or you're being pushed for better results), or they've been quietly sliding for a while, and the pressure you're under is to fix it by sending more, testing more subject lines, or redesigning the template again.

I'm going to save you some time. That almost never works, and here's why.

Opens and clicks are not levers, they're just symptoms. They're what shows up at the end of a chain of decisions that started long before anybody saw your subject line - decisions about who's on your list, whether you arrived in the inbox at all, what you promised at signup, whether the last email you sent was worth their time, and whether your copy gave anything before it asked for something.

## The inbox has two audiences

This is the idea the whole guide hangs on, so it's worth sitting with for a second. Your first audience is the human being. They decide, emotionally and in about a second, whether you're worth their attention. They open or they don't. They read or they skim. They click, or they delete, or they hit unsubscribe, or, worst of all, they do nothing at all, forever.

Your second audience is the mailbox provider. Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo, Apple. And the mailbox provider is watching what the human decided, and using it to work out where to put you next time.

Which means your copy is a deliverability input. Not metaphorically - literally!! When people consistently ignore, delete, or complain about your emails, the provider reads that as a signal that you're not wanted, and your placement degrades. When people open, read, click and reply, it reads that as a signal that you are, and your placement improves.

So deliverability and copy are not two separate disciplines that sit next to each other on a to-do list. They are the same discipline, measured in two different places.

## The 80/20

Five things carry almost all of your result. Everything else is rounding error, and most of the effort in most email programmes goes into the rounding error.

The 20% that moves the needle

- Who is on your list, and how they got there
- Whether you're landing in the inbox at all
- The pre-open layer: from name, subject line, preheader
- What the first screen does with the promise you made
- Your give-to-ask ratio across the whole programme

The 80% people fiddle with instead

- Testing emoji in subject lines
- Button colours and CTA styling tweaks
- Send-time micro-optimisation
- A/B tests on segments far too small to tell you anything
- Redesigning the template for the third time this year

If you only do one thing with this guide, work down that left-hand column in order. It is deliberately ordered, because there is no point perfecting a subject line for an email that is landing in spam, and no point improving placement for a list of people who never wanted to hear from you.

## Step 1: Get in the inbox

Everything you measure is a subset of the emails that actually arrived somewhere a human would look. If your placement is poor, every other number in your reporting is a distorted version of reality and you'll spend months optimising things that were never the problem.

Delivered is not the same as inbox placement  
This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in email marketing. Your ESP's delivered rate counts an email as delivered if the receiving server accepted it. It does not care whether it then went to the inbox, the promotions tab, or straight to spam.

So a 99% delivered rate tells you almost nothing. You can have a 99% delivered rate and a catastrophic inbox placement rate at the same time, and nothing in your standard reporting will tell you. If you have never measured placement separately from delivery, that's the first thing to go and find out.

Authentication is the price of entry  
SPF, DKIM and DMARC are how a mailbox provider verifies that you are who you claim to be. Without them you are an unverified stranger, and unverified strangers get filtered. SPF tells providers which servers are allowed to send on your behalf. DKIM cryptographically signs your mail so it can't be tampered with in transit. DMARC tells providers what to do when the first two fail — and this is where most people stop too early. A DMARC record set to p=none is monitoring only. It reports, it doesn't protect. Moving to quarantine and then reject is where the actual benefit sits, and you do it gradually once your reporting is clean.

Since the major providers tightened bulk sender requirements, this stopped being optional. Authentication, easy one-click unsubscribe, and keeping spam complaints low are now conditions of getting delivered at volume, not best practice you can get around to.

Your list is your reputation  
How people got onto your list is the single strongest predictor of how your email performs. Not your copy, not your design, not your send time. A list built from people who actively wanted to hear from you will forgive an average email. A list built from a competition entry, a scraped export, a purchased file, or a pre-ticked box will punish a brilliant one — because the people on it never asked, don't remember you, and will mark you as spam without a second thought. If you inherited a list you don't trust, that's not a failure, it's just a starting point. But you cannot copywrite your way out of it, and pretending otherwise wastes a year.

Sending to the unengaged is actively harming you  
This one causes arguments, so let me be plain about it. Continuing to send to people who have not engaged in a very long time doesn't just fail to generate results, it drags down the results for everybody else, because it's feeding the provider a steady stream of evidence that your mail isn't wanted. Suppressing the long-term unengaged almost always improves outcomes for the people who do want to hear from you. Yes, your list gets smaller. Your results get better. Those two things are not in conflict, however uncomfortable that conversation is with a stakeholder who reports on list size.

But sending too little hurts too  
Consistency is a signal in itself. Long silences followed by a sudden large send look, to a mailbox provider, quite a lot like a compromised account. If you send four times a year and then blast the whole list at Christmas, you should expect to have a bad Christmas. A steady, predictable rhythm builds reputation. It also builds the human kind of familiarity, which is the same mechanism working on both audiences at once.

## Step 2: Set the expectation

Almost every results problem I audit is, underneath, a broken promise. Somebody was told they'd get one thing and received another, and every email since has been read through that disappointment.

The signup promise  
Go and look at your own signup form. What does it actually promise? Most say something like “join our newsletter”, which promises nothing, sets no expectation, and gives the reader no reason to anticipate anything. Now compare that with what you actually send. If someone signed up for a discount code and now receives three product launches a week, they didn't change their mind about you — you changed the deal. Tell people what they're getting, how often, and what it's for. Specific expectations are easier to meet, and meeting them is what builds the anticipation that makes the next email get opened.

The from name  
The most under-thought piece of copy in the whole programme, and the first thing a human processes. It carries trust, recognition, and every feeling they already have about you. Changing it casually resets all of that.  
Pick one, make it recognisable, and leave it alone.

Cadence as a promise  
If you say weekly, be weekly. Predictability is not boring, it's trust made operational, people can only anticipate something that reliably arrives.

## Part 3: Earn the open

Now, and only now, does the subject line matter. Because a subject line is a promise made to somebody who already has a view of you, in a place you have already earned the right to be.

Being clear & direct beats cleverness, every time  
The most common subject line failure isn't being boring, it's being vague. Clever wordplay that doesn't tell the reader what they're getting asks them to gamble their attention, and they won't. Specific and plain will outperform witty and opaque in almost every programme I've ever looked at.

Four hooks that work

- Tension — open a loop the brain wants closed. “The one thing most email audits miss.” Always close it with substance, or it's clickbait and it costs you next time.
- Confrontation — challenge something they believe. “Your open rate is not a success metric.” Only confront what you can back up.
- Recognition — describe their reality so precisely they think that's me. “You inherited this setup and you're scared to touch it.”
- Specificity — a real number, a named scenario, a concrete detail. Never an invented one.

The preheader is not a spare subject line

It's the second half of the sentence. If it repeats the subject line, or worse, says “View in browser”, you've thrown away one of the three things a person uses to decide whether you're worth a second of their day. Write it every time, and write it as an extension.

## Part 4: Keep them reading

Build the bridge  
When someone opens, their brain asks exactly one question: did I get what I thought I was going to get? Your first screen has to resolve the promise, not repeat it. They already read the subject line. Repeating it back at them as a header is the most common way emails lose people who had already decided to give you a chance.

Answer WIIFM, early  
What's in it for me. If the reader can't answer that within the first couple of lines, the sentence hasn't earned its place, however true it is. Try this on your last send: find every sentence starting with we, our, or your brand name, and rewrite it starting with you. Not just swapping the pronoun — actually answering what changes for the person reading. If you can't answer it, cut the line.

Outcomes, not features  
Nobody buys the specification. They buy what it lets them do. “Our platform has 200+ integrations” is a fact about you. “It connects with the tools you already use” is a fact about them.

Use a [framework](https://weareastral.co.uk/thevault/how-to-write-better-email-copy-framework-and-guide), because sequence is the point  
PAS, the 4P's, FAB, BAB and AIDA are not formulas to fill in. They're forced emotional order — they stop you leading with yourself, which is what every business instinctively wants to do. Pick one per email. One message, one purpose, one goal. If you can't state in a single sentence what you want the reader to understand, feel or do, you're not ready to write it yet.

## Part 5: Make it easy to act

Design isn't decoration and it isn't the thing that converts. Its job is to reduce effort — to tell the eye where to go, to support scanning, and to get out of the way of the message.

The image-off test  
Strip every image out of your last campaign. Does it still make sense? Is the message still clear? Could somebody still act on it? If not, you don't have copy, you have decoration. And plenty of people will see it that way anyway, because images don't always load.

One primary action  
One clear thing you want them to do, expressed as what they get rather than what you want. Not “click here” but “see how it works”. Secondary content can exist, but it must never compete.

Hierarchy and scanning  
Short paragraphs, clear sections, real whitespace. The brain prefers what's easy to process, and if reading your email feels like work it won't get read. Simplicity isn't dumbing down, it's respecting the reader's attention.

Accessibility is a results issue  
Proper alt text, sufficient contrast, readable font sizes, sensible heading structure, meaningful link text, dark mode that doesn't fall apart. This is a legal question in some markets now, but set that aside — every one of these things also makes the email easier for everybody to read, and easier to read means more likely to convert.

## Part 6: Give more than you ask

Here's the pattern I see more than any other. A programme where almost [every email is an ask](https://weareastral.co.uk/thevault/give-more-than-you-ask-the-email-ratio). Buy this. Book that. Register here. Last chance. And then confusion about why engagement is falling.

People do not owe you a purchase because you sent an email. Trust is built through consistency over time, and trust is what actually converts.

Count it, don't guess it!  
Take your last ten sends and label each one: give, or ask. Be strict, an email that gives a bit of insight and then asks for a demo is an ask with a preamble. Most people are shocked at the result, and it's the single easiest diagnostic in this guide.

What actually counts as giving

- Insight the reader can use whether or not they ever buy from you
- A genuine point of view, including ones that cost you something to say
- Practical education — the how, not just the what
- Tools, templates, checklists, things that do a job

What doesn't count: a case study that's really a sales pitch, a “valuable resource” gated behind a demo booking, or a newsletter that exists to announce things about you.

Urgency, honestly  
Urgency has a place, but only when it's real and only late in the decision, when somebody already knows what they'd get and is weighing whether now is the moment. Then a very real deadline tips a decision that was already forming.  
Applied early, it does the opposite. It pressures somebody who isn't ready, it makes the brand feel desperate, and it trains your audience to wait for your last-chance emails because they've learnt that's when the real offer appears. If every email is an emergency, none of them are.

## Measure it

If you take the argument in this guide seriously, your reporting has to change too, because you cannot manage a loop you're only measuring one point of.

Instead of open rates...

- Inbox placement rate — where you're actually landing
- Inbox impressions
- Inbox reach
- Unsubscribe rates

Instead of delivered rate...

Click-to-delivered — action against what was sent

Stop leading with

**List size:**

Instead lead with engaged list size, by recency cohort.  
  
**Total sends:**

Complaint rate and unsubscribe rate as trend lines  
  
**Campaign-level clicks**  
Revenue or conversions per send, and reply rate
