**TL;DR: A website doesn’t need to be redesigned because it’s old. It needs to be redesigned when it’s no longer helping the organization succeed.**

People who know me know I’m a big walker. I’m fortunate to live in a neighborhood with a Walk Score of 92, a true “Walker’s Paradise,” and I take full advantage of it. Every morning, Django and I head out for an hour-long walk, followed by another 30 minutes at lunch.

So what does this have to do with websites?

Bear with me.

I also have a bad habit: I hang onto my sneakers far longer than I should. Even after the tread is gone, I convince myself they still have a little life left. My feet eventually remind me that being frugal isn’t always the same as being smart. Worn-out shoes may still look fine, but they no longer provide the support they were designed to.

Organizations often do the exact same thing with their websites.

It’s easy to keep patching an aging website, adding a feature here, fixing a bug there, refreshing a page now and then. But eventually those updates become less like improvements and more like stitching together a patchwork quilt. Quilts are charming. Websites? Not so much.

Over time, the foundation becomes harder to maintain, content is more difficult to update, performance begins to slip, and the experience for both your visitors and your team suffers. At some point, continuing to “make do” costs more in time, money, and missed opportunities than investing in a thoughtful redesign.

Just as replacing a worn-out pair of shoes can make every step feel better, replacing an outdated website can transform how your organization performs. Sometimes the smartest investment isn’t another patch. It’s a fresh start.

Curious what our team thought, I posed the question in our “Watercooler” Slack channel yesterday:

Unsurprisingly, the answer wasn’t a single number. While most people clustered around 4 to 6 years, everyone agreed that a redesign should be driven by the organization’s needs, not simply by the calendar.

The responses ranged from 3 years to 20 years (thanks, David 😄), but there was a clear consensus.

## The Short Answer

Most of the team landed somewhere between 4 and 6 years for a full website redesign, with many saying 5 years is a healthy benchmark.

But the more interesting conversation was why.

## A Website Doesn’t Expire Like Milk

Several people pointed out that websites don’t suddenly become “bad” because they hit a certain birthday.

Instead, a redesign depends on questions like:

- Is your website still meeting your organization’s goals?
- Is it easy for your team to update?
- Does it reflect your brand today?
- Does it provide a good user experience?
- Is the technology still modern, secure, and maintainable?
- Is it accessible and compliant with current standards?

If the answer is yes, you may have more life left than you think.

## Sometimes You Need an Enhancement, Not a Complete Redesign

One theme came up repeatedly: don’t confuse a redesign with continual improvement.

For example, visual updates can be applied to individual pages without overhauling the entire site.

Another great observation was that your audience doesn’t see your website every day the way you do. You may be tired of your homepage long before your visitors are. Sometimes thoughtful refinements are enough.

## Technology Ages Faster Than Design

Several team members noted that the underlying technology often becomes the limiting factor before the design does.

Content management systems evolve. Accessibility standards change. New development tools emerge. Integrations become outdated.

A website that still looks great on the surface may be increasingly difficult, or expensive, to maintain underneath. We see this all the time. A website may still look modern while the technology beneath it becomes increasingly fragile, like a house of cards, and expensive to maintain.

## It Depends on What Your Website Does

Another person made a completely valid point:

“There are websites that are 20 years old and still serve their audience exceptionally well.”

That’s true.

A simple informational website has a very different lifespan than a nonprofit site managing donations, events, member portals, and integrations with multiple systems.

The more your website is expected to do, the more important it becomes to periodically rethink the foundation.

## A Redesign Is a Business Decision, Not a Design Decision

One thing became clear from everyone’s responses: deciding to redesign a website isn’t really about design.

It’s about whether the website is still helping the organization accomplish what it was built to do.

If it’s attracting visitors, converting them into donors, customers, members, or volunteers, is easy to manage, accessible, and technically sound, then it may not need a redesign just because it turned five.

## Our Take

If we had to give one answer, it would be this:

**Plan on evaluating your website every 3 to 5 years and expect a significant redesign around the 5-year mark.**

Not because the design is necessarily outdated, but because your organization, your audience, technology, accessibility standards, and search behavior have likely evolved.

The goal isn’t to redesign your website because it’s old.

The goal is to redesign it when it’s no longer helping your organization move forward.

That brings me back to my sneakers.

I can convince myself they’re “still fine” long after they’ve stopped providing the support I need. The wear happens so gradually that I hardly notice, until my feet start complaining.

Websites don’t suddenly become outdated. They wear down gradually. Another plugin. Another workaround. Another integration. Another page template. Each change makes sense on its own, but together they create a foundation that’s harder to maintain than rebuild.

Eventually, replacing the shoes, or the website, isn’t an expense. It’s an investment in making every step that follows easier.
