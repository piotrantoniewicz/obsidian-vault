Power

07.06.2026

If you want to understand the new physics of political communication in Europe, start with the simple fact that Google and Meta decided it was easier to pull back from political ads than to fully comply with the EU’s new Transparency and Targeting of Political Advertising Regulation (TTPA).

This is not a technical footnote; it is the end of an era in which the best-funded campaign with the best data profile could quietly flood precisely segmented audiences with bespoke messages.

\-

When the Feed Goes Quiet: Political Persuasion After TTPA and the Rise of AI Search

For a decade, the centre of gravity in political campaigning sat inside two advertising systems. Meta and Google offered scale, precision, and a feedback loop fast enough to test a message in the morning and rebuild it by lunch. That era has closed in the European Union, and most parties have not yet absorbed what replaces it.

The trigger is regulatory. The deeper shift is technological. Together they rewrite how persuasion works, who controls discovery, and what a competent campaign now has to build.

#### Why TTPA exists, and what it actually changed

Start with the problem the regulators set out to solve. Four issues had been building for years. Hidden influence, where voters saw different political realities without understanding why. Aggressive micro-targeting based on sensitive personal data. Foreign interference through covert funding and divisive issue ads. And platforms that wrote their own rules and enforced them inconsistently.

The Transparency and Targeting of Political Advertising regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/900, turns that arrangement inside out. It entered into force in April 2024 and applies fully from 10 October 2025. Any platform that profits from political advertising now carries explicit obligations: clear sponsorship labels, public and searchable archives of every ad, strict limits on the use of sensitive personal data, a ban on foreign sponsors in the three months before an election, machine-readable records kept for seven years, and fines reaching six percent of global annual turnover.

The platforms did the maths and walked. Meta ended political, electoral and social-issue advertising across its EU platforms in October 2025, calling the requirements unworkable and the legal exposure untenable. Google reached the same conclusion, with YouTube switching off political ads in September 2025. The most powerful, scalable persuasion tools of the last decade are now either gone or severely constrained for European campaigns.

This does not just add paperwork. It collapses an entire playbook.

That collapse changes incentives. When you can no longer quietly segment by inferred ideology or personal vulnerability, your message has to be sturdy enough to survive transparency. It has to sit in an archive, be explainable on its own, and stay defensible once it is detached from the narrow audience it was built for. In this environment, a position that cannot be clearly explained, defended, and made credible in public will not survive.

#### AI search: the new discovery choke point

Into that gap steps a new gatekeeper. When voters have a political question today, a growing share of them no longer scroll through search results. They ask an AI. And when they do, they are not just getting information. They are getting interpretation.

The usage curve is steep. ChatGPT grew from roughly 400 million weekly users in February 2025 to about 900 million a year later, and crossed a billion monthly users in 2026, handling somewhere near two and a half billion prompts a day. Traditional search still dominates by raw volume, with Google processing trillions of queries a year. But that headline understates the shift, because AI punches well above its weight on exactly the questions campaigns care about most, the high-stakes "what should I think, and what should I do" queries that decide votes. Surveys through 2025 and 2026 show these tools being adopted faster than social media was, while traffic to traditional news and search keeps sliding.

This is where visibility becomes the new currency. When a voter asks what a party's position is on nuclear energy, or which candidates back rent caps in their city, the model is forced to select a handful of sources to cite, to synthesise, or to ignore. That is an SEO problem for the AI age. You either structure your content, signals and authority in ways these models recognise and reward, or you are quietly erased from the answers that matter.

The two logics now collide. TTPA makes it harder to push targeted messages into people's feeds. AI search concentrates power in models that reward clear, stable, well-structured public content. In that world, a party without real web infrastructure, not a homepage and a manifesto but topical explainer pages, FAQs, issue hubs and consistent metadata, simply will not appear when it counts.

#### From SEO to LLM optimisation

A new class of firms has noticed this, and they are selling what they call LLM optimisation, or generative engine optimisation. The pitch is simple. What happened to brands in the search era is now happening to parties and NGOs in the model era. Companies once learned to tune titles, internal links and schema to satisfy Google's crawlers. Political actors now have to understand how models crawl, embed, rank and cite their sources. The machinery is different, vector embeddings and fine-tuning rather than PageRank, but the strategic question is identical. When someone asks a politically relevant question, do you show up in the answer?

The method is twofold. First, map how often and in what context the major models mention or cite a given organisation across a battery of issue questions and vote-choice prompts. Second, refactor the content so the models can reliably associate that organisation with specific policies, values and constituencies, using structured pages, consistent naming and technically clean sites.

The evidence that this works is no longer anecdotal. A Princeton and Georgia Tech study presented at KDD in 2024 found that adding expert quotations to a page lifted its visibility in AI answers by around forty percent, adding statistics by about a third, and citing authoritative sources by roughly thirty percent, all through restructuring rather than redesign. Research from GEO firms suggests the overlap between the top organic links on Google and the sources AI systems actually cite has fallen from about seventy percent to under twenty. AI search also shows a clear bias toward earned media, the third-party authoritative mentions on places like Wikipedia, sector press and review sites, over a brand's own pages. The commercial results follow the same shape. One auto insurer reportedly grew its AI mentions by several hundred percent in six months, and by 2026 a majority of software buyers were starting their research with a chatbot rather than a search engine.

None of this is neutral. It is an attempt to influence the epistemic infrastructure of democracy. When a model answers a voter's question, it appears to speak from nowhere, but its answer is shaped by a latent map of sources, reputations and signals. By optimising for that map, campaigns are competing to become the default reference point inside a machine that most people treat as a trusted explainer. It is a new kind of lobbying, aimed not at lawmakers or editors but at the models that now sit between citizens and political knowledge.

#### Micro-targeting dies, content discipline lives

Here is the more hopeful reading. Constraints can be productive. Without micro-targeted ads, campaigners have to make hard choices about where money and attention go, and that tends to push them back toward fundamentals.

For years, parties underinvested in their own web infrastructure, treating landing pages and newsletters as an afterthought while pouring budgets into Meta. That is no longer viable. Explicit consent for political processing is harder to obtain and track, cookies are regulated, and cheap reach has gone.

Consider a trade union that assumed its construction-site members would never use email, on the grounds that half of them barely have an address. Persuaded to test a few simple, focused landing pages with a clean email-capture flow, it ended up recruiting several times more new members a month than before. Another organisation stopped burying its key call to action at the bottom of a cluttered page, designed instead around a single testable conversion path, and watched both registration and engagement climb. From a technical standpoint this is ordinary. From a democratic standpoint it matters, because when a party has to earn and maintain a direct line to its supporters through email, owned apps or consented channels rather than renting micro-audiences, its incentives tilt back toward building durable communities. Across many countries, the newsletter has quietly become the most robust call to action left standing after Meta.

There is a class and geography problem hiding inside this, though. Programmatic connected TV and mobile video can still force attention in ways a social feed cannot, with completion rates above ninety percent on connected TV and around half that on mobile, if you can afford the inventory. But those audiences are often the politically engaged urban middle classes who already know your policies. The real challenge is the out-groups: young men in rural football clubs, workers in fragmented unions, disillusioned voters who only meet politics through memes, gaming apps or messaging platforms. In that sense, TTPA forces a painful question. Were campaigns ever really reaching those people, or just retargeting variations of the same base?

#### The gatekeeper problem

The deeper worry is about trust. A political ad can be labelled, archived and audited. A chatbot's answer cannot easily be traced back to its training data and its ranking choices. When a billion people a month ask AI systems who supports what, who is credible, and what is fair, they are delegating part of their democratic reasoning to intermediaries no one can fully inspect.

This is not theoretical. A 2025 Cornell study published in Nature found that even short conversations with a chatbot moved voters' candidate preferences by a few points, a larger effect than traditional video advertising tends to manage, and that the shift persisted weeks later. In the run-ups to the 2025 Canadian and Polish elections, the same researchers measured swings of around ten points among opposition voters. The model that answers "who should I vote for" is not a neutral pipe. It persuades, whether or not anyone intends it to.

There is a way to bend this toward accountability rather than pure persuasion. Retrieval-augmented generation pipelines can feed models authoritative links and citations that challenge a misleading or one-sided narrative, showing the system high-quality contrary evidence, repeatedly, until it stops repeating an error. That is a technical fix, but it points at a broader democratic task: making sure that when people turn to AI for political answers, they are not trapped inside a distorted mirror of their own side's content.

The asymmetry is hard to ignore. Well-resourced actors will hire the firms, rebuild their sites and optimise for the models. Grass-roots movements and smaller parties may struggle to keep up, or may decide on principle not to chase opaque algorithmic favour. And the regulatory system that finally clamped down on micro-targeting has not begun to catch up with this newer form of influence, where the decisive move is to become the featured citation in a chatbot's reply.

Watch where the pressure goes when a channel closes. After Meta's ban, researchers documented Hungary's governing Fidesz finding ways around the rules and pushing paid political messaging into harder-to-track spaces. Squeeze one channel, and campaigns hunt for the next opaque one. The instinct does not disappear. It relocates.

#### The new playbook: visibility, coherence, humility

The new rules are not only legal or technical. They demand a different political temperament.

Campaigns can no longer assume that persuasion happens in private, in ephemeral micro-audiences that vanish after the impression. What you say has to make sense when it is preserved, indexed, and compressed into a few sentences by a model speaking to a sceptical voter. That requires ruthless internal coherence: policy positions that do not contradict each other across channels, narratives that hold up under scrutiny, and a content architecture that mirrors how citizens actually ask their questions.

It also requires the humility to treat AI as a potential critic rather than a megaphone. If your positions are thin, contradictory or backed by weak evidence, optimising for the models is lipstick on a pig, because systems trained on the open web will find and surface the gaps. The end of micro-targeting is, in the end, an invitation to return to something closer to politics as persuasion in public, rather than behavioural manipulation in private.

The open question is whether parties will accept that invitation, or simply fight just as hard for the next invisible channel.
