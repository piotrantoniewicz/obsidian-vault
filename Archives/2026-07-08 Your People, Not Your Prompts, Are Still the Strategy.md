### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | July 8, 2026

You’ve probably sat through enough meetings this year to spot the pattern. Someone reads an article about AI saving a Fortune 500 company millions and asks, not unreasonably, why haven't we “done something with AI” yet. Meanwhile, the people who deliver your mission every day are quietly worried that “something with AI” is a code word for their jobs that may disappear. Both reactions are understandable.

I spent over three decades in the corporate world before I joined a nonprofit, and I've seen this tug-of-war before, just with different technology, still labeled “disruption.” What's different this time is the speed. Two major reports released in June, one from Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI and the other from Dr. Bertalan Meskó's work on generative AI in healthcare, say something every leader of a mission-driven organization needs to hear: the technology is no longer the hard part. What *you do with i* t now is what matters.

### Executive Summary

- **AI Benchmarks are becoming a wash.** As every major AI model converges on the same raw capability, your competitive advantage no longer comes from which tool you choose. It comes from how thoughtfully you integrate it into human work.
- **Automation isn't the goal. Partnership is.** Your best deployments will pair human judgment with AI speed, what researchers call “centaur” teams, rather than chasing full automation of anything that touches the people you serve.
- **Confidence is not competence.** Left unmanaged, these tools can quietly encode bias, fabricate confident-sounding nonsense, and dull your team's critical thinking, all while appearing entirely credible. You need to actively guard against cognitive offloading.
- **You don't need Silicon Valley money to do this right.** Small, secure, locally run AI tools now enable lean, resource-constrained organizations to adopt impactful solutions while protecting sensitive client data without an enterprise budget.

### Five Lessons Worth Stealing

Strip away the jargon in both reports, and you're left with a leadership framework any of us running people-first organizations can start using right away.

***Build “Centaur” Teams.*** The strongest AI deployments know when to act on their own, when to defer to a human, and how to say “I'm not sure” out loud. Chasing full automation in work that touches the people we serve is a race to the bottom; you'll save a little money and lose a lot of trust. The better approach is what the researchers call a centaur team: mainly human, with AI support, each doing what it does best.

Think of it the way you’d approach growing anything worthwhile: the gardener's approach. You don't replace the gardener with a machine because weeding is tedious. You give the gardener a better hoe so they can spend more time tending the plants that need nurturing. Let AI pull the weeds, the transcription, the paperwork, and the data entry that burn your staff out. Keep human hands on everything that requires trust, eye contact, and an empathetic listener.

> *“Radiologists who use AI will replace radiologists who don't.”* **— Dr. Curt Langlotz, Stanford Center for Artificial Intelligence in Medicine and Imaging**

***Carefully Review What You're Feeding AI.*** Data strategy is AI strategy. Left unchecked, the data behind most mainstream models skews heavily toward affluent, English-speaking, Western experience. Feed that into your operations without a second look, and you risk automating your inherent biases while calling it “innovation”. If your organization serves anyone outside that narrow model default, different language, different culture, different ability, different zip code, you owe it to them to check what your tools were trained on before you trust what they tell you.

> *“Human-centered AI needs to be shaped with diverse communities, not imposed on them.:* **— James Landay, Director, Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI**

***Don't Confuse Confidence with Competence.*** AI models are the most sophisticated autocomplete engines ever built, and they have no real understanding of what they're telling you. Left to their own devices, they will confidently fabricate a statistic, a quote, or a heartbreaking anecdote, and they can be wrong in ways that sound completely convincing. The fix isn't to distrust the tool. It's about building verification into how your team works, just as you'd never let a new hire submit a report without a second set of eyes. Skip that discipline, and you don't just risk bad data; you risk your team's judgment slowly eroding from cognitive offloading.

***Small Tools Are the Great Equalizer.*** You don't need an enterprise-sized contract to do this responsibly. A new class of small, open-source language models can run securely on a standard office desktop, completely offline, for a fraction of the cost of a big consumer platform. For organizations that handle sensitive client information on a lean budget, which is most of us, this can be a game changer. It means you can process case notes and compliance records without ever putting a vulnerable person's data on someone else's server.

***Treat This as a Discipline, Not a Software Purchase.*** **T** he organizations getting this right aren't the ones with the biggest AI budgets. They're the ones who hold someone accountable for it, someone asking hard questions across programs, legal, and operations, rather than letting every department download whatever tool looks good in a webinar. Governance sounds like a boring word for a boardroom. Treat it instead as an ongoing habit: who's watching for bias creeping in, who's reviewing what staff produced with AI, and who has the authority to say, “We're pausing this until we understand it better.” Lean on your boards to support a robust governance approach.

### Your Five-Step Playbook

You're not going to get your AI strategy perfect on the first try, so set appropriate expectations up front. Start small, anticipate that some of it may not pan out, and build on what you learn. Here's where you can start.

- ***Map the repetition.*** At your next leadership meeting, split your workflows into two columns: “repetitive” and “relationship-driven.” Pilot AI only in the first column, for tasks like meeting notes and report formatting, and leave the second column alone for now.
- ***Think beyond your direct users.***Ask who's affected by your data and tools beyond the people on your team using them, including clients, families, and communities who never touch the software but live with what it decides.
- ***Set data ground rules.*** Put clear, written rules in place: no unredacted client files, donor lists, or proprietary program data may be entered into a public, consumer-grade AI tool. Make it as routine as locking a filing cabinet with client files.
- ***Spot-check for AI fabrications.*** Pull five AI-assisted documents your team recently produced and check them line by line against the original source material. You're looking for fabricated numbers and staff who've stopped double-checking AI output.
- **Run one small pilot.** Pick one team, run one small pilot, and use one open-source tool. Measure success by staff well-being and hours returned to direct service.

### The Close

Organizational resilience has never been about replacing the heartbeat of your mission with AI. It's about using technology to absorb the bureaucratic weight that wears your people down, so they can bring their full, undistracted selves back to the people they serve. I know this well, having lived on both sides of the technology-versus-people argument in two very different careers.

> The leaders who get this right are the ones who stay honest about what AI *can't do* and protective of what only *their people can do*.
