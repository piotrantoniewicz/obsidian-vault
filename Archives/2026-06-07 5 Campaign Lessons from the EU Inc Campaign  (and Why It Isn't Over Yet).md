Power

07.06.2026

On 18 March 2026, the European Commission tabled COM/2026/321, its proposal for the so-called 28th regime, branded EU Inc. Within hours, Scale Ireland and a swathe of European startup associations welcomed it as "a solid first step". Within the same hours, the original organisers of the EU Inc campaign, Andreas Klinger, Philipp Herkelmann, Simon Schaefer and Vojtech Horna, were calling it a major missed opportunity in private and a useful starting point in public.

Both readings are correct. Living in Brussels just got very interesting with a taste of that single market that is not yet fully cooked!

\-

For those who have not followed the file: EU Inc launched in October 2024 as a grassroots petition by founders, investors and operators arguing that Europe's startup ecosystem is fragmented across 27 national company law systems and needs a single, optional, pan-European legal entity. Fully digital. Standard articles of association. Stock options that work across borders. Inside fourteen months, the campaign gathered over 22,000 signatures, 600 venture capital firms, 9,000 startups and 20 industry associations. It produced a detailed legislative blueprint by December 2024. It became, in Klinger's own words, the defacto go-to expert voice on the 28th regime in Brussels.

Then the Commission proposed the law. And the campaigners moved into the awkward, less photogenic phase that decides whether all of that work actually changes anything: the trilogue between Parliament, Council and Commission, with national transposition discretion still latent inside the text.

This is the moment most campaigns lose. Below are five lessons I have taken from how EU Inc handled the first phase, and what political campaigners and policy advocates should be doing as the file moves into the second.

A note before the lessons: the EU Inc campaign is not over. The proposal is now being negotiated by the European Parliament and the Council. The legal basis chosen by the Commission, Art. 114 TFEU rather than the Art. 352 the campaign wanted, leaves room for national interpretation. The scope has crept from startups to all companies. Disputes still route through national courts. The tech ecosystem is actively lobbying to close those loopholes before the end-2026 deadline the Commission has set itself. So read these lessons as a halftime report, not a post-mortem.

#### Lesson 1: Make Your Ask Short Enough to Repeat in a Lift

The campaign behind: EU Inc was a single proposition. A pan-European, optional, digital-by-default legal entity for startups. Anyone could repeat it in eight seconds. That brevity was not an accident. It was a discipline.

The political lesson: campaigns with one ask travel further than campaigns with twelve. In Brussels, where any policy file collects amendments like barnacles, the campaigns that survive are the ones whose central demand cannot be diluted without breaking it. EU Inc made itself indivisible.

What this looks like in practice:

Write your demand on a Post-it. If it does not fit, you do not have a campaign, you have a manifesto. Manifestos are read by allies. Campaigns are repeated by strangers.

Use a brand, not a description. "EU Inc" beats "harmonised cross-border corporate legal framework" because it lives in the mouth, not the file note. The brand should be technical enough that policymakers cannot dismiss it as a slogan and vivid enough that journalists will repeat it for free.

Resist the temptation to add. Every coalition partner will want their priority bolted on. Most should be told no. The reason your campaign got attention was that it did one thing clearly. Adding a tax annex, a labour annex and a competition annex turns a campaign into a working group, and working groups do not move politicians.

#### Lesson 2: Coalition Is the Message

The campaign behind: EU Inc did not just collect 22,000 signatures. It collected them across every member state, every layer of the ecosystem (founders, VCs, employees, lawyers, accelerators), and 20 industry associations. The signatory map became the argument. Brussels could not credibly tell a coalition that broad that the demand was niche or geographically narrow.

The political lesson: in the Brussels institutional process, who signs matters more than how many sign. A petition with 22,000 names from one member state moves no one. A petition with 5,000 names from all 27 moves rapporteurs. The shape of the coalition is the political weight of the campaign.

What this looks like in practice:

Map your signatories by jurisdiction and sector before you launch. Empty cells are your call list for week two. The campaigns I have watched succeed in Brussels are the ones that treated coalition building as a procurement exercise: which countries do we need, which associations confer legitimacy, which signatories will quote us back in their own national press?

Get associations on board, not just individuals. An individual signs a petition. An association represents an organised constituency that can be invoked at the negotiating table. EU Inc got 20 of them. That is what made it impossible to dismiss as a Berlin and Stockholm hobby project.

Name your most credible signatories in every press hit. Anonymous totals do not move officials. Quoted names with a CV behind them do.

#### Lesson 3: Show Up With a Draft, Not a Demand

The campaign behind: by December 2024, two months after launch, EU Inc had published a detailed industry blueprint for the legal entity. Not a manifesto. A draftable text, structured the way Brussels structures regulations. The campaign then spent over a year meeting "almost every politician working on this," in Klinger's phrasing, from Directorates-General to Council working groups to thinktanks to taskforces. They turned themselves into the policy reference.

The political lesson: in Brussels, the people who write the text shape the law. Campaigners who arrive with grievances get sympathy. Campaigners who arrive with usable language get clauses. The Commission does not have the capacity to draft every file from scratch. When a coalition arrives with a workable draft, parts of it survive into the proposal almost by gravity.

What this looks like in practice:

Invest in legal and policy capacity early. Hire or partner with a law firm before you launch the petition, not after the Commission asks you for input. EU Inc had legal expertise behind its blueprint from very early on, and it showed.

Publish your proposal in the format the institution actually uses. Regulations have recitals, articles, scope clauses, definitions. Drafting in that format signals to officials that you understand the file and are not asking them to do unpaid translation work.

Walk into meetings with a one-page redline, not a list of complaints. Officials remember the campaigns that made their job easier. They forget the ones who made it harder.

#### Lesson 4: Borrow the Institution's Own Narrative

The campaign behind: EU Inc did not arrive in Brussels saying "Europe is broken." It arrived saying "the Draghi report and the Letta report identified fragmentation as the central competitiveness problem, von der Leyen agreed at Davos in January 2025, and here is the file that delivers what you already said you wanted." The campaign anchored itself to existing institutional commitments. By March 2026, four Commissioners stood at the press conference.

The political lesson: institutions defend their own commitments. They resist external lectures. Make your campaign the test of whether the institution means what it said, and you will find allies inside the building. Pick a fight with the institution and you will find none.

What this looks like in practice:

Read the speeches of the people you need to convince. Quote them back to them. The Draghi and Letta reports were not just reports. They were the political authorisation for everything EU Inc asked for. Campaigners who do not read the foundational documents of their target institution are campaigning blind.

Frame your ask as the natural conclusion of an existing political document, not as a new agenda. New agendas need political champions and election cycles. Existing agendas need delivery. Delivery is easier.

Avoid morally loaded framing where technocratic framing will do. "Single market completion" travels further in a Council working group than "Brussels has failed us." Save the rhetoric for the press release. Use the institution's own grammar in the institution's own rooms.

#### Lesson 5: A Commission Proposal Is Halftime. Behave Accordingly

The campaign behind: the 18 March proposal landed with serious gaps. The legal basis is Art. 114 TFEU, not Art. 352 the campaign wanted, which means qualified majority rather than unanimity but also more national interpretation. Scope expanded from startups to all companies, the lobbying preference of the established corporate sector. National business registers stay in the loop. National courts retain jurisdiction over disputes. Allied for Startups, the European Startup Network and the Dutch Startup Association all said publicly that the text is not yet a true 28th regime.

And yet, the public message from the broader ecosystem was "solid first step." That gap, between the public welcome and the private push, is not hypocrisy. It is craft.

The political lesson: managing the distance between public optimism and private negotiation pressure is one of the hardest skills in advocacy. Declare victory too loudly and your allies disengage, your funders move on, and Parliament rapporteurs assume the file is settled. Disparage the proposal and you lose the institutional access you spent eighteen months earning. The campaign that handles this phase well keeps both lanes open at once.

What this looks like in practice:

Pre-write your "solid first step" line before the Commission publishes. You will need it within the hour. Allies will tweet whatever you publish, so publish carefully.

Keep your toughest criticisms in bilaterals with the rapporteur and the Permanent Representations. The press gets the constructive tone. The negotiators get the specifics: the legal basis, the loophole in Art. X, the missing recital on Y.

Resource the second half. Most campaigns spend 90% of their budget pre-proposal and run dry in trilogue, which is the phase that decides whether the law actually works. Reverse the spend. The post-proposal phase needs lawyers, MEP-facing capacity, Council working group monitoring and a press function that can sustain attention for another twelve months.

#### So, what can Polical Campaigners like us learn from this?

If you take only five things from EU Inc into your own file, take these:

**Tip #1:** Audit your ask. Can a stranger repeat it after one reading? If not, cut until they can. Do this before you book the launch event.

**Tip #2:** Map your coalition by jurisdiction and sector. Empty cells are your call list. A 27-state map with one or two blanks looks credible. A petition with 100,000 signatures from three countries does not.

**Tip #3:** Hire legal and policy drafting capacity in month one, not month nine. The campaigns that influence text are the campaigns that arrive with text.

**Tip #4:** Read the foundational political documents of the institution you are lobbying. Quote them back. Make yourself the delivery vehicle for a promise the institution has already made.

**Tip #5:** Budget the trilogue. Build a second-phase team and a second-phase comms plan before the Commission publishes. The campaign that wins is the one still standing in November.

#### Time to Dig In

EU Inc is not yet a win or a loss. It is a stress test of whether Europe's tech ecosystem can do politics at the level the rest of Brussels operates at. So far, the answer has been: yes, more than people expected. The proposal exists. Four Commissioners showed up. The end-2026 deadline is on the table. Ireland holds the Council presidency in the second half of 2026 and is well placed to push.

What happens next will depend on whether the campaign keeps its discipline through Parliament's rapporteur process and Council's working groups, whether it can close the national-register and national-court loopholes that currently sit inside the text, and whether it can keep its coalition mobilised through a phase of negotiation that is, by design, less visible and less satisfying than the petition stage.

The campaigners I respect are treating the March 2026 proposal as the moment to dig in, not the moment to declare. That is the right instinct. The lessons above are about advocacy craft. The deeper question, whether Europe's institutions can deliver a usable single market for innovation in time to matter, will be answered by what happens in the next twelve months.

Until then, the campaign continues.
