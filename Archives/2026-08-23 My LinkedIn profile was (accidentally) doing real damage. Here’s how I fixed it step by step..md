**This week I found out my LinkedIn profile was a much bigger problem than I thought.**

After training hundreds of execs and professionals on their LinkedIn profile, I’ve realized most treat it as an afterthought.

Kinda like their garage. It's dusty. There's a bike with a flat tire in the corner and four boxes nobody's opened since the move. But the car fits, and there's room for the Christmas stuff, so… *good enough*.

Profiles get the same treatment. Profile photo is there, current role's listed, and maybe a couple skills are listed, so… *good enough*.

Then I opened my own garage.

Here's where the analogy breaks down. A messy garage costs you nothing.

**But your LinkedIn profile is** ***by far*** **your most valuable digital asset.**

One reason is pure volume of eyeballs.

More people will see your LinkedIn profile than shake your hand this year.

![Treść artykułu](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQE2CEYM27d5vg/article-inline_image-shrink_1500_2232/B56aAQYtgqIwAY-/0/1786981308726?e=1789603200&v=beta&t=VgmV0530TV4DgeS0aTvYf_FGIUc6rstZBMtMUfFA6kE)

More than 26,000 people saw my profile over the past 12 months. I travel for work and I get around, but I'm not shaking that many hands. You probably aren’t either.

Even though I knew better, I had all the usual excuses lined up to defend why I’d let my profile get dusty,

*Business is good.*

*It's not doing any harm.*

*Does rewriting my About section really sound better than golfing?*

But researching the [new rules of LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/new-rules-linkedin-here-now-im-tearing-down-my-100k-account-reed-lxdxc/?trackingId=7nWALmaHbzNQyaopGlciaA%3D%3D) and rebuilding my strategy from the ground up, I found that "not doing any harm" was dead wrong. **It was doing real damage, quietly, the whole time.**

So I audited and updated my entire profile to improve my visibility — to humans and LLMs — and to make sure LinkedIn’s new classification engine knows who I am and what I do.

Today I'm revealing every finding, every update I made, and why. Partly because this series has taken off more than anything I've written this year, and you crazy people clearly want the unedited version. Mostly because I want you to be able to do this too.

### The goal of my profile is simple

Attract and convert the people who can buy from me and people I can help.

Usually those are the same group. But not always. Some people can hire me to speak or bring me into a brand deal, and some are just interesting humans I want to trade ideas with. It's not always about the Benjamins.

Whatever your goals are, your profile has to support them. That part hasn't changed.

**What has changed is how people find you.**

LLMs entered the chat. As search shifted from Google to AI assistants, LinkedIn became the #2 most-cited source for LLMs ([Meltwater, 2026](https://www.meltwater.com/en/press-releases/how-ai-is-changing-the-way-information-is-found)). Which means people can now find you through your profile without ever seeing your content.

Then LinkedIn's interest graph joined and made it a group chat.

The machine now has a much bigger role in deciding who, if anyone, sees you. And it determines your audience and reach by reading your profile to figure out what you're about. ([*I broke that down in full here*](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/i-attracted-wrong-audience-heres-what-im-doing-devin-reed-owhzc/?trackingId=nxJrt3k2YmZW52ru0Kg98w%3D%3D).)

Anytime you're playing on someone else's territory, you're playing by their rules. LinkedIn changed the rules. But my profile was still playing the old ones.

So I audited my profile, and…

### I found more than I expected.

Before the “what,” here’s the “how.”

1. I took my original LinkedIn profile system — the one I've used to train hundreds of execs, leads, and corporate professionals — and rebuilt it based on everything I learned from researching the new rules. I turned that into a Claude skill so I can easily run it again and again.
2. Then I downloaded my profile PDF and my entire LinkedIn archive, which includes every post, comment, connection, skill, and every attribute LinkedIn stores about me. ([Here's how to pull yours](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339364/downloading-your-account-data). Takes 24 hours to process but 100% worth it.)
3. I ran my data through the skill to grade my profile, find the gaps, and tell me what to fix.

**The verdict: C-**

I was never a 4.0 GPA kinda guy, but a C-minus from your own system stings. There's nobody to blame but me.

My own audit flagged things I've never thought to look for. And others that had been wrong for years.

Here are the five that mattered most — and the last one is something I never expected.

## 1\. My banner was a three-year-old promo for a podcast I stopped making

The largest and most valuable real estate on my profile — full-width, top of the page, the first thing all 26,000+ of those people saw — was a promo for [*Reed Between The Lines*](https://www.youtube.com/@TheReederCo) with three platform buttons.

![Treść artykułu](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQFKwPn7KCLpKA/article-inline_image-shrink_1500_2232/B56aAQZA6cG0AU-/0/1786981388246?e=1789603200&v=beta&t=JuLsw-7dsEEkUtvdOffjekbmXrvUzWUDv2003Zl6igo)

I created it when the show launched three years ago because promoting my new project was the right move.

But I’ve since paused new episodes, and I’ve grown The Reeder into a full-time business. So every visitor learned I have a podcast, but not much about what I sell or what I believe. That’s a big gap on the conversion front.

### How I fixed it

Your banner is a billboard. It’s the largest image on your profile, so if you sell something, it should point to your core offer or newest launch (then back to core offer).

So I swapped the podcast for what I actually sell: B2B content strategy, and the promise behind it. Then I added a proof bar underneath, because a claim is more believable when it isn't standing there by its lonesome.

## 2\. My headline was missing the two words I've built my entire business and reputation on

Old headline: **I help B2B marketers and execs build durable content systems | Founder @ The Reeder | 12K+ newsletter readers**

I see two problems.

1. **It names two audiences, which means it names neither.** "I help" is the weakest opening in the game and I used it twice — headline and the first line of my About. And "durable content systems" is close to my category without being my category.
2. **The words I want to be found for — content strategy — are missing.** That's my positioning “one word” that I’ve been using as a north star for 5+ years, and it's the phrase I want the machine to latch on to.

### How I fixed it

First, I had to work out what the headline is even for now that my banner is doing real work.

But that banner is a JPEG. LinkedIn's classification engine only reads text — headline, About, work history, skills, etc. Every word Emily designed into it is invisible to the machine.

So the banner only helps once people make it to my profile.

My headline goes everywhere. Every comment I leave on someone else's post carries my name, my photo, and my headline. That's the entire introduction. No banner, no logo bar, no proof.

So the headline has two jobs the banner can't do. Give the machine my category. And give a cold stranger the whole “pitch” in one line.

New headline: **B2B Content Strategy | Every company creates content. Almost no one creates believers. | Founder, The Reeder**

Category term up front for the machine. My actual belief in the middle for the human. And it plays off the banner instead of repeating it — the banner says *builds trust before it's time to buy,* the headline says *creates believers*. Same idea, two angles.

## 3\. My About section had 6 CTAs

The first half of my About is the best writing on my profile. It opens on building trust before people need it, explains the 95-5 Content System, and gets to the point fast.

But then it falls apart and devolves into a résumé with big headers like “MY BACKGROUND” and “WHAT I DO NOW.” Then closes with a list of every channel I publish.

I counted the ways off my profile. *Six.*

Most LinkedIn profiles are dead ends, but mine was the opposite problem — which turns out to be the same problem. Six doors is a hallway, and a hallway isn’t great for conversions.

### How I fixed it

I cut the résumé headers and the channel list, and rewrote the opening around what I actually believe: most content teams are fighting over the 5% who are already looking, and losing the 95% who decide everything.

The proof is still in there. Gong, Clari, Ramp, Notion. It just comes after the belief now instead of standing in for it.

Then I went from six CTAs to two.

I went back and forth on this. The obvious move is "book a call," because that's where the money is.

**But my profile isn't built for the handful of people ready to buy today. It's built for the much larger group still deciding whether I'm worth listening to.**

Subscribe is the honest ask for that group. And the people ready to hire me will find the calendar link just fine.

## 4\. My Featured section was a highlight reel, not a next step

I had two video posts doing roughly the same job, and one lead magnet.

![Treść artykułu](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5612AQGdC8CkmFZisg/article-inline_image-shrink_1000_1488/B56aAQZdsOJMAI-/0/1786981506079?e=1789603200&v=beta&t=lBM5UI_KqPaBi5lNgvYvsWGNmacSNLjEnL347EZKcUw)

I'd picked those two videos because one was to announce a new YouTube series, and the other performed well. That was the whole thinking. Good or recent post, pin it.

The [*LinkedIn Growth Guide*](https://www.thereeder.co/free-guides/linkedin-growth-guide) is doing real work from a reputation and conversion standpoint. But the two videos weren't doing any. They were in there because they were good posts, not because they moved anyone anywhere.

And my newsletter wasn't in there at all. Which probably explains why I occasionally hear, "oh, I didn't know you had a newsletter."

That one stings every time.

### How I fixed it

I started with the math. 95% of the people who land on my profile aren't ready to buy from me. Not today, maybe not even this year.

Which is exactly why this section exists.

Featured has one job now: turn a visitor into a follower or a subscriber. Growing my audience across newsletter, YouTube, and LinkedIn is my second-biggest goal this year, and this is the highest-intent real estate I've got for it.

Three tiles, three ways in.

**Subscribe to The Reeder.** Direct link, no detour, no more "I didn't know you had a newsletter."

**The LinkedIn Growth Guide**. “LinkedIn for Executives” is a real pillar of this business and my #2 flagship offer, and that guide is genuinely good. It earns its slot.

**Watch YouTube videos.** YouTube is new, and it matters. It'd be silly not to move credibility and traffic from here to there — same audience, longer format, more room to actually teach.

## 5\. My #1 skill was Sales. Content Strategy was #17.

This one I did *not* see coming.

My profile shows a "Top Skills" card, and it reads Content Strategy, Content Marketing, Writing, Sales. Looks great. I'd glanced at it a hundred times and moved on.

But that card is a *display setting*. Underneath it is the actual Skills *list*. And that's the one LinkedIn reads to decide what I'm actually expert in. Because I spent the first half of my career in sales, mine was ordered Sales, Customer Service, SaaS, Account Management, Marketing, Social Media Marketing, [Salesforce.com](http://salesforce.com/), Tutoring.

**Content Strategy ranked 17th out of 20.**

Then I checked the endorsements. 150 for Sales. 114 for Customer Service. 110 for SaaS.

7 for Content Strategy.

I left Gong's sales floor in 2019. But my skills section never got the memo, and every one of those endorsements is still voting against me.

Then I went looking for how deep it went.

Buried in that same export is a file that shows exactly how LinkedIn labels you for advertisers. It's the closest thing you'll get to reading the machine's mind.

Mine listed 22 job titles. Nine were sales titles, five were strategy titles and four were content titles.

Not one of them contained the word "marketing." And "content strategist" wasn't in there at all because LinkedIn had split "Content Strategy Manager" into *Content Manager* and *Strategy Manager*, two separate entries.

My category got cut in half. Neither half is what I do.

Then I found the field feeding it. My job title said **Head of Marketing & Founder**. At a company I own with my wife. "Head of Marketing" implies I work for someone else, it buries Founder in second position, and it puts the word “marketing” in the one spot where content strategy should have been.

### How I fixed it

Reordered the list so Content Strategy is first, deleted the ones that stopped being true a decade ago. Simple.

Then changed my title to **Founder & Content Strategist**, so my category finally lives in a field the machine reads.

5 minutes of work, and it's the fastest classification change available to anyone reading this.

Go look at yours right now. You might be surprised what you find.

No grand strategy here. I just wanted a new one. Figured if I only get one first impression, it should count.

## How I'll know if it worked

It’s not profile views. Volume is tempting, but it's the wrong scoreboard for me at this stage.

For the next 90 days I'm watching **who** lands on my profile, and what they do next. Newsletter subscribers. Lead magnet downloads. DMs from people who want to hire and partner with me.

Because if profile views get cut in half, but I’m attracting my ideal audience, that's a win. Publish what your target audience will love, not what everyone will tolerate — and the same rule applies to who your profile is built for.
