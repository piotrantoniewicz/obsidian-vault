Last week I wrote about [why all the fundraising spam is hurting Democrats](https://theshortansweris.substack.com/p/how-do-we-stop-the-fundraising-spam) and the left — not just by siphoning off crucial grassroots dollars, but by eroding the trust we so desperately need. In response, I got several versions of the same (correct) next question: *“OK, but is there anything* I c *an do about it?”*

The short answer is yes, even if you’re nowhere near a digital fundraising program yourself, you can help push back against the never ending spam. Let’s look at a few different ways:

#### Increase your digital fundraising literacy (and help others do the same)

I hate to assign you homework out the gate, but the first step to fighting spam is to figure out who is actually sending that spam. Just because a text says “WE MUST ELECT JON OSSOFF” doesn’t mean a donation to it will go to… Jon Ossoff. It might be going to the candidate, or a legitimate PAC spending dollars in Georgia, but it just as easily could be going to a scam PAC that’s spending more on fundraising consultants than they are voter contact.

There are telltale signs to watch out for. Texts and emails are required to say who they’re sent from *in each message*, and most legitimate operations do. If it’s still not clear, check the ActBlue page before donating. They always have a “Contributions will go to \_\_\_\_\_” line just above the donate box, so you can see if you’re funding a candidate directly, giving to an outside group, or splitting between the two.

Most importantly, if you see any of the organizations below, they’re red flags that you should probably avoid giving directly to. These are organizations that researcher Adam Bonica [identified](https://data4democracy.substack.com/p/hook-and-squeeze) as particularly bad actors using high-volume spam tactics:

#### Report bad actors to ActBlue and push other vendors

After years of requests, ActBlue recently updated its terms of service to better hold people accountable if they’re sending spam or deceptive messages and still trying to use ActBlue as their donation platform. The policy updates are only as effective if they’re actually enforced, though, and that means reporting spam to ActBlue to ensure they actually see it. (In an [update](https://www.actblue.com/posts/reinforcing-trust-our-q2-account-use-policy-update/) last week, ActBlue said that last quarter they removed 7 entities from the platform, and brought 18 more “into compliance,” which is encouraging at least.) You can see the [full details here](https://help.actblue.com/hc/en-us/articles/33083970701847-how-can-i-report-a-potential-violation-of-actblue-s-terms-and-policies) about how to report spammers to ActBlue, but generally it requires sending an email with details, or clicking the ‘Report this form’ button on any donation form. And then beyond ActBlue, if you’re ever talking to anyone whose tools are part of the fundraising ecosystem (from email and texting platforms to donation processors to data vendors), ask them to crack down harder on bad actors too.

#### Mark senders as spam

This sounds obvious but it really can have an impact, especially for email! Any email address someone is sending from (like donate@scampac.org) has to convince each inbox provider (Gmail, Outlook, etc.) that they’re trustworthy enough to make it to your inbox. For genuine spam, you should hit the “Mark as Spam” button to drive down a bad sender’s reputation in addition to unsubscribing. Enough of those complaints will keep future emails out of not just your inbox, but other people’s, too.

You can do this on text as well, and technically the providers should shut down numbers if there are enough complaints. In reality, the spammers will likely just jump to a different number, unfortunately.

#### Vote with your dollars

Moralizing isn’t going to save us here. The only way that spam fundraising will actually stop is if it stops raising money. The good news is we’ve each got the power to show fundraisers that we’ll give to good, compelling, thoughtful messages instead of rushed, anonymous spam — by actually donating when we see a quality ask we opted in to receive. Dollars raised is the only metric that these fundraisers actually care about, so if enough of us get our networks to stop giving to bad sends, we can help tip the scales.

#### We’ve all got a role to play

Of course, this isn’t to let campaigns, candidates, PACs, or consultants off the hook. If you’re running a fundraising program yourself, I promise you, you can raise all the money you need to win without burning everything down around you. I’ve done it myself. And if you’re considering a firm to do your fundraising for you, *please* do your due diligence to see the kinds of tactics they use, and whether they’re helping prop up any spam PACs themselves.

This is an issue that has felt like a bubble waiting to pop for too long now. Eventually, we *have* to figure out how to raise grassroots money in sustainable, ethical ways — or we’ll be left with audiences not just tuning out our fundraising pleas, but ignoring the Democratic message entirely.
