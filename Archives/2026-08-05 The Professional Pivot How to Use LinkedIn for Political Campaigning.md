While Facebook and X have long been the primary battlegrounds for political messaging, they are becoming increasingly cluttered, restricted, and hostile, making genuine connections with voters feel forced and inauthentic.

This creates a challenge for campaign professionals: finding a space where the audience is more focused, the discourse remains (at least theoretically) professional, and the information people provide about their professional interests and networks is unusually detailed.

So let’s take a look at LinkedIn.

\-

By Attila Kerekes

By now, LinkedIn is far removed from its original purpose of simply matching employers and job seekers. You can use it to build connections with and influence decision-makers, recruit highly engaged followers, and engage with specific professional communities.

For the right campaign, it can become a very powerful tool in your arsenal, particularly because it remains greatly underused for political campaigning.

### Creating a strategic advantage via LinkedIn?

In a series of my previous articles, I highlighted the importance of high-quality, first-party data and the need for ethical engagement. To that end, LinkedIn is actually the ultimate "first-party data" environment because users provide their own professional history, industry, and interests.

#### Audience quality

LinkedIn’s user base skews toward higher income and education levels. These are your "opinion leaders": even if not strictly speaking ‘influencers’, but people who influence their communities and workplaces.

#### Targeting by profession

Unlike Facebook, where you target by "interests" (which can be tricky to fine-tune, as they are many times purposefully broad and vague), LinkedIn allows you to target by job title, company, and industry. Want to reach every healthcare worker in a specific region to discuss policy? LinkedIn makes that precise targeting possible.

#### One of the last organic “paradises”

Contrary a bit to the previous point, true strength on LinkedIn lies in organic outreaches, and network building. Although engagement has somewhat dropped compared to the pre-COVID and COVID golden age of social networking, LinkedIn is probably one of the last networks where you actually see posts by people you follow, or ones that could be interesting based on your activity records, rather than only paid advertisements on top of each other

### The "professional context" buffer

Users on LinkedIn are in a "work mindset." They are more receptive to policy papers, white papers, and long-form thought leadership than they are to the sensationalist, short-form content that dominates other social networks.

There is a heavy push still, though, on LinkedIn too towards video content - nonetheless, the content of the video as well focuses more on professional, detailed and well-thought-out exchanges and proposals, rather than bombastic news and paid ads

### LinkedIn Campaigning 101: How to Start Tomorrow

If you are a campaign manager, or maybe a candidate even yourself, and looking to integrate LinkedIn into your strategy, your most advantageous and long term strategy should be to stop treating it like a "repost" site for your Meta content. Here is your operational checklist for an effective LinkedIn campaign:

#### Audit your profile

Ensure your candidate’s profile functions as a professional political asset, not simply as a résumé.

It should feature a professional headshot, a clear headline that explains the candidate’s areas of expertise or political focus, and a “Featured” section highlighting the strongest policy proposals, interviews, articles, or campaign material.

Make sure the profile is verified where LinkedIn verification is available. Unlike paid verification systems on some other platforms, LinkedIn’s verification system is intended to confirm aspects of a user’s identity or professional affiliation rather than simply provide a badge in exchange for a subscription.

Most importantly, treat the candidate’s profile as a long-term asset. A network built consistently over several years is far more valuable than one activated three months before election day.

#### Create content for LinkedIn

Move away from simply reposting quick, flashy content and towards more substantive material.

Post about specific policy challenges and your proposed solutions. Use document posts and PDF carousels to explain complex proposals clearly. Experiment with video as well, but do not assume that every TikTok-style reel belongs on LinkedIn simply because it performed well somewhere else.

And do not create Al-generated long-format posts for the candidate's feed.

LinkedIn is already drowning in them: predictable hooks, long bullet-point lists, identical sentence structures, and an emoji introducing every supposed insight. You have probably seen them too.

They can kill authenticity in seconds.

The same principle applies to links. LinkedIn generally wants users to remain on LinkedIn, so do not make every post a signpost directing people immediately to YouTube, your campaign website, or another platform. Give people something worth reading or watching on LinkedIn itself, and use external links when there is a genuine reason for the audience to continue elsewhere.

#### Leverage your team’s voice as well

Your staff, key volunteers, local supporters, and policy experts can all be valuable assets.

If your campaign manager or a prominent local supporter shares an argument from their own professional perspective, their network may be much more likely to engage with it than with the same message posted by a faceless party page.

This does not mean giving everyone the same copy and asking them to post it simultaneously. That looks coordinated because it is coordinated.

Instead, allow different people to speak from their own position. The candidate can explain the political decision. A policy adviser can explain the substance. A local supporter can explain why the issue matters in their profession or community.

Comment on, repost, and engage with your own team’s content as well. A campaign is already a network. LinkedIn gives you an opportunity to use that network more effectively.

#### Target your outreach

Treat key local influencers or business leaders like "accounts", such as you would in a B2B sales strategy. Connect with them, engage with their content first, and build a relationship before asking for engagement from their end.

### Respect the restrictions

It is important to note that LinkedIn has strict policies against "political advertising" in many regions. However, unlike Meta’s broad ban, LinkedIn’s restrictions often allow for "social issue" or "public awareness" content if it does NOT explicitly call for a vote.

**Therefore, your golden rule should be:** Do not try to run a "Vote for XYZ" ad. Instead, run an "informational" ad about a policy issue you champion, like "The Future of Local Infrastructure" or "How to Fix Healthcare in Our District." This builds your brand and gathers data on who is interested in your platform without triggering ad bans. BUT, **paid ads should always be a secondary layer** to your campaign efforts on LinkedIn. Remember: organic reach is key, and in fact, still very possible on the platform. You just have to stay consistent.

### Stand out by bringing depth to your socials

The shift toward LinkedIn is a move from "shouting" to "communicating." While Facebook and Instagram are a megaphone for mass reach, LinkedIn is a surgical tool for building credibility with the people who could prove to be incredibly valuable to you down the line.

By treating your campaign like a professional organization and your voters like an educated audience, you can bypass the "shadow bans" and the noise of the mainstream social media wars by building consistent and reliable organic reach. Authenticity on LinkedIn doesn't mean being aggressive; it means being and staying an expert. And in a political climate where voters are exhausted by polarization, the candidate who speaks to them like a professional, rather than another politician hooked on the “attention economy”, will stand out from the crowd much more.

Politics is always about finding the right balance between persistence and grabbing on the momentum. And LinkedIn can be the perfect tool for building the foundation with the first part. Start building your organic professional network today and who knows? Your next major donor or key coalition partner might show up tomorrow in your "Suggested Connections."
