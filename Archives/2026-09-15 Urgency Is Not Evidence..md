When the companies building AI tell us they are worried about moving too fast while continuing to build at extraordinary scale, nonprofits and philanthropy should pay attention — but perhaps not in the way the AI industry wants us to.

I have been sitting with that contradiction this week.

Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has been making an increasingly urgent argument that frontier AI development needs to slow down, that capabilities are beginning to move faster than our ability to understand and govern them, and that simply continuing to accelerate because everyone else is accelerating is reckless. In his own words, there is a point at which “building it too fast is reckless.”

I think you and I should listen to that.

I also think we should ask much harder questions about who is saying it – not to dismiss the warning, but to make sure he isn’t the only voice we are weighing it against.

Because Anthropic is not standing outside the AI race warning everyone else about the danger. It is deeply inside it. It has reportedly signed compute agreements worth up to $517 billion, secured enormous amounts of computing capacity, continued expanding its products and customer base, and positioned itself as one of the major players in what may be the most expensive technology race we have ever witnessed.

So, I find myself holding two thoughts at once: I believe Dario Amodei may be genuinely concerned about where this technology is going, and I do not believe genuine concern makes Anthropic — or any AI company — a neutral narrator of what should happen next.

Those ideas can coexist.

In fact, I think understanding that they can coexist is one of the most useful things nonprofits and philanthropy can take from this moment, because for several years we have been told, implicitly and explicitly, that the people building AI know where all of this is going and that the primary danger for the rest of us is failing to keep up.

There is always another capability coming, another model being released, another workflow we apparently should have automated yesterday, another board asking whether the organization has an AI strategy, another voice explaining that organizations that do not move now will be left behind, another funder not investing truly in “AI readiness” while evaluating grantees on AI capabilities.

Underneath all of that are some assumptions we rarely stop to examine: that speed itself tells us something, that because billions of dollars are moving toward this technology we should move toward it too, that because technology companies are racing the race must be meaningful, and that because everybody else appears to be moving, hesitation is evidence that we do not understand enough.

But urgency is not evidence.

And perhaps that is the difference our sector needs more than another list of AI tools.

*I am interested in the warning. I am also interested in the incentive.*

One of the things I find frustrating about conversations around AI companies is how quickly we are pushed toward trusting these voices.

We are told – by these companies – that they are building products that will make our work more efficient, democratize access to knowledge, help small organizations compete with large ones and free humans from tedious tasks, and then, when those same companies begin publicly talking about safety, existential risk, loss of human control or the danger of moving too quickly, we are expected to treat the warning itself as proof that responsible people are in charge.

I don't think that asks enough of us.

I can appreciate that Anthropic has invested substantially in safety research and that its leadership has publicly advocated for stronger governance. I can appreciate Amodei saying that commercial competition can create a race to the bottom.

But I can also notice that Anthropic is participating in that very commercial competition, making enormous amounts of money, attracting extraordinary investment and building enormous amounts of infrastructure because its future value depends, in no small part, on AI becoming more capable, more useful and more deeply embedded in how we work and live.

And that matters for context too.

When the people who stand to benefit enormously from continued AI adoption tell us they are concerned about how quickly AI is advancing, their concerns may be completely sincere — but sincerity does not erase incentives.

I increasingly think our sector needs AI literacy that extends much further than understanding prompts, hallucinations, or which large language models exist. We should understand the system surrounding the technology too: how to design sustainable budgets for this technology, how to safely experiment for it, what infrastructure commitments we need to responsibly make, who benefits when adoption accelerates, who absorbs the consequences when experiments fail, and most importantly, how do continually define what “responsible” AI means along the way.

Because when a company commits hundreds of billions of dollars to future computing capacity, I do not automatically read that as evidence that AI will transform every organization in exactly the ways being promised.

I read it first as evidence that the company has made a very large bet.

If a nonprofit committed many times its present revenue to a future program because leadership was confident that demand, funding and outcomes would eventually justify the investment, I suspect most boards would have a few questions about risk, assumptions and evidence.

Why is it that when technology companies do it, somehow, we call it the future?

Is it because a large capital has a remarkable ability to make uncertainty look like inevitability? Once enough money enters a space, infrastructure gets built, vendors emerge, consultants specialize, conferences multiply, professional roles change, investors expect returns and companies need customers, until eventually one very large collective bet begins to look like a destination everyone independently decided was inevitable.

Maybe part of that bet will prove right. AI is already useful in ways that would have sounded extraordinary only a few years ago, and I am not interested in pretending otherwise.

But usefulness is not inevitability.

Investment is not evidence.

Scale is not evidence.

And urgency is certainly not evidence.

I refuse to accept Silicon Valley’s race as ours.

AI companies have their reasons to move quickly. They are competing with one another, competing internationally, attracting capital and talent, securing compute, racing for IPOs, and making bets today based on what they believe AI will be capable of years from now.

Those are their incentives.

Our incentives are different. *It must be*.

Maybe you are a domestic violence organization holding information that could endanger someone if it’s mishandled. Maybe you are a community health organization stewarding relationships built over decades. Maybe you are a fundraiser holding sensitive stories that donors and community members never expected to become prompt context. Maybe you are an advocacy organization working with people who already experience surveillance and exclusion. Maybe you are a foundation using technology inside decisions about which organizations and communities receive resources – and which do not.

So don’t we need a pace defined by us, for us?

And perhaps more importantly: why must we compete on urgency and call it our insecurity?

I hear some version of “we don't want to fall behind” constantly.

Behind whom? And toward what?

There is a strange assumption embedded in the phrase *falling behind*: that there is one road, one direction and one finish line, and that somebody further ahead has already established that the destination is worth reaching.

But that is not where we are with AI.

We are collectively discovering where it is genuinely useful, where it fails, where it produces different kinds of harm, where efficiency becomes meaningful mission value and where efficiency simply allows us to create more things faster.

The people building technology are discovering those things too. They simply have significantly more money riding on the answer. Perhaps that should give nonprofits permission to establish our own conditions instead of inheriting theirs.

And this is where I want to be very clear: I am not arguing that we should avoid AI until somebody else figures everything out. I want you and I to know much more about it, to experiment thoughtfully, to understand these technologies deeply and become a more confident user of them – because knowledge should expand your choices, not narrow them.

I want you and I to be able to look at one use case and say, “Yes, this is low risk, we understand what happens to the data, it solves an actual problem, and it gives valuable time back to our team.”

And I want us to be able to look at another use case and say, “No – this involves information we shouldn’t hand over, we don’t understand the data flow, the community affected hasn’t been part of the decision, and the benefit being promised doesn’t justify the uncertainty.”

I especially want our organizations to feel comfortable saying, “Not yet.”

Not from a place of lack of knowledge. But because they know enough to establish conditions.

That, to me, is what AI literacy should produce: not universal enthusiasm, but better judgment.

> There is an enormous difference between saying no because you do not understand something and saying no because you understand enough to know your boundaries.

The latter is informed institutional maturity.

So, naturally, I become uncomfortable when the Silicon Valley companies selling AI become our primary teachers about AI. We absolutely should learn from them — they understand the technologies they are building in ways most of us never will — but if the organization teaching you what is possible also benefits financially when you conclude that you need what it sells, perhaps we should learn from somewhere else too — from researchers, technologists, and community voices who have nothing to sell us.

And this is where I want philanthropy to step up and fund agency (not simply AI adoption).

I believe philanthropy can do something meaningful and interesting in response to this moment.

It can help nonprofits encounter AI from a position of knowledge rather than scarcity.

In ways that means

- recognizing that access is not the same thing as agency.
- giving an organization an AI subscription is different from funding the staff time required to determine whether that tool should be used.
- funding an AI pilot is different from funding the evaluation that determines whether the pilot should continue.
- providing prompting training is different from building governance capacity.
- paying for AI implementation is different from paying community members to participate in decisions about technologies that may affect them.
- (and most importantly) sending out the message that offering discounted technology is different from giving organizations access to independent technical advice from people who have nothing to sell them.

I am increasingly convinced that the ability to refuse technology should be considered part of equitable access to technology.

Otherwise, what we are funding is not agency. It is consumption.

And maybe that is why we – you, I, our sector - needs to carefully observe and understand this concerned narrative version from AI companies.

We don't actually need to determine whether Dario Amodei is sincere. He may sincerely believe that AI can profoundly benefit humanity, sincerely be alarmed by what increasingly capable systems can do and sincerely want parts of the industry to slow down, while Anthropic simultaneously continues making enormous commercial bets on a future in which AI becomes much more capable and much more widely used.

It tells us that you and I should continue to be cautious about outsourcing our judgment to them. It means making sure the loudest voice in the room isn’t also the one with the most money riding on the answer. And they are not the ones dictating the terms of safety.

It tells us that their risk tolerance does not have to become ours.

Their definition of useful does not have to become ours.

Their evidence threshold does not have to become ours.

And their urgency does not have to become ours.

We can listen carefully to what AI companies tell us about the technology — including when they warn us about it — without handing them the authority to decide what those warnings should mean for our organizations and communities.

*That is agency.*

Our collective goal cannot be in keeping up with AI companies.

```
Our collective priority at the moment is building every organization in this sector knowledgeable enough, resourced enough, and powerful enough to decide when keeping up is actually worth doing.
```

The people building AI are allowed to have their urgency. But that urgency is not our evidence to pursue the same path.
