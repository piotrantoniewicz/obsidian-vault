Power

29.05.2026

Spain’s recent regional elections in Extremadura, Aragón and Andalusia offer a reality check on the hype around artificial intelligence in politics.

\-

By Sebastian Rodriguez

True, AI is now part of Spanish campaigning: synthetic videos, fake personas, satire accounts and low-cost meme factories are all becoming more visible. The far right and grassroots digital activists appear especially comfortable using these tools to provoke, ridicule and mobilise.

But it has not changed the fundamentals. The strategic narrative remains **change versus more of the same.** It’sthat simple.

Firstly, this is why these three regions matter.

**Extremadura** tested anti-incumbency in low-density rural conditions.

**Aragón deserves special attention.** Spanish analysts often call it the country’s “Ohio” because it combines urban and rural Spain, depopulated interior provinces, a large metropolitan pole in Zaragoza, traditional PP-PSOE competition, regionalist politics and an ideological distribution close to the national average. As sociologist Ignacio Urquizu put it, Aragón is treated as a “mini-Spain” where “what happens in Aragón does not stay in Aragón”.

**Andalusia matters because of scale and symbolism.** As Spain’s most populous region and a former PSOE stronghold, the PP has governed for seven years but is now dependent on Vox after losing its absolute majority.

Taken together, these elections show something campaign professionals should pay attention to: **how digital campaigning, AI-generated content and disinformation now interact with a much older political force: anti-incumbency.**

Pedro Sánchez, Spain’s sitting PM, remains the central dividing figure in Spanish politics. The right is positioned as the vehicle of change. The unresolved question is whether that change comes through an EPP-affiliated PP majority with Vox outside government, or through a harder right-wing bloc in which Vox directly shapes the next political cycle.

For campaign professionals, Spain’s lesson is that technology changes the tempo of the campaign, but **strategy still decides the campaign.**

Here are the five lessons.

#### 1\. AI Is Still a Tactical Tool, Not the Strategic Engine

Spain has not yet entered a fully AI-driven campaign environment. The technology is being used, but mostly at the margins: videos, memes, fake images, comic attacks, synthetic satire and low-cost disinformation.

The 2026 evidence does not show AI deciding Spanish regional elections. It shows that AI is being used as an amplifier. It makes content cheaper, faster and more emotional. It gives fringe creators the production capacity of a small digital agency. But it does not replace message discipline, candidate credibility, field operations or the master narrative.

The clearest 2026 examples are not complex persuasion operations. They are simple synthetic clips, often tied to migration and Vox. [Newtral’s Lara Montoto reported](https://www.newtral.es/video-mujer-vuelve-pais-ayudas-vox-inteligencia-artificial/20260131/) in January 2026 on an AI-generated video of a veiled woman supposedly saying that if Vox won, she would return to her country because she did not want to work and would lose access to benefits. [RTVE Verifica also analysed](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20260204/ia-migrante-no-dice-se-ira-si-gana-vox/16923101.shtml) a similar clip and said its detection tools gave a 99% probability that the video had been generated with AI

Activists and partisan creators are using AI to reduce production costs and increase content velocity. A video that once required a small production team can now be produced by one motivated operator with a laptop, a few prompts and a strong political instinct.

What is being overstatedisthe idea that AI is already deciding elections. In Spain, AI is not yet replacing the strategic question. It is helping campaigns express that question faster, cheaper and more aggressively.

**Key takeaway:** Treat AI as an acceleration layer. Use it to test formats, translate content, visualise attacks, produce rapid-response assets and increase creative speed. But do not confuse content volume with strategic control.

#### 2\. The Far Right Understands the Meme Layer Better Than the Mainstream

AI-generated political content fits naturally with the far-right digital style because it is emotional, provocative, visual, anti-elite and designed to trigger outrage.

The migration examples are not accidental. They work because they compress a whole worldview into a few seconds: migrants receive benefits, Vox will stop it, and the system is unfair to “ordinary Spaniards”. Whether the person in the video actually exists is almost secondary to the emotional payload.

[Maldita reported](https://maldita.es/desinfo/20260513/video-de-una-supuesta-joven-musulmana-en-el-que-afirma-que-si-gana-vox-regresaria-a-su-pais-porque-considera-que-ya-le-tocaria-trabajar/) in May 2026 on another AI-generated video of a supposed Muslim woman saying that if Vox won, she would return to her country because she did not like working. Maldita identified the Sora watermark, the “AI-generated” label and the TikTok account behind the original publication. [Cadena SER’s Carolina Cuesta Vergara,](https://cadenaser.com/nacional/2026/05/19/de-los-ingenieros-marroquies-de-sanchez-a-la-paguita-inventada-los-bulos-sobre-migracion-que-arrasan-en-redes-cadena-ser/) with disinformation expert Marc Amorós, placed this type of content within a broader wave of migration hoaxes designed to criminalise migrants and make resentment travel quickly through social networks.

My reading is that this is not always a formal party strategy. That is precisely why it matters. Much of it looks like decentralised activism: anonymous accounts, ideological creators, content farms and meme operators producing material aligned with Vox’s narrative without necessarily being directed by Vox.

Vox-adjacent ecosystems understand that digital politics is not always about persuasion in the traditional sense. It is about identity formation, ridicule, emotional activation and narrative saturation.

What is being misunderstood is that not every synthetic provocation deserves a response. The far right often wins when mainstream actors turn fringe content into national controversy.

**Key takeaway:** Build a triage system. Classify content into three buckets: ignore, debunk or counter-frame. The worst mistake is to treat every AI meme as a five-alarm crisis. The second-worst mistake is to ignore the one that is actually breaking through.

#### 3\. Disinformation Works Best When It Hooks Onto a Real Political Fight

The most effective disinformation is not invented from nowhere. It attaches itself to an existing political conflict.

That is what makes the Spanish case important. The AI-generated migration videos did not appear in a vacuum. They circulated in a political environment where migration, social benefits and “priority for nationals” had become part of the PP–Vox negotiation space.

[Maldita’s April 2026 explainer](https://maldita.es/prebunking/20260422/interpretaciones-prioridad-nacional-pp-vox/) on VOX’s campaign “Spanish first” or “ *prioridad nacional* ” is useful here. It reported that PP and Vox agreements in Extremadura and Aragón included the concept of giving priority in public resources to people with “real, lasting and verifiable roots” in the territory. Maldita also noted that PP and Vox were not interpreting the idea in the same way: the PP linked it to rootedness and territorial ties, while Vox linked it more directly to Spanish nationality.

That is the main lesson. Disinformation does not need to create the issue. It only needs to simplify it, emotionalise it and make one side’s interpretation feel like common sense.

**Key takeaway**: Do not only debunk the asset. Debunk the frame. A strong response should say: the video is fake, here is who benefits from it, and here is the real policy question being distorted.

#### 4\. Digital Momentum Matters, But It Must Serve the Master Narrative

Digital performance is useful only when it reinforces the strategic frame.

Aragón is a good example. [Demócrata’s 2026 analysis](https://www.democrata.es/elecciones/aragon-2026/azcon-gana-a-alegria-vox-disparado-redes-sociales-ultima-semana/) of the Aragonese digital campaign, using Poder Digital data, found that Jorge Azcón, the PP candidate, overtook Pilar Alegría, PSOE’s candidate, in digital influence in the final stretch of the campaign, while Vox showed sharp acceleration in the online conversation. In the end, the PP won in Aragón but will depend on a strengthened Vox, while the PSOE fell to its historic low and Vox doubled its representation.

But the lesson is not “the candidate with the best digital operation wins”. That would be too simplistic.

The lesson is that digital momentum works when it attaches itself to the right strategic terrain. In Aragón, the terrain was not AI. It was change, anti-Sánchez sentiment, right-wing consolidation and the question of whether Vox would become indispensable.

Digital campaigning helped accelerate that environment. It did not create it.

**Key takeaway**: Build every digital asset around one strategic test: does this reinforce the central frame? If a piece of content does not help define change, continuity, risk or credibility, it is probably just noise.

#### 5\. Strategy Still Beats Technology

Even when AI can accelerate a campaign, it cannot decide what the campaign is about.

This is the central lesson from Spain. AI can make the attack funnier, faster and cheaper. It can produce a fake image, a synthetic voice, a parody video or a micro-targeted meme. But it cannot replace the master narrative.

And in Spain, the master narrative is clear: **change versus more of the same.**

The PP wants change to mean moderation, management and alternation. Vox wants change to mean rupture and cultural confrontation. The PSOE wants the next national contest to be a warning against the far right rather than a referendum on Sánchez.

That is the real campaign.

Andalusia illustrates the point, and not because it produced the most interesting AI example, but because it shows the strategic stakes at scale. [El País reported that the PP](https://elpais.com/espana/elecciones-andalucia/2026-05-18/que-ha-pasado-en-andalucia-las-elecciones-en-siete-graficos.html) won with 41.6% of the vote but lost its majority, while the PSOE fell to its worst result in the region and Andalusia continued its shift to the right of Spain as a whole.

The right is using digital energy to reinforce a broader demand for change. Vox is using cultural conflict and online provocation to make itself central to the debate, while the PP is trying to convert anti-incumbency into a more controlled and governable alternative.

**Key takeaway**: Campaigns should separate tools from strategy. AI is a tool. Disinformation is a threat. Digital campaigning is a delivery system. None of them answers the core question: what is this election about?

#### The Real Lesson from Spain

AI is changing the tempo of Spanish politics.

Campaigns are faster, cheaper, more visual and more chaotic. Smaller actors increasingly have access to production capabilities that previously belonged to professional teams.

The next Spanish campaign will not be decided by who makes the best AI meme. It will be decided by who owns the strongest interpretation of the national mood. Campaigns still win when they define what the election is about and build an operation capable of reinforcing that interpretation across every channel.

Right now, that mood is moving towards change. The unresolved question is what kind of change Spain is willing to accept: a PP-led alternation framed as moderation and competence, or a harder right-wing cycle in which Vox becomes a condition of power.

The winning campaign will be the one that turns digital energy into a credible answer to the oldest question in politics: **is it time for change?**
