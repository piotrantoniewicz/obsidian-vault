Being boring is the riskiest play in modern politics. A note for readers outside Australia: this one is local, but the lesson is universal.

Pauline Hanson is a populist right-wing senator who has been a fixture of Australian politics since the late 1990s. Her party, One Nation, trades in anti-immigration, anti-establishment grievance. Long underestimated, Pauline (increasingly called by only her first name, which should set off alarm bells…) has proven that being wrong on the facts doesn’t count against you if you are consistent, and people are angry enough.

Because right now Hanson is dominating Australian politics again. Her party currently leads in the polls, despite having only one lower house MP. It feels like her every utterance, no matter how incoherent, is treated to a breaking news alert. The words are almost beside the point. What gets transmitted is the fact of her having said *something* yet again.

If this is ringing a bell, it’s because we have seen this movie before. Trump, in 2016. The thing people (myself included) missed at the time was that the novelty of Trump was never the policy. It was the capacity to make himself impossible to ignore, day after day, for free. The substance such as it was, was a bug, not a feature.

**In a brilliant book last year called The Sirens’ Call, Chris Hayes delved into the core currency of modern life: attention.** His core claim borrows from the economist Herbert Simon, who saw it coming back in the 1970s: in a world drowning in information, the scarce resource is not information.

> Information is oversupplied, infinite in nature, and therefore not valuable. Attention, however, is finite. Whoever controls it wins.

What should be keeping Labor strategists up at night is Hayes’s insight that negative attention beats no attention. The outrage that is pulsing through the Australian community right now is not a side effect of Hanson’s dominance of the media. The outrage is the product. Every time we explain why she’s wrong, every vague condemnation just delivers her outrage deeper into the public consciousness.

Have you noticed that Hanson jumps wildly from one issue to the next? Of course you have. She understands that, as Hayes points out, it is far easier to grab attention than to hold it. So Hanson doesn’t even try. She pops up grabs everyones attention, lets go, and then repeats it again next week with something new, with very little reference to what came before. The churn is the point. There is nothing to argue or push back against because there was never really a coherent argument or proposal, just attentional flares.

So, now for the uncomfortable part: us. I’d characterise it as a stunned silence. The Prime Minister appears to have the bearing of a man who has just been handed a spider. He delivers buzz-phrases like “serious reform" with a learned earnestness to an electorate so contemptuous of the political establishment that every word feels like a lecture, even when it isn’t.

So on the rare occasion when Labor is able to command attention, it just results in people becoming even angrier, because it reminds them that the messenger represents the political establishment, and this is the thing they are most angry at.

> I’m reminded of Steve Bannon's doctrine, to flood the zone with sh\*t. Labor floods nothing. Instead, they announce, flinch, and change tack, without really acknowledging the climb down (but that’s an issue for another day...).

So here we are: the progressive side of politics does not have a theory of attention. It has a theory of correctness, along with an unshakeable faith that correctness will eventually be noticed on its own. I am skeptical. Conflict and emotion are the price of entry to modern politics. A story, a villain, stakes, and a face, that’s what moves the electorate.

Being interesting and wrong is more important than being boring and right. Ideally, you’d be interesting and right but in 2026 I don’t know if we are allowed to have nice things anymore.

It’s darkly amusing that a politician, or NGO leader, sounding like an actual person rather than a press release, has become a radical act. For a generation of leaders, being interesting is something to be avoided. Having lived a life of complexity and range is a risk to be managed, if not simply disqualifying. It is if having some sort of spark, humour, emotion, or quirk will ruin your career.

None of this is a call to become Pauline Hanson. But, it is an invitation for progressives to stop being so damn boring. Serious does not have to mean boring. Get a life, loosen up, and be interesting.
