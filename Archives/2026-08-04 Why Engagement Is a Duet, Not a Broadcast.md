### TEAMS! Newsletter | Engagement Edition | August 4, 2026

Early in my career, I sat through more all-hands meetings than I can count, where “engagement” meant a slide deck, a confident voice at the front of the room, and a Q&A that ran out of time before it ran out of hands. We called it communication. It was closer to a broadcast, with a single signal going out and hundreds of receivers hoping to pick up something useful. The room clapped politely and went back to their desks unchanged.

The nonprofit sector is not immune to this instinct, and I know this because I have caught myself doing it, too. It is tempting to treat engagement as a transmission problem: get the message right, deliver it with enough energy, and people will feel engaged. But engagement is not a transmission. It is closer to a duet: one voice offers a line, and another voice answers it, shapes it, and sends something new back. The music only works because both sides are listening as hard as they are playing.

### Executive Summary:

- **Broadcasting Isn’t Engagement.** A well-produced, one-way message confirms that leadership provided an update, which is important. But it does nothing to confirm *the team* was heard. It is one-way communication.
- **The Room Always Answers.** Silence, side conversations, and half-empty follow-through are responses too, just not the ones a “broadcast model” is designed to notice.
- **Improvisation Outperforms a Script.** Teams trusted to adapt in real time engage more deeply than teams handed a fixed set of lists and told to follow them.
- **Engagement Compounds When Everyone Gets a Solo.** Sustained participation depends less on a single rousing chorus than on every voice getting a turn to lead.

**Setting the Tempo.** Every duet starts with a tempo, and whoever sets it first sets the expectation for everyone who joins afterward. The same is true the moment someone new steps into a program or a new hire joins a team: the first weeks establish whether this is a place where they will be asked to keep up with a fixed rhythm or invited to help set one.

- ***Real*** - ***Life Example:*** At our Cooperative Housing residences, the difference is immediately apparent. Residents who are walked through move-in with a rushed checklist absorb a compliance tempo. Residents who are asked what pace of independence feels right for them and are checked in with accordingly absorb a tempo of partnership and support, and that tempo tends to be far more effective at building a relationship that can lead to greater independence.

**The Call and the Response.** A call without a response is just noise in a room. Genuine engagement requires a structural commitment to hearing the answer, not just issuing the question. A staff survey is a call. A published, specific account of what changed because of it is the response. Skip the response, and the team stops bothering to answer the call.

- ***Real-Life Example:*** A vocational job coach does not walk into a new job placement with a fixed script for how a participant should perform in the role. The coach opens with a “call,” asking what feels manageable today and what doesn’t, then shapes the coaching plan around the response.

**Room for a Solo.** No ensemble stays engaged for long if the same lead voice takes every solo. Engagement rises sharply the moment a quieter team member is given real ownership of a project rather than a supporting role in someone else’s. The solo does not need to be flawless. It needs to be genuinely theirs.

- ***Real-Life Example:*** We repeatedly see this play out in our Art program. The participants who return week after week are rarely the ones most closely directed, but rather the ones who were handed the brush and supported in making the canvas their own. Teams work the same way.

**Reading the Room.** A musician who keeps playing the planned set after the room has clearly shifted in mood is tone-deaf. Engagement requires the same situational awareness. Leadership teams that treat their quarterly plan flexibly, adjusting the next initiative based on the team’s actual energy rather than on what the calendar says is due, keep people leaning in instead of checking out.

***Real-Life Example:*** Our Camp Via West counselors read a cabin’s and a camper’s energy every single morning and adjust the day’s plan accordingly. The schedule is a starting point, not a contract.

**The Encore.** The songs that get an encore are rarely the loudest in the set; they are the ones the audience asked to hear again. Sustained engagement works the same way: it is built from the moments staff choose to revisit voluntarily. Recognize the encore-worthy moments as they happen, and people will show up wanting to give you another one.

- ***Real-Life Example:*** Our Stroke Awareness and Recovery program sees this play out over months, not minutes. Recovery is slow, and the participants who stay engaged through the hardest stretches are consistently the ones whose small wins are noticed and named in the moment, not filed away for an annual recap.

### Putting It into Action:

- **Trade an Announcement for a Call.** Replace one scheduled update this week with a genuine question, and build in time to truly hear the answers before you move on.
- **Think About Who Gets the Solo.** Review the last month of assignments and recognition. If the same two or three people are carrying every visible moment, hand the next one to someone new.
- **Close the Loop Within 48 Hours.** Whenever you collect feedback from staff, volunteers, or families, commit to naming one specific change that resulted from it within two days.
- **“Reading the Room” Check-In.** Before your next team meeting, ask one open question about energy and workload before opening the agenda, and let the answer shape the next hour.
- **Name the Encore-Worthy Moments.** Build a habit of flagging small wins in real time, such as an email or a hallway comment, rather than saving all recognition for a formal review.

### The Close

### A broadcast asks nothing of its audience but attention, and it gets exactly that much back. A duet asks for something harder, namely timing, trust, and the willingness to leave space for another voice, and it gives back something a broadcast never can: a team that actually wants to play the next set.

Engagement is the ongoing exchange between what leadership offers and what a team, a volunteer corps, or a room full of families is willing to give back. Broadcast long enough without listening, and eventually no one is left in the room to hear it. Build the duet instead, and the music keeps the room full.

> ***"Most people do not listen with the intent to understand; they listen with the intent to reply."*** **— Stephen R. Covey**
