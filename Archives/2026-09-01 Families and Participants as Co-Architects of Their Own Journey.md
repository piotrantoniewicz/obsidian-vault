### TEAMS! Newsletter | Engagement Edition | September 1, 2026

Working in the private sector for several decades taught me that every business plan includes a section on the “customer’s needs”; it usually starts with a premise crafted in a conference room, with a working group making their best guesses before “field testing” their ideas. In many cases, social services organizations take a similar approach, designing intake procedures, program schedules, and the physical and emotional spaces where participants spend their days. Yet far too often, these decisions are finalized before asking the people who will actually be in the programs and spaces what they think they should look like.

This isn’t a failure of care. Real-world pressures from licensing timelines, funding cycles, and staffing realities push us to have something ready before we open the door to a new program or space. But there’s a difference between being ready to welcome someone and being ready to build something with them. The first is hospitality. The second is partnership. This edition of TEAMS! is about closing that gap, bringing families and the people we serve into the design process with us, and treating their voices not as “feedback” but as design partners from the start.

### At a Glance

- **The first conversation should be an invitation.** How an organization frames intake sets the tone for every interaction that follows, and it should feel like an invitation, not a task.
- **Feedback that isn’t closed damages trust.** Collecting input without visibly acting on it doesn’t just waste people’s time; it teaches people to stop offering it.
- **Co-design isn’t a focus group. It’s a seat at the table.** The difference between consulting people and building alongside them shows up in outcomes, not intentions.
- **The words “serve” and “partner” produce different organizations.** Language is a leading indicator of where real decision-making power sits.

### The Five Pillars Family and Participant Engagement

**The Intake Conversation.** The first meaningful exchange with a family or a participant sets an unspoken agreement that shapes everything that follows. If that exchange is dominated by forms, eligibility criteria, and program rules, the implicit message is: you are here to receive what *we’ve built*. If it opens instead with genuine questions, such as what a good year looks like for you, what has and hasn’t worked before, and what you want us to know before we know anything else, the message shifts to: we are here to help you, and you are here to help us get this right.

This isn’t about adding more paperwork or more time to intake. It’s about sequencing. Organizations that lead with curiosity before paperwork report that families arrive at every later touchpoint, including goal planning, service adjustments, and transitions, already primed to speak up and actively engage.

**Feedback Loops That Actually Loop.** Most organizations are decent at gathering input: satisfaction surveys, quarterly family meetings. Far fewer follow through on closing the loop: going back, reporting how the feedback was used, and what changed as a result of what was shared. Without that second half, feedback collection becomes a hollow ritual rather than a step in building a deeper relationship.

Closing the loop requires establishing a consistent habit: a follow-up call, a line in a newsletter, or a note at the next meeting that names the input and the response. Families and participants who keep engaging over years are, almost without exception, those who’ve seen their input heard and, in many cases, acted on.

**Co-Design in Practice.** A meaningful gap exists between asking people what they think of a program and asking them to help build it. Co-design means bringing participants and families into the room while a service, a schedule, or a physical space is still being decided, not after it’s finished. It is slower up front but more enduring over time because the people who helped design it rarely need to be convinced to use it.

Even small decisions count as co-design: a rotating participant seat on a program-improvement committee, a family advisory role with real agenda-setting power, a pilot built jointly with the people it’s meant to serve before it’s scaled organization-wide.

**The Language of Ownership.** Language is one of the cheapest and most impactful levers an organization has. “We serve 400 families” and “We partner with 400 families” describe the same reality, but they reframe the relationship, which in turn trains staff, board members, and funders to think about power differently. Words like “case,” “client,” and “beneficiary” carry a one-directional flow of help. Words like “partner,” “co-architect,” and “member” carry mutual obligation and respect.

An honest review of language across intake documents, board materials, grant reports, and everyday internal shorthand often reveals more one-directional language than any team might expect. Changing it is free. Living up to the reframing is a worthy goal.

**Governance and Voice.** The clearest test of whether engagement is genuine or performative is governance: do families and participants have a seat at the table where decisions with real impact get made? Advisory councils that never touch budget, staffing, or program design do not reflect empowered partnership.

Reframing roles starts with a commitment to making every person served feel like a valued friend and partner. A valued friend has opinions that carry weight. Building governance structures that reflect that commitment turns a mission statement into a daily practice and part of the organization’s DNA.

### Your Action Playbook

- **Review your intake script.** Read it as if you were a new family hearing it for the first time. Count how many sentences are about compliance versus how many are genuine questions with a welcoming tone.
- **Set a loop-closing standard.** Commit to following up on every piece of family or participant feedback within a fixed time frame, even if it means saying “not yet” or “no, and here’s why.”
- **Create one standing seat.** Add a participant or family voice, with real influence, to one existing internal decision-making meeting this quarter.
- **Revisit language.** Scan your intake materials, board decks, and grant reports for one-directional service language, and rewrite the top offenders toward partnership language.
- **Pilot one co-designed element.** Choose a single program feature, a schedule, a space, an activity, and rebuild it jointly with the people who will use it before rolling it out.
- **Report back publicly.** Tell your broader community, in a newsletter, a meeting, or a family update, exactly what changed this year because someone spoke up.

### Closing Thoughts

None of this requires a new department or a new budget. It only requires a willingness to open the door to engagement from families and participants a little earlier in the process than may feel comfortable today. Trading “control” of a planning process may be slower, but it will likely lead to better outcomes when building it together.

> ***Organizations willing to change based on what they hear will build deeper engagement and better outcomes. That’s the real difference between a guest list and a blueprint.***
