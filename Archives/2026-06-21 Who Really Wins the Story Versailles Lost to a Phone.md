Power

21.06.2026

At the 2026 G7, Macron pulled off the summit's biggest diplomatic win, and Meloni's phone video stole the week. Today, we review five lessons for the communications officers who speak for elected officials.

\-

Picture two images from the same week

In the first, Emmanuel Macron is standing in the Palace of Versailles, three centuries of gold and chandeliers behind him, watching Donald Trump sign off on a deal to end the war with Iran. "Bravo," Macron says. The room, caught completely by surprise, breaks into applause.

In the second, Giorgia Meloni is sitting somewhere far less grand. Alone. Talking straight into the camera on her phone. No set, no podium, no press corps. Just a leader, a grievance, and a single line. "Neither I nor Italy ever beg."

One of those moments was the most important diplomatic moment of the entire summit. The other is the one you actually remember.

That gap, between what mattered and what travelled, is the whole subject of this piece. And it's written for one specific group, the communications officers who work for elected officials. The people who never get to be the principal, but who decide how the principal is seen. If that's you, Évian just handed you a masterclass and a warning in the same week. Read what follows as patterns, not recipes. Meloni's phone worked in her situation. It could be malpractice in yours. Knowing the difference is the job.

##### Lesson 1. The biggest move is almost never the one that wins the week

The moment behind. Macron spent the summit being what the press called the Trump whisperer. He kept Trump in the room when he'd walked out early in 2018 and again in 2025. He got him to sign the joint statement backing Ukraine, more air defence, tougher sanctions on Russia. And he choreographed the Iran signing at Versailles, possibly the last great act of his presidency. Serious, history-book diplomacy. And it got swamped inside forty-eight hours by a row about a photo.

The lesson. Substance and salience are two different currencies, and they almost never convert one to one. Your principal can win the room and still lose the week. If you assume the achievement will speak for itself, it won't. Something louder will speak over it.

In practice.

- Treat every real win as only half the job. The other half is the moment that carries it.

- Decide the shareable artifact before the win happens, not after, the clip, the line, the image.

- Never let the press release be the whole plan. Nobody shares a press release.

##### Lesson 2. A phone beat the entire machine

The moment behind. When Trump told Italian TV that Meloni had "begged" him for a photo, she didn't convene the comms shop. She didn't draft a statement, clear it with the lawyers, book a hall. She picked up her phone, looked dead into the lens, and posted to X within hours. No production. No budget. And it out-travelled every official channel at the summit.

The lesson. Here's the hard part for people in our job. The apparatus that exists to protect your principal is often the exact thing that kills the moment. By the time the statement clears its fourth approval, the window's shut. Speed and a human face beat process, almost every time.

In practice.

- Build a phone-first rapid-response protocol and rehearse it before you need it.

- Pre-authorise your principal to go direct to camera, so a fast reply doesn't need six sign-offs.

- One trusted aide, one phone, one take. Keep the chain short enough to move at the speed of the story.

##### Lesson 3. If it doesn't reduce to one line, it won't travel

The moment behind. "Neither I nor Italy ever beg." Five words. You can repeat it, caption it, put it on a placard. Now try to recall a single line from the actual G7 communiqué. You can't, and neither can anyone else. The substance had no line. The clapback had the only line that mattered.

The lesson. The line is the unit of memory. Not the policy, not the paragraph, the line. If your principal's position cannot survive being compressed into one repeatable sentence, it will not leave the room.

In practice.

- Write the line before the event, not in the panic afterwards.

- Test it on someone outside politics. If they can say it back a day later, it works.

- The best line does three things at once, it owns the frame, it carries dignity, and a stranger can repeat it.

##### Lesson 4. Emotion travels. Grandeur doesn't.

The moment behind. Think about what Macron actually built. Versailles. The most expensive stagecraft on the planet, an instrument of influence used by French leaders for three hundred years. And a woman with a handset out-shared all of it.

The lesson. Production value is not reach. The phone won because it had what the palace didn't, a clear antagonist, a real emotion, and speed. For an elected official, the glossy set-piece very often underperforms the candid, slightly raw, unmistakably human moment.

In practice.

- Stop over-producing. Polish reads as distance, and distance doesn't share.

- Let your principal be a person on camera, not a hologram of one.

- Give the audience a foil. People don't rally to a position, they rally against something, for someone.

##### Lesson 5. The real goal is to be both, and to know what it costs

The moment behind. Meloni won the feed. Macron won the substance. But notice the bill. Meloni's clapback didn't come free, her foreign minister cancelled a planned trip to Washington, and a fraying alliance frayed a little more. Macron's win, meanwhile, is real and durable and almost invisible. Each leader nailed one half and missed the other.

The lesson. The job is not to choose between the two. The operation you actually want does the real work like Macron and makes it travel like Meloni. And before you greenlight the viral moment, you count the cost, because a clapback that wins the day can lose a relationship that mattered more than the day.

In practice.

- Pair every substantive win with a deliberate plan to make it travel. Don't leave salience to chance.

- Before the clever response goes out, ask one question. What does this cost us tomorrow?

- Audit your principal honestly. All feed and no substance is a Meloni risk. All substance and no feed is a Macron one.

##### Win the room and the phone

The camera isn't going anywhere. The feed isn't slowing down. The officials who'll matter over the next decade are the ones whose people can do the unglamorous work that changes outcomes and make it land in a way ordinary humans actually feel. Versailles and the phone. Not one or the other. Both.
