Correct me if I’m wrong.

When I talk to teams building tools with AI features or development environments, I keep hearing the same thing: people don’t use these tools, or they work around them in their day-to-day work. Often they simply don’t want to switch to a new way of working.

Meanwhile, I look at conferences and posts about AI adoption and see: “Let’s automate,” “New model, new feature: here’s what you need to know,” “Process mapping is pointless.” And somewhere in the mix, GDPR and the AI Act sneak in.

What’s going on here?  
“AI adoption is the introduction of AI tools into everyday work.” That means someone has to actually use them. Regularly.

Yet we’re still stuck at the “introduction” stage, because it’s easy to sell: we share knowledge and don’t have to dig any deeper. But results don’t come from deployment. They come from use.  
And who’s supposed to use it? People.

Say you work in customer service. Your company bought you a license and organized a training. You know what the tool is, you know its features, and you saw the demo and the latest updates.  
Tomorrow you sit down at an inbox full of complaints and… you don’t know where to start.

Should the AI write the whole response, or just a draft? What can you paste in, and what’s off-limits?  
The training showed generic examples, not your actual tasks.

Then there’s a second question, more important than it seems: what happens if the AI makes a mistake and you don’t catch it? Who’s responsible? Will your manager appreciate that you’re trying, or ask why you handled 5 fewer tickets than usual?

Nobody told you. You have 50 tickets in the queue, so you do what you always do. The old way, fast and safe (for you).

That’s why, if you’re starting on AI adoption and building human–AI collaboration, it’s not enough to:  
◾ buy licenses,  
◾ run a training,  
◾ announce a strategy.

You also need to:  
◾ understand how people actually make decisions in their work, not how we assume they do,  
◾ build a decision environment in your organization that supports action,  
◾ remember that an AI rollout doesn’t have to look like yet another ChatGPT that serves everything up on a platter. The interface can support the decision-making process,  
◾ measure whether and how people actually use AI, not just how many have been trained.

Adoption comes down to micro-decisions. Sometimes the big changes aren’t in the implementation but in the communication.

I’m sorry to say it, but there’s a huge gap between deployment and actual use.  
So if you’re designing human–AI collaboration, build it around behaviors and decisions.  
  
Maybe I’m wrong, but more and more projects and teams are showing me I’m not.
