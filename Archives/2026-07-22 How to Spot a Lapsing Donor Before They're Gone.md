## What is a lapsed donor?

A lapsed donor is someone who's given before but hasn't made a gift within a defined window, usually 12 to 13 months, based on their typical giving cycles. If someone who gives every year skips a full year, they've lapsed. Monthly donors work differently: when a recurring gift stops, it shows up as a lapse much faster, sometimes within a month or two.

### Why does a lapsed donor report arrive too late to save the relationship?

You probably catch lapse the way most fundraising teams do: pull a report filtered by the last gift date. Thirteen or fourteen months since a gift, and the donor is officially lapsed. You work the list, make calls, send reactivation appeals.

That report has its place. But it's backward-looking. It tells you who's already gone, not who's about to leave.

Timing works against you here, and it has nothing to do with how hard you're working. A donor who gave last March and hasn't engaged since has had 12 months to drift. Their connection to your mission has had time to fade, and the ask you're about to make now is harder than the conversation you could have had months earlier.

You're already juggling appeals, stewardship, grant reporting, and event logistics. Fitting in parallel retention check-ins on top of that isn’t something you likely have much time for. To top it off, a lapsed report flags a problem after it happens. It was never built to help you get ahead of one.

What you're missing is a signal that arrives early enough to act on it and this is where [predictive donor intelligence](https://bloomerang.com/blog/predictive-intelligence-for-fundraisers) can help.

### Why does reaching a donor before they lapse lead to a better conversation?

Timing is everything here. Reaching a donor while they're still engaged gets you a warmer, more natural conversation than any reactivation call after the silence has already set in.

Your donors will tell you why they give, if you ask. In the [Bloomerang Giving Signals Report 2026](https://bloomerang.com/resources/giving-signals-report-2026), 87% of active donors say giving is values-driven, and 92% say it's not primarily transactional. People give because they believe in what you do, and because someone asked in a way that felt genuine.

A reactivation call to a donor who lapsed nine months ago asks them to reconnect with something they've already moved away from. A check-in with a donor who's still technically active, but starting to drift, reinforces a relationship that's still there.

Get to a donor before the silence stretches long enough to feel permanent, and the conversation stays natural. Ask how the program they funded is going. Share a story from that work. Invite them to something without it feeling like a rescue call.

That's what predictive intelligence gets you. Not more calls. Better-timed ones.

### How should you prioritize your lapse risk list when outreach time is limited?

An at-risk list is only as useful as what you do with it. Once you can see it, the strategy is simple: prioritize by relationship depth, not just score. A major donor with a strong personal connection and a moderate risk score probably earns a phone call. A mid-level donor with a high score and no recent personal touch might get a warm email first.

[A few principles](https://bloomerang.com/blog/turning-lapsed-donors-into-devoted-donors) that hold across portfolio sizes:

- **Act early.** A donor at 60% lapse risk is a much easier conversation than one at 90%. The window is wider, the ask is softer, and the relationship is warmer.
- **Personalize the contact.** Reference something specific: a gift they made, a program they've funded, a connection they've built with your organization. You have that history in the record. Use it.
- **Don't lead with the ask.** Retention outreach that opens with a donation request misses the point. Lead with the relationship. Check in. Share impact. The ask comes later, or it comes on its own.
- **Log everything.** Whether the outreach lands or not, document it. The system learns from what worked and what didn't, which sharpens the next round of scores.

If you manage a [recurring giving program](https://bloomerang.com/blog/3-strategies-to-keep-your-recurring-donors), lapse risk scoring matters even more. A recurring donor who cancels is a different kind of loss than a one-time donor who doesn't return, and the patterns that predict a cancellation are detectable before it happens.
