In the Harvard Business Review article by Alison Wood Brooks and Andy Atkins, *“How the Best Leaders Shape Conversations”* (August 17, 2026), the two make a case that is as demanding as it is easy to understand: a team’s work is driven by the engine of its conversations. Brooks, who heads TALK Studios at Harvard Business School, and Atkins, a global practice leader with BTS, call the art of managing those exchanges **conversational stewardship**. Most encouraging is their insistence that it is a skill one can acquire.

They back this up with hard numbers. In surveys of more than 1,000 people from 100-plus teams across nearly 70 organizations.

> ***They found that meeting frequency was not an indicator of success. How the time was spent was.***

A team that left a session with defined owners and next steps was four times as likely to be deemed effective as one that did not.

At AbilityPath, we consider this a matter of Talent, not just a matter of meetings. People grow through conversations. A job coach may recognize what is great during a debrief about a difficult placement. A teacher with Learning Links may find her voice in the huddle or choose to be quiet. Our program coordinators and supervisors learn to run meetings by watching their managers.

We place great value on developing leaders from within through several programs we offer, such as *S.C.A.L.E.* for senior leaders, *You as a Leader for managers and coordinators,* and *Everyone a Leader* for new supervisors and leads. But this article reminds us that meetings are already classrooms, so we need to use the time wisely and teach with intent.

Brooks and Atkins define relationship stewardship as three elements: **goals, roles, and soul**. From the campfire, you can see why we are there, who is tending it, and whether it is warm enough for open talk.

**Why we gathered.** The authors say our conversations should be equally clear and be divided into **purpose, process, and products**.

- **Purpose** answers why we are in the room.
- **Process** is how we handle disagreement and let ideas come forward.
- **Products** are what we take with us, whether a plan or a better understanding of one another.

The data is clear: teams that make a habit of recording next steps score almost a standard deviation higher in overall effectiveness.

For example, Atkins tells the story of a Fortune Global 25 leadership team at odds for months, with the functional and client sides each certain the other was blind to their pressures. When they reframed the discussion to serve the entire organization, they found a solution in 90 minutes.

> ***Their research shows that putting a shared goal on the table early eases friction.***

For us, that goal is seldom elusive; it is usually in the room with us, embodied in the families or participants we serve.

**Who is tending?** A group can lose its way if it is unclear who is responsible for keeping things moving. The authors make a point of naming the facilitator as the one who should do the work of stewardship: drawing people in, handling dissent constructively, and advancing the day’s business without taking over.

Having a decision-maker in the room means someone is on the hook for the result, which prevents the group from drifting into choices no one can truly put their name to. It also forces clarity about what the rest of us are required to do. Do we all have to put in our two cents? Is this a vote? Is someone here to be blocked by or to block? Or are they just there to get context and take the work from here?

> ***What struck me was the reminder that these roles don’t necessarily follow the org chart.***

A junior person can facilitate; an expert can hold the key view; a senior leader can do nothing but listen for the duration of the meeting. The article tells of a chairman at a 150-year-old company who had to decide on a potential acquisition bid. He made a point of staying silent throughout a multi-day discussion so his voice would not sway the room.

I will confess I have been in many a meeting thinking I was listening when, in fact, I was steering. As a leader, it is often most useful to step away from the conversation and let someone else have the floor. Not only is it a sound way to develop people, but it is also one of the most impactful team-building and talent development tools we have. Putting an emerging leader in the facilitator’s chair gives them the kind of practice every role will eventually require.

**Is it safe enough to share?** Then there is the matter of whether people are comfortable enough to speak. The authors focus on getting to the heart of the conversation. They put trust in two boxes:

- **Relational**: The assurance that others respect you.
- **Transactional:** The confidence in their capability.

You build both through transparency and humor in the ordinary course of things. But psychological safety is something else. Citing Amy Edmondson of Harvard Business School, the article defines it as:

> ***A shared conviction in the room that you can be candid without fear of being put down or punished.***

I found that to be the piece’s most valuable idea. You can have strong one-on-one relationships with your colleagues, yet they go quiet in meetings, hesitant to share an idea or offer a contrary opinion. Brooks and Atkins’s data show that open teams, those willing to discuss hard truths, make better decisions and spot risks earlier. The converse is sobering. At an internet provider, leadership could see that expenses were outpacing revenue, but no one raised it at the table. Two years later, the business was history.

This lesson really resonates with us. Our mission is grounded in the belief that everyone has a seat at the table and a voice in the communities we serve. We should extend the same care to the rooms where we serve one another.

We tend to think good conversation will happen on its own, but it seldom does. I like that this article does not call for a new system or a consultant to come in and fix things. Instead, they offer an alternative. The habits that improve it are small, but they add up over time to shape the culture people either want to be part of or will quietly walk away from. Here are three ways we can put their ideas into practice this week.

- To start, use the authors’ suggestion to open a team talk with “By the end of this, we should…,” and close it by assigning ownership of the next steps.
- Put the roles on the table: who is running the show, who is making the call, and who is there to advise or challenge.
- Even better, give the reins to someone who hasn’t held them before.

Before the meeting is done, ask a question meant to elicit candor rather than a nod of agreement. “Whose perspective are we not hearing?” or “What concern haven’t we put on the table?” When you get an answer, be curious, not defensive. That is how you help develop your team members, build engagement, and achieve better results.

> ***The best conversations are not something you find. They are something you nurture.***
