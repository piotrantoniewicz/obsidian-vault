Our AI audit came back with 21 things we could build. Twelve of them were rated high impact. The work that actually got finished since then was all from the easy end.

That is the whole lesson of this edition.

This is Part 3 of four. [Part 1 was about building your own judgment](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-tell-answer-ai-just-gave-you-any-good-wendy-clow-isixf) before you trust anything the AI hands you. Part 2 was about [deciding, as an organization, what may go into the tool at all](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-policy-we-approved-last-week-wendy-clow-pvigf), and it ended by naming the work we had to rule out. This one picks up on the other side of that line. Here is what was left, what we did with it, and what happened.

### Where the list came from

A couple of months ago I sat down with people across five programs at Habitat for Humanity of Michigan, where I run operations, and asked each of them to walk me through their real work. I wanted the day-in-the-life version rather than the job-description version, including the parts everybody knows are redundant and nobody has time to fix.

Twenty-one opportunities came out of those conversations. Each one got scored twice, once for how much it would help and once for how hard it would be to build.

Here is the shape of the list:

- High impact, low difficulty: 4
- High impact, medium difficulty: 4
- High impact, high difficulty: 4
- Medium impact, medium difficulty: 7
- Medium impact, low difficulty: 2

Twelve of the 21 are high impact. Only four of those twelve are also low difficulty.

### Both ends of the list

The four that are high impact and low difficulty are almost boring to read. A searchable place to keep our written procedures, so people stop asking the same three colleagues the same questions. Meeting recordings turned into task lists and follow-up drafts. Reply drafts for the client emails we answer forty times a month. Triage on a shared inbox so the urgent things surface above the routine ones.

None of that will impress anyone at a conference. Every one of them can be running inside a week by the person who has the problem.

The four at the other end are the ones you actually want. One client record that holds together across four systems. A documentation setup for our housing counselors, who currently spend thirty to forty-five minutes writing up every appointment and enter a good deal of it twice. Compliance checking on the hardest ten percent of our closings, which is where nearly all the pain lives. Electronic intake forms so nobody retypes a PDF into a spreadsheet again.

Those four are still untouched, and they are untouched for a reason that has nothing to do with AI. Each one needs systems connected, data cleaned up, and money approved.

### The re-sort

Impact tells you what to want. Difficulty tells you what you can finish.

If you have a technology department, sorting by impact is reasonable, because you have somebody whose whole job is turning hard things into done things. If you are a nonprofit with five to fifty staff and no dedicated technical person, sorting by impact puts everything you cannot complete at the top of your list, and you will spend your first three months there.

So we re-sorted by difficulty and worked from the easy end. Here is what came of it.

### What actually got built

**Grant research.** This is the one I would point to first. The workflow is unremarkable in description: before anyone writes a word of an application, the AI reads the funder's guidelines, eligibility rules, exclusions, and FAQ.

I ran it this month across four different funding needs at once. It produced three applications genuinely worth starting, one renewal conversation that matters more than all three, and roughly a dozen phone calls that need making. That is a modest yield and I want to be honest that it is modest.

The most valuable thing it returned was a rule rather than a funder. Check the funder's geography and their charter before you look at program fit, because a perfect program match at a funder that only gives inside one county is still a rejection.

That rule now lives in the file the workflow reads every time it runs, so I do not have to remember it.

**Content and communications.** Less dramatic and more consistent. The content work runs about five to six hours a month lighter than it used to. That is a real number, it repeats every month, and it came from the low-difficulty end of exactly this kind of list.

Neither of those scaled until two unexciting things existed first. We needed a written data policy so staff knew [what they were allowed to put into the tool](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-policy-we-approved-last-week-wendy-clow-pvigf). And we needed training, which turned into ten short recorded videos and a small first group working through them together, with our CEO in that group rather than watching from the outside.

*Neither a policy nor a training program is an AI feature. Both had to exist before any of the AI mattered, and between them they took more of my time than every workflow on this list combined.*
