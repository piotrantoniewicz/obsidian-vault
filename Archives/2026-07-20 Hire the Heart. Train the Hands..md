### TEAMS! Newsletter | Talent Edition | July 20, 2026

***Why Interviewing for Values, Not Just Skills, Improves Mission Impact***

Early in my career, I hired based on résumé line items. I looked closely at educational background, technical skills, and former employer pedigree, and, more often than I'd like to admit, I got burned. The skills were real. The person behind them wasn't who the résumé promised. It took me longer than it should have to learn what should have been obvious: you can teach most skills. You cannot as easily teach someone to care, to be driven, or to be a good teammate.

That lesson is incredibly important in the nonprofit and social services sector. When a hire doesn't work out here, it's a family that trusted us with their loved one's care, a participant who bonds with a job coach and then watches them leave, or a Camp Via West counselor whose patience runs out at exactly the wrong moment. We recently put together an Interview Playbook, built around a set of questions designed to do one thing well: get past the rehearsed answer and into the real person. It's the foundation for this edition and is reshaping how we think about hiring for every open req on our team.

### Executive Summary

- **Skills are teachable; values aren't.** A candidate can learn a case management system in a week. Learning to sit with a family in crisis with compassion takes something that the job description can't instill.
- **The best interview questions resist rehearsal.** Generic questions elicit generic answers. Questions that ask for the first thing that comes to mind, or the same story told from three angles, are much harder to game.
- **Motivation reveals itself sideways, not head-on.** Why someone is leaving their last job tells you more about who they are than almost anything they'll say when asked directly about their strengths.
- **A single interview means little; a baseline means everything.** The real value of a strong question set isn't any one conversation; it's what you learn when you ask the same questions across dozens of hires and track how those answers predicted performance.

### Skills Get You In the Door. Values Keep You in the Room.

Every hiring manager already screens for skills; it's the easy part. The résumé, the work sample, and the reference check on technical competence: all of that is verifiable before a candidate ever sits across from you. What's much harder to see, and what predicts whether someone lasts eighteen months or eighteen years in mission-driven work, is whether they have an accurate read on their own strengths, blind spots, and impact on the people around them. That's not a skills question. It's a character question, and it doesn't show up on a résumé at all.

> ***Hire for attitude, train for skill.*** *—Herb Kelleher, co-founder, Southwest Airlines*

### The Core Four: Questions Built to Resist a Rehearsed Answer.

Our interview question playbook opens with four questions to ask in every interview, regardless of role or level:

- How would your boss, peers, and team each describe you?
- What’s the first challenging situation that comes to mind, and how did you resolve it?
- What are your three strongest qualities and the one you're working to develop?
- Where do you see yourself in three to five years?

None of these is clever. That's the point. A candidate who gives an identical, glowing answer across bosses, peers, and the team is describing a self-image, not a real working relationship. Texture and a little friction are usually the more honest answer. And the instruction to name the first challenging situation that comes to mind, rather than the polished one, is a deliberate short-circuit around a standard answer they may have rehearsed.

### Motivation Reveals Itself Sideways.

The strongest interviews don't march through a script; they follow the story and ask the next logical question. Why did you leave your last role, or why are you leaving now, is one of the highest-yield questions in any interview because the answer reveals whether someone is moving toward something (growth, mission, scope) or away from something. Both are legitimate, but they point to very different futures. What attracted you to this specific opportunity separates candidates who've done real homework on your organization and our current moment from those running a generic job search. And what your first ninety days would look like tells you whether someone plans to listen first or arrives with a fixed playbook, regardless of the culture they're walking into.

> ***Hire for character. Train for skills.*** *—Peter Schutz, former President and CEO, Porsche AG*

### From Gut Check to Baseline.

The real power of a strong question set isn't in any single interview; it's in what happens when you ask the same core questions consistently across many candidates and hires and track the pattern between how someone answered and how they performed once hired. That pattern is your baseline, and building one takes discipline. Most of us skip writing down the candidate's exact words immediately after the interview, tag the outcome at the 30-, 60-, and 90-day marks, and go back later to note whether the hire was a strong fit, a mixed one, or a miss. No single answer will ever be a perfect predictor. A baseline built across ten, twenty, or thirty hires will start to tell you something valuable.

### Where This Shows Up: Our Proof Points.

This isn't theoretical for us. The teachers who make an impact aren't always the ones with the most polished résumés; they're the ones whose answer to "why did you leave your last job?" was a desire to do work that mattered, not an escape from a bad manager. The counselors who thrive are the ones who, when asked to name a challenging situation, tell a story about staying calm through it rather than one where something simply happened to them. Our vocational job coaching team runs on exactly the kind of self-awareness the playbook is designed to surface, because coaching someone else toward independence requires knowing your own patience, limits, and blind spots first. Values-based hiring isn't a nicety; it is a commitment to lifelong learning and growth. It's the mechanism that makes that commitment real, one hire at a time.

### The Action Playbook

- **Pick your core five to seven questions.** Commit to asking them, in roughly the same form, to every candidate for a given role type. Consistency is what makes comparison and, eventually, a baseline possible.
- **Ask before you pitch.** If you sell the candidate the role first, you'll get answers shaped to match what you just said rather than the candidate's honest starting point.
- **Write down the quote, not the vibe.** "Good energy" tells you nothing six months later. The candidate's actual words, captured immediately after the interview, are something you can test against how they show up.
- **Let silence do its job.** These are first-thing-that-comes-to-mind questions, on purpose. Resist the urge to fill a pause; the more considered second attempt is usually the more rehearsed.
- **Tag the outcome at 30, 60, and 90 days.** Go back and note how the person actually performed (strong, mixed, or a miss) and, if possible, why. This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the one that turns a question log into a real baseline.
- **Recalibrate for the role.** For an individual contributor, deep technical specificity is a green flag, but it may be a yellow flag for a leadership role, where you also need to see evidence of impact on others. Keep separate baselines by role type.

### Closing Thoughts

You can't force a values match through a faster process or a sharper scorecard. What you can do is ask better questions, write down what you actually hear rather than what you hoped to hear, and let the pattern reveal itself over years rather than in a single interview. In our case, a hiring playbook gives every hiring manager on our team a way to build that pattern deliberately rather than by accident. The résumé will always tell you what someone has done. Only a real conversation tells you who they are; that's the hire that actually holds and makes an impact.
