### TEAMS! | Stewardship Edition | June 26, 2026

Recently, our Finance Committee reviewed the FY27 budget and asked some tough questions about our strategy for the coming year and beyond.

We came in with a plan, a deliberate pivot to get ahead of the headwinds facing our sector before they overtake us. We believe in that plan. Then someone asked the question I have come to respect: what if it doesn't work fast enough? If the pivot doesn't play out as we hope, what is our Plan B? Do we keep investing, or do we retrench?

The call got quieter. Because that is the real question, and there is no comfortable answer to it.

I want to focus this edition on that moment because I think it sits at the very center of what stewardship means, and because I want to speak directly to the board members and committee volunteers who ask questions like that one.

> ***Your engagement is not a courtesy we tolerate. It is a gift that makes this organization better. Let me explain why.***

### The headwinds we did not choose

First, the terrain. The questions only make sense against it. Our sector is absorbing pressures none of us created. Inflation and the cost of living keep climbing faster than the wages we can offer the Direct Support Professionals, who are the heart of our work. State rate changes have actually *reduced* the reimbursement we receive for the services we provide, not held it flat, but reduced it. Universal TK is reshaping demand for our Learning Links preschools. And HR-1, along with the upcoming changes to special education and Medicaid, threatens to directly affect the lives of the community members who depend on us most.

We did not write any of those policies. We did not set the inflation rate. These are structural forces that affect the entire IDD field, and no amount of careful budgeting makes them disappear.

So we made a choice. Rather than wait and react, we are investing ahead of the curve, pivoting to build the capacity, partnerships, and services we believe will carry us through. That is the plan the committee was examining. Investing ahead of uncertainty is exactly the kind of decision that warrants hard questions.

### A steward manages a house that they do not own

The word *steward* tells us something. A steward is entrusted with a household they did not build and do not own. Their authority is real, but it is borrowed. Because it is borrowed, the steward is answerable to the founders, the family, and the people who will live in that house long after the steward is gone.

That is our situation. None of us built AbilityPath. We inherited a 106-year-old mission, and one day we will pass it on to someone else. We are accountable to people we will never meet: those who founded this incredible organization in 1920 and those who will need us in 2050.

Once we embrace stewardship this way, the relationship to scrutiny is completely reversed. A steward who resents being asked to account for the house is not a steward at all; that is an owner protecting turf.

> ***The willingness to be questioned is not separate from stewardship. It is stewardship.***

This is why the board's hard questions are not friction to manage. They are the mechanism that keeps a mission honest. When a committee member presses on our Plan B, they are doing the work of stewardship on behalf of everyone who came before us and those who will come after.

### Why this differs from the corporate world

I spent over thirty years in finance and technology, where I learned a great deal about budgets, boards, and hard choices. But I want to be honest about a difference that matters.

In the corporate world, you make cuts to improve the bottom line and satisfy shareholders. Sometimes those cuts come at the expense of employees. They are difficult, often painful decisions, but the logic is familiar: protect the financial result and answer to the people who hold the stock.

In the social sector, that logic inverts in a way that changes everything. The people we serve are our shareholders. A budget cut here is not a number that disappoints an investor on a quarterly call. It is a person who does not receive the service they were counting on. It is a family without respite. It is a child without the support needed to learn. When we balance a budget on the backs of our programs, the cost is not abstract or merely financial. It is personal, and it shows up in someone's daily life the very next morning. That is why the Plan B question carries such weight.

> ***Retrenchment in social services not a spreadsheet exercise. It has a face. It is another human being. It is a family. It is a community.***

### Holding optimism and reality at the same time

Here is the challenge I want every reader to sit with because it doesn’t resolve neatly and I don’t think it should.

Stewardship in a moment like this is neither optimism nor caution. It is the discipline of holding both at once. We have to invest ahead of the headwinds, with genuine hope that our pivot will work, while remaining clear-eyed and unflinching about the possibility it might not. Hope without that honesty is just wishful thinking with a budget attached. Caution without hope is retreat. Neither, by itself, is stewardship.

What lets us hold both together is a commitment to use every dollar as wisely as if someone's daily life depended on it, because it does. Investing ahead of uncertainty is an act of faith. Doing so responsibly is an act of stewardship. The two coexist only when we are honest about the possibility of being wrong, and when that honesty makes us more disciplined, not less, with the resources entrusted to us.

The Plan B question forces that honesty into the open. It does not let us pretend the pivot is guaranteed. It makes us name the conditions under which we would change course and prove that we are spending today's resources in a way we could defend tomorrow. That is not a question that gets in the way of the mission. That is the mission, protecting itself.

### To the people who ask the hard questions

This edition is, in part, a thank-you.

To our board and committee members: the questions you ask make us better. When you press our assumptions, ask what happens if we are wrong, and refuse to let optimism substitute for a plan, you are not slowing us down. You are doing the truest work of stewardship on behalf of people, many of whom you will likely never meet. I would rather sit in the room that pushes than in the one that nods along. That pushing is what keeps a 106-year-old mission worthy of the next hundred years.

To our teams: I hope you carry that same approach into this work. Bring pointed questions to the table. Ask how our plans affect you and the people we serve. Ask what HR-1 does to a family that depends on us. Ask whether our plan is honest about the headwinds or merely hopeful about them.

We are stewards of something we did not build, for people we will never meet, and against forces we did not choose. That is a demanding place to stand. The hard questions do not make it any harder. They are how we stay worthy of it.

> ***Stewardship and accountability are how we honor what we were handed in service to others we care deeply about.***
