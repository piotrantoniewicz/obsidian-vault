### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI Edition | August 12, 2026

Twenty-five years in corporate technology leadership, much of it scaling global development operations, taught me a fundamental truth: enterprise scale came from having the most people who knew what to do with it. The most vital indicators for a line of business rarely appeared on an executive dashboard first. They emerged in conversations, noticed by someone close enough to the daily work to spot operational shifts long before a standardized report caught up to the current reality. That knowledge lived in people, not in software, dashboards, or KPIs. This is still true today, even as AI usage explodes.

Today, as the chief executive of a social impact organization, I see artificial intelligence sweeping through the philanthropic and mission-driven sectors at breathtaking speed. It is immensely tempting to let these powerful platforms speak *for* us, to draft strategic frameworks, author participant assessments, synthesize complex stakeholder feedback, or summarize meetings we lacked the bandwidth to attend. AI will accomplish these tasks with incredible speed and reasonable competence, saving us time, which we can refocus on our mission-related work.

However, the moment an automated tool begins *speaking for us* rather than *with* us, we surrender the foundational currency of the social sector: institutional trust, hyper-local domain knowledge, and ethical human judgment. Deploying artificial intelligence successfully is not a technology implementation challenge; it is an executive governance discipline. Nonprofit leaders and foundation boards must establish where automation ends, and human accountability begins. This is how trust is built, and we can best serve our community. Today’s TEAMS! The newsletter provides a roadmap that can serve as a starting point for creating a structure to keep the human voice present in your use of AI.

### Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence offers transformative operational efficiency, but unmanaged adoption risks ceding core values and institutional trust to black-box algorithms. Nonprofit executives and board members must implement deliberate governance frameworks that treat tacit frontline knowledge as an unassailable asset, set clear human-in-the-loop operational boundaries, continuously audit platform usage, and foster board-level AI literacy. AI must be deployed to augment human capability and reduce administrative friction, preserving organizational voice and mission fidelity.

> ***Trusted AI integration requires leaders to use technology to amplify human expertise rather than replace the irreplaceable judgment and trust that frontline teams carry.***

### Five Key Takeaways

- **Local Knowledge Is an Unassailable Asset:** Tacit, frontline expertise cannot be extracted or codified into algorithms; AI must amplify local experts rather than replace them.
- **Human Participation Is an Operational Choice:** Routine administrative workflows can be fully automated, but touchpoints involving human dignity and mission alignment require human oversight.
- **Reject Third-Party Default Values:** Vendor-supplied AI defaults silently dictate ethical standards unless organizations explicitly define their own boundaries for platform use.
- **Governance Demands Scheduled Audits:** Trust is sustained through recurring, formal reviews of data exposure, privacy agreements, and active human override controls.
- **Literacy Precedes Board Oversight:** Boards and executives must develop practical technical fluency to govern AI meaningfully rather than rubber-stamping policy templates.

### Beyond the Algorithm: Five Pillars of Human-Centered Governance

**Local Knowledge Is the Asset, Not the Obstacle.** Every high-performing organization relies on deep, tacit knowledge that never makes it into an employee handbook or training manual. Consider Ray, a veteran vocational job coach on our team. Ray can read a program participant’s morning demeanor in thirty seconds, immediately discerning whether the day calls for a standard routine or a gentler approach, and quietly adapt a shift at a corporate partner site before anyone needs to intervene.

Over three decades of partnership, no one recorded that nuanced judgment because tacit knowledge does not remain still long enough to be captured in a policy document. It is earned, dynamic, hyper-local, and updated every day through human connection.

Too many technology initiatives treat this unstructured human expertise as an operational inefficiency, a gap that software needs to close or standardize away. That is a critical strategic error. The organizations that extract the most enterprise value from AI are those that deploy it to support the people who carry local knowledge. By clearing administrative burdens, summarizing complex data, and drafting initial marketing collateral, AI frees frontline personnel to double down on their core human capabilities.

> ***AI should make a skilled practitioner faster and more focused on being themselves; it should never attempt to impersonate them.***

**Human Participation Is a Design Choice, Not a Delay.** Keeping human judgment embedded in AI-supported operations is not about resisting technological progress for its own sake. An automated tool should execute what it can reliably do without intervention. Tasks such as generating first-draft intake summaries, flagging calendar scheduling conflicts, or pulling baseline donor analytics from existing databases require no manual oversight.

> ***The primary design question for leadership lies in delineating low-risk operational automation from high-stakes mission touchpoints.***

At our inclusive preschools, an AI-generated draft lesson plan serves as “raw material” that an educator can quickly and effectively tailor to better meet the students’ needs. By contrast, a family support plan directly affects an individual’s personal goals, lived history, and fundamental dignity. That document requires absolute human review and endorsement before it is used. A trustworthy architecture intentionally designs for this boundary from day one, rather than applying automation uniformly across all workflows for mere convenience.

**Reject Default “Values” from Vendors.** Every commercial artificial intelligence tool arrives pre-configured with default settings, underlying developer assumptions, and factory-set safety thresholds. Left unmanaged, these third-party configurations silently supplant an institution's own values.

Leaders must deliberately establish explicit rules specifying what automated systems are permitted to do, what is strictly forbidden, and what requires formal sign-off. Organizations must codify these standards in their own words rather than accepting vendor terms by default. Software providers can supply scalable computation, but they cannot define institutional mission or ethics.

**Auditing Is the Discipline That Sustains Trust.** Well-intentioned governance statements fade quickly without structural enforcement. Governance requires a recurring operational review that forces leadership to examine actual usage patterns rather than assuming company policies are being followed.

To maintain operational integrity, at our organization we engaged an external firm to conduct an agreed-upon procedures audit of every platform that processes organizational data, with a focus on areas where AI intersects with our systems.

The goal of a recurring audit is not to claim flawless performance but to create predictable opportunities for managers to justify tool deployment in clear language. Scheduled accountability turns abstract claims of "human-centered AI" into verified, enforceable policy.

**Build Institutional Fluency from the Boardroom Down.** In 2014, Toyota made headlines by reintroducing master human craftsmen to its highly automated factory floors. Senior executive Mitsuru Kawai explained the strategic rationale simply: mastering an automated system requires the fundamental skills to teach it.

That principle applies directly to knowledge work and the social sector. An organization that cannot explain what a software tool executes on its behalf has not modernized; it has abdicated effective oversight.

**Achieving AI fluency requires structured educational commitment at all leadership levels:**

- **Board Governance:** Trustees must move beyond approving generic policy templates. Governance bodies should complete practical literacy modules—understanding the mechanics of large language models and working through scenario-based exercises using real enterprise figures.
- **Workforce Capability:** Staff development must move beyond basic user training. Teams require sufficient technical fluency to use automated tools efficiently while maintaining a critical eye for identifying subtle error modes, hallucinations, and bias.

### Your Playbook to Retain Your Organization's Voice

- **Maintain an Active AI Asset Inventory:** Catalog every automated tool that interacts with client, financial, or personnel records. Organizations routinely run significantly more unmapped AI applications than executive teams realize.
- **Classify Data Sensitivity Pre-Deployment:** Define strict data-access policies and privacy thresholds before deploying new platforms.
- **Assign Single-Point Process Ownership:** Designate an accountable human owner for each automated output, someone explicitly responsible for reviewing accuracy and ethical standards.
- **Institutionalize Recurring AI Use Audits:** Embed platform reviews in the annual operating calendar to continuously evaluate actual usage against stated policies.
- **Develop Practical Board Literacy:** Educate board members on core AI capabilities so oversight reflects informed strategic guidance rather than passive rubber-stamping.

### The Strategic Horizon

Strategic plans often focus on honoring individual journeys through growth, adaptability, and deep personal commitment. Advanced technology does not alter those core human objectives. Artificial intelligence can help us expand reach, increase operational velocity, and recover thousands of staff hours that can be reallocated to direct relationships.

> ***AI software cannot evaluate what human relationships fundamentally require. Only People can.***

The organizations that lead effectively in the coming years will be those that maintain fluency in their values, protect human judgment, and leverage modern tools without surrendering their institutional voice.

***"Computers are incredibly fast, accurate, and stupid. Human beings are incredibly slow, inaccurate, and brilliant. Together they are powerful beyond imagination."***
