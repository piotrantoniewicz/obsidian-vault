### TEAMS! Newsletter | AI & Technology Edition | September 30, 2026

*Sixty-four percent of workers used AI on the job this year. Only 51 percent say they can get the training needed to use it well.*

Those two numbers moved in opposite directions. PwC's 2026 Hopes and Fears survey, released this week, found AI use up by ten points from last year. Access to learning fell from 59 percent to 51 percent. More people are using the tools, yet fewer are being taught how to use them properly and with confidence.

This gap is today’s story. Not whether our teams are using AI, because we already know they are. The question now is who is learning to use it well and who is being left to figure it out on their own.

### Two speeds

PwC surveyed nearly 50,000 workers, divided into four groups.

- **Front-Runners:** 14 percent pair scarce skills with strong AI ability.
- **AI Insurgents:** 18 percent use the tools to punch above their role.
- **Indispensables:** 11 percent have scarce skills but haven't picked up the tools yet.
- **The Engine Room:** 56 percent of the workforce lacks needed skills and much AI experience, and only two in five say they can get the learning they need.

Peter Brown, PwC's global workforce leader, warned that the workforce is starting to move at two speeds. The front-runners are already moving. They're far more likely to ask for a promotion, and nearly a third expect to change employers within a year. These groups are based partly on self-reported skills, so treat them as a lens, not a given fact. But it's a lens worth looking through in your own organization.

### The Engine Room

In our sector, the engine room isn't an abstraction. It includes direct support professionals, classroom aides, drivers, and job coaches. It's most of our workforce, and it's where the mission lives, and the work happens.

Here's one finding every nonprofit leader should take seriously. Daily AI users were more confident in their job security, at 68 percent compared with 57 percent for infrequent users. They were 15 points more likely to trust top management and 21 points more confident they could learn new skills. Learning to use the tools didn't make people more anxious. **It made them steadier and more likely to trust their leaders**.

A new software tool or process that nobody is taught to use doesn't fail loudly. It fails quietly, one workaround at a time, until the real system and the documented system are two different things. The fix was never just a better tool. It was teaching people how to use it effectively.

### Guardrails were the first ask

When we asked every department what they needed from AI, people asked for guardrails first. We’re building them with thoughtful restrictions and built-in rules, such as which data never goes into a model and where human judgment remains non-negotiable.

Guardrails answer what not to do. They don't answer the next question: how to do it well. PwC's data suggests this second question separates a steady, more empowered workforce from an anxious one. It's also where the Engine Room is most often skipped.

The risk for organizations like ours isn't that frontline staff won't use AI. It's that the back office learns fast, the frontline learns by guessing, and we mistake the average for the whole, and ultimately leave the largest part of our teams on an island.

### This week

Start with a conversation, not a curriculum. Sit down with one of your frontline teams and ask what they'd want to learn about AI if the training were designed for them. Then look honestly at who has attended any AI learning so far, sorted by role rather than by department. The pattern will likely tell you more than a survey would.

From there, keep it small and real. Pick one routine frontline task, such as a schedule-change note to families, and build a fifteen-minute training session around doing it well and safely. And make sure that first session is one where you thank the person who admits they don't know, not correct them. That's one of the best ways to support the Engine Room as it learns and grows.

### For reflection

If you mapped your team to PwC's four groups, how many would fall into the engine room? And when was the last time any of them learned something new about these tools, rather than being warned about them?

> ***Everyone is using your AI tools. The teaching and support must to catch up to their needs.***
