## Ecosystems Are Not Programs: Why Social Impact Work Needs Flow, Trust, and Interdependence

  

The nonprofit sector knows how to build programs.

We build workshops.  
We build campaigns.  
We build conferences.  
We build coalitions.  
We build strategic plans with colour-coded workbacks that make us feel, for one beautiful second, like everything is under control.

And sometimes, those things are exactly what we need. But not always. Because the kind of work many of us are actually trying to do, the work of creating care, trust, belonging, collaboration, shared learning, and long-term change, does not always behave like a program.

It behaves more like an ecosystem. And ecosystems are harder to control. (Annoying, I know.)

But also kind of the point.

This was the thread running through a recent Nonprofit Hive LinkedIn Live conversation with three people who understand ecosystem-building from very different angles: [Pete Bo](https://www.linkedin.com/in/petebombaci/) [m](https://www.linkedin.com/in/petebombaci/) [baci](https://www.linkedin.com/in/petebombaci/), Founder and CEO of GenWell, [Osayi Alile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/osayi-alile-1351896/), CEO of Aspire Coronation Trust Foundation, and [Ami Dar](https://www.linkedin.com/in/amidar/), Founder of Idealist.

Together, they helped unpack a question that feels increasingly important for the social impact sector: **What actually makes something an ecosystem, not just another program, platform, event, or initiative?**

Because “ecosystem” is one of those words we use a lot. Sometimes meaningfully.  
Sometimes because it sounds better in a grant proposal. Sometimes because “a bunch of loosely connected things we are trying to make sense of” needed a friendlier name. But when we slow down, the word matters.

An ecosystem is not just a collection of people or organizations. It is not just a calendar full of events. It is not just everyone joining the same Slack group and hoping magic happens. An ecosystem is alive. It has movement. It has relationships. It has dependency.It has surprise, tension and trust.

And if it is working, it keeps creating value even when the original organizer is not standing in the middle of it, waving their arms around, trying to keep everything moving.

\* Which, as a founder, I say with both enthusiasm and a tiny bit of nervous laughter.

## Biggest Lessons from our Ecosystem Building Talk

If you only take a few things from this conversation, let them be these:

1. If everything depends on the founder forever, it may not be an ecosystem yet.
2. Programs produce outputs. Ecosystems create flow.
3. Interdependence is not weakness. It is infrastructure.
4. Proximity does not automatically create connection.
5. Real ecosystems require complexity, diversity, and surprise.

## What Is a Social Impact Ecosystem?

A social impact ecosystem is a living network of people, organizations, communities, resources, ideas, and relationships that move together toward shared wellbeing or change.

That sounds a little polished, so let’s say it another way: **It is what happens when trust, knowledge, care, and action do not just sit in one place.** They move.

Between organizations. Between people. Between communities. Between sectors.  
Between someone who has been doing the work for 30 years and someone who just joined their first nonprofit and is already wondering why the CRM is held together with duct tape and optimism.

Osayi offered one of the clearest definitions in the conversation. *She said a program is designed to produce specific outputs. But an ecosystem is about flow.* Not a flurry of activities, but knowledge, trust, and resources constantly moving between different circles and different actors.

That distinction matters. Because activity can look impressive. But activity is not the same as movement. And movement is not the same as impact.

An ecosystem asks a different set of questions:

*Is knowledge moving?**Is trust growing?**Are resources circulating?**Are people becoming more connected to one another, not just more connected to us?****Can this work keep breathing if one person steps back?***

That last question is a big one. Especially for founders. Especially for community builders. Especially for those of us who sometimes accidentally become the emotional support human for the whole thing.

## A Program Produces Outputs. An Ecosystem Creates Flow.

Programs are not bad. In fact we need them. Programs give shape to work. They create clarity. They help us define what is happening, who it is for, what the goal is, and how we will know if it worked.

But ecosystems ask us to think beyond delivery.

A program might ask:

*What are we offering?**Who is attending?**What are the outcomes?**What did we produce?*

An ecosystem asks:

*What keeps moving after we leave?**Who is connected to whom?**Where is trust forming?**Who needs one another to succeed?**What happens when we are no longer managing every interaction?*

That is where Osayi’s idea of interdependence becomes so important.

She named genuine interdependence as one of the signs of a real ecosystem: organizations and leaders who actually need one another in order to succeed. I loved that. Because in the nonprofit sector, we talk about collaboration all the time.

But interdependence? That word makes us a little twitchy. Interdependence means we are not pretending we can do this work alone. It means admitting that our missions are connected. Our challenges overlap. Our learning is stronger when it is shared. Our wellbeing is affected by the health of the people and organizations around us.

That can feel vulnerable in a sector where so many organizations are still pushed to prove independence, uniqueness, and constant self-sufficiency.

But what if interdependence is not a weakness? What if it is the actual infrastructure of social change?

Because when nonprofit professionals are isolated, the work gets heavier. When organizations hoard knowledge, everyone moves slower. When leaders are expected to carry complexity alone, burnout starts to look inevitable.

Ecosystem thinking invites us to stop pretending separation is strength.

## Proximity Is Not the Same as Connection

Pete brought in another necessary layer. At GenWell, his work focuses on social health and human connection. And one of the most powerful lines from the conversation was this:

***“We mistook proximity to being connection.”*** Oof. That one lands, doesn’t it?

Because nonprofit professionals are often around people all day. Meetings. Emails. Committee calls. Partner updates. Donor conversations. Community consultations. So many touchpoints. And yet, plenty of people in this sector still feel deeply unsupported.

That is because proximity and connection are not the same thing.

Being in the same room does not mean we feel known.  
Being on the same email thread does not mean we feel supported.  
Being in the same sector does not mean we have spaces where we can be honest.  
Being busy with people all day does not mean we are connected in ways that sustain us.

This is why ecosystem-building has to be intentional. We cannot assume connection will happen just because people are gathered. Connection requires trust, invitation, safety, structure, and enough humanity that people actually want to show up.

Pete also made the point that we have spent decades teaching people about physical health, but much less time helping people understand social health.

We tell people to eat well. Move their bodies. Drink water. Sleep. Reduce stress. All good things.

But we rarely teach people that healthy connection is also part of wellbeing. In the nonprofit sector, that gap matters. When people are already working inside urgency, scarcity, emotional labour, and constant complexity, weak connection is not a small issue.

It is a risk. Not because connection fixes everything. It does not. But disconnection makes almost everything harder.

## If Nothing Can Surprise You, It May Not Be an Ecosystem Yet

Ami brought in a different but equally important angle. For him, ecosystem means complexity. Not one neat thing repeated over and over, but diversity, variety, and surprise.

He used the example of a Christmas tree farm versus a true ecosystem. A Christmas tree farm may have a lot of trees, but if they are all the same, arranged in rows, grown for one purpose, that is not really the same as a living, diverse ecosystem.

That image is so helpful. Because sometimes, in community-building, we say we want an ecosystem. But what we actually want is a very well-behaved Christmas tree farm.

We want people to gather, but not disrupt.  
We want participation, but not too much unpredictability.  
We want shared ownership, as long as everyone shares it exactly the way we imagined.  
We want community, but without the mess of actual humans bringing their own ideas, needs, timing, humour, grief, brilliance, and occasionally very strong opinions about the font.

But as Ami pointed out, ecosystems surprise you. And if you are building something that can move beyond you, some of those surprises are not bugs in the system. They are proof that the system is alive.

That is where ecosystem-building feels so different from program design. In a program, we often try to reduce surprises. We want the agenda to run on time, the right people in the room, the intended outcomes, and a post-event survey that says everyone had a meaningful experience and the snacks were acceptable.

But ecosystems ask us to hold a different posture.

We still need boundaries.  
We still need values.  
We still need containers.  
We still need care.

But we also have to leave room for people to make meaning, build relationships, create unexpected connections, and carry ideas in directions we did not plan.That is beautiful. It is also inconvenient. **Welcome to community.**

## The Container Matters, But You Are Not the Whole Ecosystem

One thing this conversation kept bringing me back to is that community builders often hold two jobs at once. We build the container. And then, if we are not careful, we try to control everything that happens inside it.

A good container matters. It creates safety, sets expectations, protects trust, and helps people know how to enter, contribute, and care for one another.

At The Nonprofit Hive, we think about this constantly. Our structure is intentionally simple: one-to-one connection, clear expectations, no solicitation, and an easy way for nonprofit professionals to meet someone who gets it.

But the structure is not the magic. The magic is what people bring into it. Their stories. Their questions. Their lived experience. Their “oh my goodness, me too” moments. Their practical tips. Their quiet encouragement. Their willingness to say, “Here is what we tried, and here is what I wish someone had told me earlier.”

It is not just us connecting people. It is people becoming connected enough to support, challenge, encourage, and resource each other.

That is the difference between being the centre of everything and building something that can actually hold more than you. For me this is both the dream and the challenge.

Because many founders and community builders start with personal energy. They care deeply. They invite people in. They hold the early shape. They are often the reason the thing exists in the first place.

But if the ecosystem is going to grow, it eventually needs more than the founder’s enthusiasm. It needs shared ownership. It needs relationships that do not always run through one person. It needs people who feel not just welcomed, but needed.

## What This Means for Nonprofit Leaders

So what does this mean if you are trying to build community, partnership, collaboration, or movement in the nonprofit sector? **It means you may need to look beyond the program**.

Not abandon programs. Not throw away your logic model. Not dramatically delete every spreadsheet while whispering “flow” to yourself. Please keep the useful spreadsheets. But also ask better questions.

- Instead of only asking, “What are we delivering?” ask, “What are we helping move?”
- Instead of only asking, “How many people attended?” ask, “Who became more connected because of this?”
- Instead of only asking, “Did we hit our outputs?” ask, “Did trust deepen?”
- Instead of only asking, “How do we keep people engaged with us?” ask, “How do we help people build relationships with each other?”
- Instead of only asking, “How do we scale this?” ask, “What needs to be protected so this can grow without losing its soul?”

That last one might be the real work.

Because ecosystems are not just built by adding more people. They are built by strengthening the quality of what moves between people. And those things do not always show up neatly in a dashboard. But they shape whether people stay, whether they share, whether they collaborate, whether they ask for help, whether they feel less alone, and whether the work can keep going.

## Questions to Ask Before Calling Something an Ecosystem

If you are building, funding, hosting, or participating in something you are calling an ecosystem, these questions can help you look a little deeper.

| Instead of only asking… | Ask this ecosystem question |
| --- | --- |
| How many people showed up? | Is there real flow here, or just activity? |
| How connected are people to us? | Are people connected to one another, or only to the host organization? |
| Did we share enough information? | Is knowledge moving across the network, or staying with a few central people? |
| Are all partners doing their own part? | Are we comfortable with interdependence, or are we still pretending everyone should be self-sufficient? |
| Did we gather people in the same space? | Does this work create healthy connection, or just proximity? |
| Did we create enough structure? | Is there enough structure for people to participate with confidence? |
| Did things go according to plan? | Is there enough openness for people to surprise us? |
| Is the founder or lead organization holding it well? | If the founder or lead organization stepped back, what would continue? |
| Are people attending? | Are people helping carry the work forward? |

None of these questions are easy. But they are useful. Because the sector does not need more things that look connected from the outside but feel lonely on the inside. We need more spaces where people can actually find one another. More systems where care can circulate. More communities where knowledge moves without being hoarded. More movements where no single person has to hold the whole thing alone.

## Rethinking Ecosystem-Building in the Social Impact Sector

Ecosystems are not built through activity alone. They are built through relationships strong enough to move value, trust, and care. That was the deeper invitation in this conversation.

Osayi reminded us that ecosystems require flow, interdependence, and resilience.

Pete reminded us that connection is not a soft bonus. It is part of social health, and we cannot keep mistaking proximity for the real thing.

Ami reminded us that ecosystems need complexity, surprise, and the humility to let people carry ideas beyond our control.

And together, they offered something the nonprofit sector needs right now: A bigger imagination for how we support the people behind the work. Because a sector built on care cannot keep running on disconnection. It cannot keep asking people to collaborate while rewarding isolation. It cannot keep treating community as a nice-to-have while wondering why so many people feel unsupported.

If we want stronger movements, healthier organizations, and more sustainable change, we need to think beyond programs. We need ecosystems that breathe. Ecosystems that connect.

Ecosystems that keep moving, not because one person is forcing them to, but because people have found enough trust, value, and belonging to keep showing up.

That is the work. Messy? Absolutely. Worth it? Also absolutely.

### Frequently Asked Questions about Nonprofit Ecosystem Building

********What is the difference between a program and an ecosystem?********

A program is usually designed to deliver a specific outcome, service, or experience. An ecosystem is a living network where people, knowledge, trust, resources, and care move between different actors over time.

********Why does ecosystem-building matter in the nonprofit sector?********

Nonprofit work is complex, relational, and often under-resourced. Ecosystem-building helps people and organizations share knowledge, build trust, reduce isolation, and strengthen the relationships needed for long-term social change.

********Is interdependence a bad thing for nonprofits?********

No. Interdependence can be a strength when it is rooted in trust, clarity, and shared purpose. Social impact work often requires organizations, leaders, funders, communities, and peers to rely on one another in healthy and transparent ways.

********How do you know if you are building an ecosystem?********

You may be building an ecosystem if value continues to move between people even when you are not directing every interaction. Signs include shared ownership, trust, peer-to-peer exchange, collaboration, resilience, and the ability for the work to continue beyond one person or organization.

******Can an ecosystem still have structure?******

Yes. In fact, healthy ecosystems often need clear containers, expectations, and values. The difference is that the structure supports connection and movement instead of controlling every outcome.
