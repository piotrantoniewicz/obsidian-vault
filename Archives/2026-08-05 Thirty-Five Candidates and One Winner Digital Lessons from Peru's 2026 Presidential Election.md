Everyone talks about the Netherlands when the conversation turns to fragmented multi-party democracies. Twenty-six parties in parliament. Coalition negotiations that last longer than some governments. A proportional system that turns elections into elaborate political jigsaw puzzles.

\-

But if you want to understand what true democratic fragmentation looks like, and what it demands of political communicators, Peru is your laboratory. The April 2026 first round of the presidential election featured thirty-five candidates. Thirty-five. The ballot required voters to pick from a field so large that logistical problems on election day, including ballot papers not arriving on time to dozens of polling stations in Lima, forced authorities to extend voting by an extra day. Blank and null votes, the universal language of democratic despair, accounted for nearly 17% of all ballots cast. More people chose nobody than voted for the eventual winner in the first round.

That winner was Keiko Fujimori. With 17.18% of valid first-round votes, she led a field of thirty-five and then ground her way to a 50.11% victory in a June runoff decided by fewer than 50,000 votes out of more than 18 million cast. She was declared president-elect on 3 July 2026, confirmed as the first woman ever elected to Peru's presidency. She also, rather remarkably, did it on her fourth attempt.

Before going further, a word of caution. Peru is a uniquely volatile political environment. The country is on its ninth president in a decade. Three consecutive presidents faced criminal prosecution. Keiko Fujimori herself spent time in preventive detention over campaign finance allegations before her case was dismissed in January 2026, weeks before her candidacy launched. The political context, the legacy baggage, the security crisis, the institutional fragility, all of it shaped what worked and why. Use these lessons as a set of principles, not a blueprint to photocopy.

#### Thirty-Five Candidates Is Not a Problem. It Is a Relief.

Let us start with the obvious question. How do you cut through in a first round with thirty-five candidates, where the winner needs only a plurality and most voters have never heard of most people on the ballot?

The answer, counterintuitively, is that extreme fragmentation clarifies your communications job. When the field is this large, voters are not choosing between two coherent visions. They are navigating noise. The campaigns that win in that environment are the ones that are the most audible, the most legible, and the most emotionally specific.

Fujimori's campaign understood this at a structural level. The slogan "Perú con Orden" (Peru with Order) was the entire message in three words. In a country where extortion crimes had increased by 1,000% between 2023 and 2025, where over 500 schools faced gang extortion, where 33% of Peruvians reported personally knowing someone affected, "order" was not an abstract political value. It was the thing people needed most and associated most directly with the Fujimori name.

This is the first digital lesson of extreme multi-candidate elections: in a crowded field, clarity is a competitive advantage. Most campaigns try to say more when they are competing against more people. Fujimori's said less. Three words that mapped directly onto the single most urgent emotional reality in voters' lives, repeated across every platform, every format, every appearance.

When your party and your candidate are already well known in a country, name recognition is table stakes. What you need is a razor-sharp emotional hook that survives a thirty-second TikTok, a six-second audio pre-roll, and a poster glimpsed from a moving bus. "Perú con Orden" did all three.

#### On Running a Fourth Time: Persistence as a Brand Value

Keiko Fujimori ran for president in 2011, 2016, 2021, and 2026. She lost each of the first three in the second round. She was imprisoned on remand in 2018, spent over a year in preventive detention, was released, and came back. By any conventional analysis, a politician who has lost three consecutive runoffs, spent time in jail, and carries her father's historically divisive legacy should be finished. Political careers have not survived less.

What turned those four campaigns into an asset rather than a liability was, in part, a communications choice about how to frame them. The narrative that emerged by 2026 was not one of failure and retry. It was one of persistence in the face of a system that had repeatedly tried to remove her. The legal cases, the imprisonments, the narrow losses, were framed not as evidence of unsuitability but as evidence of how much her opponents feared her. Whether you find that framing credible or troubling is a separate question. It is unambiguously effective.

For digital communications, the lesson is about narrative architecture over time. If you are advising a candidate or party that has lost before, the framing question is not "how do we put this loss behind us?" It is "how does this loss become part of a story that matters to our supporters?" Repeated failure, in certain political contexts, can generate something more powerful than a victory that came easily: a community of supporters who feel personally invested in the eventual win.

The messaging in 2026 reflected this. Fujimori's campaign leaned into the idea that this was not just her fourth attempt but the one that had been earned through years of opposition, prosecution, and persistence. That is a narrative that travels naturally on social media, because it has the structure of a story. It has a protagonist, obstacles, antagonists, and a resolution. Stories get shared. Policy platforms do not.

#### The Overseas Diaspora as a Digitally Organised Constituency

Fujimori won the second round because of overseas voters. After Sánchez initially led among domestic votes, the foreign ballots, counted last, tipped the balance. She ultimately prevailed by around 50,000 votes and the diaspora vote was the decisive margin.

This did not happen by accident. Fujimori's political base has historically included a significant share of Peruvians living abroad, particularly in Japan, the United States, and parts of Latin America. These communities, generally more economically mobile, more likely to have benefited from the stability of the 1990s under Alberto Fujimori, and physically distant from the insecurity dominating Peru's domestic political conversation, represent a reliable constituency. But reliability alone does not deliver votes. Organisation does.

The campaign ran parallel digital operations targeting overseas communities through platforms, group chats, and content specifically framed for diaspora audiences. For Peruvians abroad, the stakes of the election are both visceral (family members at home affected by crime and instability) and abstract (they experience Peru primarily through screens and conversations). That is precisely the environment in which digital campaigning is most effective. Physical rallies cannot reach Osaka or Madrid or New York. WhatsApp groups, Instagram videos, and YouTube live streams can.

The practical lesson applies broadly. If your party has significant support among diaspora communities, and most European parties with immigrant or second-generation constituencies do, a dedicated digital operation targeting those communities is not a nice-to-have. It is a vote bank that is uniquely accessible through digital channels and uniquely underserved by traditional campaign infrastructure. They are already online. They are already talking. The question is whether your campaign is in that conversation.

#### Using Technology to Turn Security Into Something Visual

One of the more interesting digital-policy intersections in the 2026 campaign was Fujimori's "Perú con Orden" platform proposal for a public-facing real-time crime dashboard, integrating police reports, risk zones, crime heat maps, and community alerts into a single accessible interface powered by AI and predictive analytics.

This is worth noting not because it was implemented (she has only just won) but because of what it signals about how policy proposals function as digital content. A complex security policy agenda becomes, in communication terms, something you can show rather than just describe. A crime heat map on a social media post, a visualisation of extortion rates rising across Lima neighbourhoods, before-and-after data from comparable security interventions elsewhere: these are formats that land on digital platforms in a way that a policy white paper does not.

The campaign understood that in a digital information environment, policy credibility is built through demonstration rather than declaration. Saying "I will make Peru safer" is a promise everyone makes. Showing a tangible, technology-driven mechanism for how that would work, with a visual interface voters can understand intuitively, is a different order of communication. It is specific enough to be credible, visual enough to be shareable, and technical enough to project competence without being inaccessible.

This is a lesson that European political campaigns, many of which communicate policy almost entirely through manifesto text and press releases, would benefit from taking seriously. Translate your flagship policy into something visual. Make the mechanism, not just the promise, the content.

#### Navigating a Legacy: The Art of Selective Distance

The single most significant communications challenge for Keiko Fujimori in every campaign has been her father. Alberto Fujimori governed Peru from 1990 to 2000, was convicted of crimes against humanity including forced sterilisations of Indigenous women, and spent sixteen years in prison. He died in September 2024. The question of how his daughter relates to that legacy, publicly, consistently, and credibly, is the central tension of her entire political career.

What the 2026 campaign did more effectively than the previous three was to stop trying to fully resolve that tension and instead manage it. The campaign did not centre Alberto Fujimori. It did not ignore him. It acknowledged the pain of the sterilisation programme, maintained support for reparations, and moved on. The digital content focused almost entirely on Keiko's own platform, her own story, and the current crisis of security, not on relitigating the 1990s.

This is a genuinely difficult communications lesson, but a transferable one. Every established political family or party in Europe carries legacies that portions of the electorate find troubling. The instinct is either to defend everything or to apologise for everything. Neither works. What Fujimori's campaign modelled in 2026 was a third option: acknowledge, position clearly, and redirect attention to the forward-looking case. Do not make your inheritance the centre of your campaign. Make it a settled background question with a clear, consistent answer, and then campaign on what you are going to do next.

#### The Six-Day Debate Marathon and What It Tells You About Content Strategy

Peru's electoral authority, faced with thirty-five presidential candidates and an obligation to give each a platform, scheduled debates over six days, grouping candidates in threes and rotating through them. Twelve candidates per day. Six days. Two themes per day.

This is, from a campaign communications perspective, a fascinating stress test. In a traditional two or three-candidate debate, your preparation is deep and specific. In a twelve-per-day marathon, you are one of many, and viewer attention is exhausted by the third candidate of the morning. Getting noticed in that format requires a completely different approach.

The campaigns that performed well in Peru's debate marathon were those that treated each debate slot not as an opportunity to present a comprehensive platform but as a chance to land one memorable moment, one line, one specific commitment that would cut through online after the broadcast ended. The debate is not the product. The social media clip of the best moment from the debate is the product.

Most campaigns still prepare for debates as if the debate itself is the audience. It is not. The audience is the thirty seconds that gets clipped, captioned, and circulated. In a field of thirty-five, that discipline is not optional. It is the only way to exist in the conversation.

#### A Note on What 17% Can Win

There is one more lesson from Peru that European campaigners working in proportional or fragmented systems should sit with. Keiko Fujimori won a presidential election by coming first in the first round with 17.18% of valid votes. Seventeen per cent. In a well-organised political party in a European proportional system, that is a governing-coalition result. In Peru's two-round system, it was enough to put her on a path to the presidency.

The structural mechanics are different in Europe. But the underlying insight holds: in fragmented electoral environments, the prize does not go to the most popular. It goes to the most organised, the most consistent, and the most focused. Fujimori did not try to appeal to all thirty-five constituencies. She was the clearest, loudest, most consistent voice in one lane, and she let the noise of thirty-four other candidates do the rest.

That is a digital strategy as much as a political one. Know your lane. Stay in it. Be findable by the people already looking for what you offer, before you try to expand.
