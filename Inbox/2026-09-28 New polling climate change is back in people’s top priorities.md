[**Persuasion UK has released polling**](https://persuasionuk.org/research/climate-tracker-polling-summer-2026) **that interrogates climate salience in the aftermath of the UK’s hottest summer on record.** Climate is now firmly in people’s top five issues; at the start of 2026 it was closer to number ten. Notably, the percentage of people ranking it a key concern has nearly doubled since the country was hit by relentless heatwaves.

This leap marks a notable shift in public opinion, reminiscent of how people in the UK felt about climate back in 2022. So what does this renewed wave of concern mean for communicating about climate?

## Extreme heat drives salience

**Unsurprisingly, climate concern has spiked particularly among progressive Labour and Green Party supporters, coming in second for Labour 2024 voter priorities.** For those already anxious about the impacts of climate change, the summer brought those fears to front of mind. However, here it’s worth remembering one of our key [Britain Talks Climate & Nature recommendations](https://climateoutreach.org/btc/2025/report/chapter-2/#2025-recommendations) – to not to take such support for granted, especially while there exists an increasingly vocal opposition. Persuasion’s UK report reflects a fragmented picture, showing a growing polarisation in the salience of climate change between different groups.

**Even so, climate backlash shouldn’t be overstated.** The Persuasion UK report tells us the majority of people connect the extreme heat to climate change, with Green voters, climate-curious Labour supporters, and Gen Z leading this perception.

The ‘it’s just the weather’ underplay fails to resonate with most people, despite the efforts of prolific naysayers like Reform UK MP Richard Tice advising people ‘just enjoy it’. That advice landed during a wave of 38°C temperatures in London – causing school closures, cancelled hospital surgeries, and severe heat mortality.

**For many of us, climate change impacts are being felt increasingly closer to home and directly affecting daily lives.** The percentage of people saying it will do a great deal of harm to their region has nearly doubled since the start of the summer, reflecting widespread impacts – from wildfires destroying homes in Stourbridge, to the heat decimating crops nationwide. [New Climate Barometer data](https://climatebarometer.org/climate-barometer-trackers/?topic%5B0%5D=332#return-to-filter) confirms extreme heat has now overtaken drought as the climate impact most personally experienced by the UK public.

## Nature and food on the frontline

**According to Persuasion UK, harm to nature and wildlife remains the public’s single largest climate concern, aligning with our** [**Britain Talks Climate & Nature findings**](https://climateoutreach.org/btc/2025/report/chapter-3/#how-people-feel)**.** Visible habitat and wildlife destruction—such as fires across Dunwich Heath and Minsmere—has likely reinforced this fear. Addressing this prevailing concern by talking about protecting nature remains one of the most effective, unifying entry points that crosses political divides.

**People’s second highest climate impact concern is that it ‘makes some food unavailable’, which has seen the biggest jump since July last year.** Research by The Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit (ECIU) predicts that [this summer’s extreme heat and drought are projected to have led to the worst harvest on record](https://eciu.net/media/press-releases/uk-harvest-even-worse-than-previously-thought-new-data), with farmers struggling with crop failure and livestock management.

With food security already rated a top policy priority by the British public [according to our research](https://climateoutreach.org/btc/2025/report/farming/#think-and-feel), warnings regarding extreme weather impacts have significantly heightened public awareness of food supply vulnerabilities.

## The rising cost of inaction

**How does this increased climate salience translate into people’s support for climate action?** Whilst the Persuasion UK report shows that change in policy support was fairly modest across the board, there is a significant uplift in support for ‘cost of inaction is too high’ arguments over ‘cost of action is too high’ reasoning. In the face of extreme heat, the public is increasingly recognising that failing to act carries a far steeper price tag than investing in solutions.

So whilst this uptick in climate salience isn’t matched by the same levels of support for net zero policy, it still offers a springboard for positive action – and a tool for pushback.

Steve Akehurst, Director of Persuasion UK, says:

> “We can understand the summer as having taken the passively supportive of climate action and – for now anyway – made them more actively supportive. This is not everything and shouldn’t be over-read. But it is not nothing either. It at the very least makes the politics of climate more competitive again, creating friction and pushback for politicians who assume the only way is toward delay or denialism.”
