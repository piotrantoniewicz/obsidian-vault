### Tip #1: Write At Least 10 Variations Of Your Subject Line

It doesn't matter how good your email is if nobody opens it in the first place.

That's why your subject line is so important. Now, it's impossible to come up with a banger subject line on the first try.

Which is why I always brainstorm AT LEAST 10 subject line variations for every email I write.

Luckily, you can use AI to do this 10x faster.

One more pro tip: always keep your subject lines under 42 characters.

That's the cutoff on most mobile screens. Anything longer gets chopped off, so anyone reading on mobile will only sees half of your hook.

### Tip #2: Write Your Intro Like A LinkedIn Hook

Just because someone is reading your stuff in their inbox doesn't mean you're not competing for attention.

So skip the long greetings and aim to capture attention immediately instead.

For example, here's the intro of the piece you're reading right now:

*"I make a living writing newsletters for 7, 8, and 9 figure founders. And after writing and editing 1,000+ newsletters, I've collected a handful of tips that instantly make your email copy more engaging.”*

Now, here's what a weaker version could have looked like:

*"Hey there! Hope you're having a great week. I've been thinking a lot lately about email marketing and what separates the emails that get opened and read from the ones that get ignored. After years of working with some of the biggest creators and founders in the game, I've compiled a list of my top tips that I think you'll find really valuable…"*

See the difference?

Good.

Now, let’s keep going.

### Tip #3: Speak Directly To The Reader

A lot of people write their emails as if they were speaking to a big audience.

But the best emails feel like a 1:1 conversation.

Here are 3 easy ways to make sure you don't make this mistake.

- **First, look for any instances where you're clearly speaking to a crowd.** "Hey everyone" → "Hey" or "Hey \[name\]."
- **Second, watch for third person references to your own audience.** This one is sneakier. Here’s an example: "Most creators struggle to stay consistent with their newsletter." A better version would be: "You've probably struggled to stay consistent with your newsletter." Same point, except now it's aimed at one person instead of described from a distance.
- **Third, run a quick ratio check.** Aim for 80% or more of your email to be written in second person. If you're using "I" or "they" more than "you," you're not speaking directly to the reader enough.

### Tip #4: Edit Your Emails On Your Phone

An email might look clean on desktop but feel completely overwhelming on mobile.

And whether you like it or not, mobile is where most people will read your stuff. That's why I always edit my emails from my phone before I send them.

The main thing to look for:

Blocky paragraphs.

![Treść artykułu](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D4E12AQF-NDRaZZ4dZw/article-inline_image-shrink_1000_1488/B4EaEWnZIMJkAI-/0/1791380793674?e=1793232000&v=beta&t=6rr9tY2apSv47J_ypxCejRhZJoeS4K5mzVoxw4VF4Tg)

If you've got a long paragraph with 2-4 sentences, add some line breaks and split it into 1-sentence paragraphs.

That simple tweak fixes 99% of mobile formatting issues.

### Tip #5: Copywork Your Favorite Emails

Copywork is the most effective way to internalize what good emails look and feel like.

The idea is simple: transcribe the best emails and sales letters you come across by hand. Yes, with pen and paper.

Here's why it works.

When you read great copy, you absorb the ideas. When you transcribe it by hand, you absorb the rhythm. The sentence lengths, the transitions, the moment the writer decides to drop to a new line.

That stuff is almost impossible to pick up from reading alone.

How I'd do it: 15 to 20 minutes, one piece at a time, pen and paper. And while you're transcribing, pay attention to things like word choice, pacing, curiosity gaps, etc.

A few classics worth copyworking:

- **"The Tale of Two Young Men" (Wall Street Journal).** One of the most lucrative sales letters of all time.
- **"The Boron Letters" by Gary Halbert.** The bible of conversational sales copy.
- **"Do You Make These Mistakes in English?"** A masterclass in pain agitation.

### Tip #6: Read Your Emails Aloud

This one is straightforward. The problem is that most people simply don't do it.

If you actually read your emails out loud, you'll immediately notice the parts that sound clunky, the sentences that don't flow, and the sections that feel boring.

Specifically, listen for 3 things:

- **Sentences you stumble on.** If you can't say it smoothly, your reader can't read it smoothly.
- **Places where you run out of breath.** That's a long sentence that needs to be split in two.
- **Sections where your own voice goes flat.** If you're bored reading it, they're definitely bored reading it.

Here’s a little pro tip, too: You can actually have your phone read it back to you instead.

Hearing your words in a voice that isn't yours makes it impossible to ignore all the things your own reading naturally smooths over.

### Tip #7: Use Sub-Heads To Make The Piece Skimmable

Most readers don't read. They skim.

Sub-heads give people signposts to quickly scan and understand what your email covers without having to read every word.

2 rules of thumb I live by:

- **If you're writing a listicle (like this piece), make each item its own sub-head.**
- **If you're not, aim for at least 3-5 sub-heads.** Find the key sentences that drive a point home or mark the start of a new idea, and turn those into sub-heads.

### Tip #8: Always Include A PS

It's counterintuitive, but the PS is one of the most-read sections of any email.

Which makes it prime real estate.

3 different ways to leverage it:

- **Value newsletter with no CTA in the body?** Use the PS for a soft, contextual call-to-action. The email stays as “pure value,” but you still get to make an ask.
- **Sales email?** Use the PS to address a common objection. Tackle the reason someone might hesitate to buy.
- **Also for sales emails:** use the PS as a reminder. When does the offer expire? What's the bonus for acting now? The PS is a great place to reinforce urgency, address objections, and show social proof.

### Tip #9: Always Make Your CTAs Contextual

If your CTA feels out of place, it will perform way worse than if it felt ***congruent***.

There are 2 levers you can pull to fix that.

- **Placing.** What's the most relevant section of the email for the CTA you're trying to plug?
- **Phrasing.** How can you frame the CTA so it feels natural and congruent with the section around it?

Here’s an example:

- A bolted-on CTA for this piece might read: "Anyway, if you need help with your emails, I offer a service. Book a call here."
- A contextual CTA, on the other hand, would sound more like this: "If reading your drafts out loud sounds like one more thing you don't have time for, that's exactly the kind of thing we take off people's plates. Click here to book a chat and see if we’re a fit to work together."

It’s the same ask. But one feels like an interruption while the other feels completely natural.

### Tip #10: Turn Blocky Paragraphs Into Bulleted Lists

This might sound harsh but it’s true:

If reading your emails feels like work, most people won’t read them.

Luckily, bullets make information feel 10x easier to consume than a wall of text.

A few rules I follow:

- **Only use 1-2 bulleted lists per section.** Otherwise the whole thing starts to feel like a bullet point dump.
- **Always include 1-2 sentences before a list.** Don't dive straight into bullets with no context. It feels weird.
- **Keep bullets short where you can.** But if they run long, bold the key part of each one so readers can still skim. (Like I did here.)
