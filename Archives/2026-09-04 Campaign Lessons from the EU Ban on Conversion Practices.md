In the spring of 2024, a European Citizens' Initiative called "Ban on Conversion Practices in the European Union" collected one million validated signatures in six days. The final count, when the campaign closed on 17 May 2024, was 1.24 million signatures across 11 member states, well above the seven required. For an ECI mechanism whose typical campaign runs the full twelve months allowed and frequently fails to reach the threshold, this was something close to a record.

\-

The Commission validated the signatures. The LIBE, PETI and FEMM committees held a joint public hearing on 2 March 2026. The Parliament held a plenary debate on 25 March 2026. On 13 May 2026, the Commission published its formal reply, the 13th successful ECI to receive one, aligning its response with the EU LGBTIQ+ Equality Strategy 2026 to 2030. The campaign asked for three things: a directive adding conversion practices to the EU's list of euro-crimes (or, in the alternative, amendment of the Equality Directive); an amendment to the Victims' Rights Directive establishing minimum standards for victims of conversion practices; and encouragement for member states without national bans to introduce them, given that only 8 of 27 currently have them.

Whether the legislative follow-through arrives is the next year's question. The euro-crime designation needs Council unanimity. Hungary and one or two others will object. The directive route depends on which Commissioner picks up the file and how robustly. The campaign's job, for now, is to keep the political weather warm enough that the proposals survive Council negotiation.

What the campaign did to get to this point, though, is highly instructive. One million signatures in six days is not luck. It is the visible output of years of unglamorous infrastructure work. Below are six lessons, three for campaigners in political parties, three for PR officers in NGOs and corporate communications functions.

#### Three Lessons for Campaigners in Political Parties

##### Lesson 1: Six Days to a Million Signatures Means Years of Preparation

The campaign behind: the campaign was led by Mattéo Garguilo and Caleb Stocco, with logistical and political backing from a deep, pre-existing infrastructure: ILGA-Europe, IGLYO, national LGBTIQ+ organisations across all 27 member states, networks of friendly MEPs in Renew, S&D, the Greens and the Left, and supporter databases built over a decade of advocacy on adjacent issues (marriage equality, anti-discrimination directives, hate crime legislation, asylum protection). When the launch happened, the infrastructure switched on. The viral numbers were the visible output of dormant capacity, not the spark.

The political lesson: the campaigns that appear sudden were assembled in public silence. Mobilisation speed is a function of network depth, supporter list quality, and the trust between coalition partners. None of these are built during the campaign. They are built in the years before it.

In practice, the unsexy investments are the ones that pay off when a campaign moment arrives. Keep your MEP relationships warm in non-election cycles. Maintain supporter lists with proper consent and segmentation, not just spreadsheets that haven't been audited since 2022. Co-fund the small partner organisations in jurisdictions where the next mobilisation will need them. The campaign you launch in 2027 is the network you are maintaining in 2026.

##### Lesson 2: Use International Consensus as Institutional Cover

The campaign behind: the ECI text leaned on a stack of pre-existing international authorities. The UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights has qualified conversion practices as a form of torture. The World Medical Association has condemned them as unethical. The World Health Organization removed homosexuality from its disease classification in 1990. The European Court of Human Rights has relevant case law. The Fundamental Rights Agency's 2023 LGBTIQ+ survey found that 24% of respondents had experienced some form of conversion practice. When you can put the UN, the WHO, the WMA, the ECtHR and the FRA into a single paragraph, your domestic opponents have to argue against the entire international human rights and medical science apparatus. Most of them prefer not to.

The political lesson: international consensus is institutional cover for domestic politicians who want to vote yes but need a reason their voters will accept. "The UN classifies this as torture" is a defensible line for an EPP MEP in a way that "my caucus colleagues asked me to vote yes" is not.

In practice, before you launch, assemble the stack. Identify every international body whose position supports yours and pull the most quotable line from each. Build the citation hierarchy into your campaign materials at every level, from the petition text through to the MEP briefing pack. Make it as easy as possible for a sympathetic politician to defend their vote in front of a hostile constituent.

##### Lesson 3: Ask for the Legal Instrument That Already Exists

The campaign behind: the ECI did not ask for a new treaty competence. It asked for use of existing ones. Adding to the euro-crimes list is a procedure that already exists under Article 83(1) TFEU. Amending the Equality Directive is a process already underway. The Victims' Rights Directive is already on the EU statute book and can be amended. National bans are encouraged through the Commission's existing convening and soft-law mechanisms. Every ask fits inside a legal architecture that does not need a treaty change to operate.

The political lesson: institutional politics rewards the small, technical ask that fits inside existing competence. Treaty change is theoretical. Directive amendment is next year. The path of least institutional resistance is the path that delivers fastest.

In practice, before launching any EU-level campaign, map the existing legal instruments where your demand could plausibly fit. The European Convention on Human Rights, the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, existing directives, existing regulations, existing funding programmes. Frame the ask as "apply the tools we already have", not "build new tools". The institutional reflex to defend existing instruments is one of the strongest political forces in Brussels. Make it work for you.

#### Three Lessons for PR Officers in Corporate or NGO Roles

##### Lesson 4: Definition Is a Strategic Asset

The campaign behind: conversion practices were defined precisely in the ECI text as "interventions aimed at changing, repressing, or suppressing a person's sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression". That definition is narrow enough to be defensible in a courtroom and broad enough to capture the realities (the religious counselling sessions, the psychiatric "therapies", the residential programmes, the medical mistreatment) that practitioners would otherwise hide behind euphemism. The campaign used the same definition across every press release, every briefing, every social asset, every formal submission.

The PR lesson: vague definitions lose. Precise definitions survive contact with hostile lawyers, hostile journalists and hostile legislators. The campaign that owns the definition of its own subject matter is the campaign that controls the legislative drafting at the end.

In practice, draft your definition with a lawyer before launch. Stress-test it against both technical defensibility (can it survive court?) and PR memorability (can a journalist quote it back?). Use that definition across every piece of campaign communication. Treat any internal pressure to soften or expand it as a strategic risk, not a small editorial matter.

##### Lesson 5: Personal Testimony from Inside the Institution Multiplies Force

The campaign behind: during the European Parliament plenary debate on 25 March 2026, Renew MEP Lukas Sieper made a personal speech that travelled across European social media. "Put me into a camp and tell me that being with a woman is a sin, and I will still be heterosexual at the end of the day. Put me into a camp and parade the most attractive man in the world in front of me, I will still be heterosexual at the end of the day. Put me into a camp and beat me up until I say differently, I will still be heterosexual at the end of the day." A heterosexual MEP, from the political centre, making the universal point in the first person. It cut through. It was repeated. It was, in PR terms, the moment.

The PR lesson: when you can produce an institutional voice with personal stake, you have an asset that institutional opponents cannot easily counter. The voice does not have to be a member of the community concerned. It does have to be authentic, prepared, and willing to speak from somewhere personal rather than from a briefing card.

In practice, identify the elected officials, executives, board members, mayors and councillors who can speak with both authority and personal investment on your issue. Build the relationships over time. Brief them when the moment approaches. The right person, ready at the right plenary, can do the work of fifty press releases in two minutes of floor time. The infrastructure is the relationship, not the speech.

##### Lesson 6: Numbers Plus First Person, Every Press Cycle

The campaign behind: the campaign paired the FRA statistic (24% of LGBTIQ+ EU residents have experienced some form of conversion practice, with trans and non-binary people disproportionately affected) with named survivors and named practitioners. The number gave scale. The names gave stakes. Press coverage that used only one (statistic without story, or anecdote without scale) was demonstrably weaker than coverage that used both.

The PR lesson: statistics alone are forgettable. Stories alone are dismissible as anecdote. The combination is the rhetorical engine. The journalist who has both will write the piece that lands. The campaign that supplies both is the campaign that journalists return to.

In practice, every press release, every social asset, every spokesperson briefing should pair at least one strong number with at least one named human voice. Train this as a discipline at the comms desk. Audit your last ten press hits. If half of them lack one of the two elements, fix the workflow before the next launch.

#### What Can You do When the Infrastructure Is There?

The Commission's 13 May 2026 response was a positive opening, not a settled outcome. The euro-crime designation requires Council unanimity. The Equality Directive amendment depends on which Commissioner takes ownership and how robustly. The 8-of-27 baseline on national bans is still the working reality, which means most EU citizens still live in jurisdictions where conversion practices are not banned by national law. The campaign won the argument that there is an EU role. The directive that delivers protection is still ahead.

What the conversion practices ECI demonstrated is that the EU's apparently slow and procedural machinery can be made to move quickly when civil society infrastructure is mature, the international consensus is overwhelming, and the political asks fit existing competences. One million signatures in six days is not a creative campaign success. It is the visible output of a decade of patient network maintenance.

For political party campaigners, the takeaway is that the wins come fast only when the infrastructure was built slowly. For PR officers, the takeaway is that defining the subject precisely and humanising the abstraction with names and numbers are the disciplines that turn a campaign moment into a legislative outcome.

The directive is the next file and the campaigners are already drafting.
